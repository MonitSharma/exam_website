**Paper:** GS IV (Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude) | **Setting this week:** Environmental regulation / industrial licensing

## Case Study

You are the Member Secretary of a State Pollution Control Board in a mid-sized industrial state. A large integrated textile-processing cluster in a backward district has applied for renewal of its "Consent to Operate". The cluster employs about 9,000 workers directly and supports several thousand more through ancillary units. It is also the main economic anchor of a district with poor human development indicators.

During a surprise inspection, your field team finds that the Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP) has been running well below design capacity for two years. Partially treated effluent is being discharged into a seasonal river. Downstream, a group of villages relies on the river and shallow groundwater. A local NGO has already submitted a petition citing skin diseases and crop damage. Lab results from your own samples confirm that colour, COD and heavy-metal levels exceed prescribed norms.

The cluster's association approaches you. They admit lapses but say that a closure notice would render thousands jobless just before the festival season. They promise to upgrade the CETP within 18 months. A senior minister from the region calls you personally and "advises" that a lenient approach is in the public interest. Separately, an old batchmate who now works as a consultant for the association hints that "a cooperative officer is never forgotten" and that a post-retirement advisory role could be arranged.

Your own Board's senior scientist privately tells you that a phased, conditional approach is technically feasible, but that the data from one sampling point is disputed by the industry and may not survive legal challenge.

The renewal decision is due in seven days.

---

## Questions  (Total: 25 marks)

1. Identify the ethical issues and the stakeholders involved in this case. **(8 marks, ~100 words)**
2. What options are available to you? Evaluate the merits and demerits of each. **(8 marks, ~100 words)**
3. Which course of action would you adopt, and why? Justify it in terms of ethical reasoning. **(5 marks, ~50 words)**
4. Which values and principles of public administration are tested in this case? How would you handle the pressure and the inducement you have received? **(4 marks, ~50 words)**

**Suggested overall length:** about 250–300 words in total, so allocate words in proportion to marks. Time limit: roughly 18–20 minutes.

# Model Approach (read only after attempting)

## 1. Stakeholders

- **Downstream villagers and farmers:** health, livelihood and right to a clean environment (Art. 21).
- **Workers and ancillary families:** jobs and income security.
- **Industry owners and the cluster association:** economic interest, reputation, legal exposure.
- **You as Member Secretary and the Board:** statutory duty, credibility, institutional integrity.
- **Political executive:** development agenda and electoral considerations.
- **NGO and civil society:** public-interest advocacy.
- **Future generations and the river ecosystem:** intergenerational equity.
- **Judiciary and the National Green Tribunal:** oversight and scrutiny.

## 2. Core ethical dilemmas

- **Public health and environment versus livelihood:** a conflict between competing rights, with irreversible harm on one side and reversible economic harm on the other.
- **Rule of law versus compassion:** strict enforcement against humane concern for workers.
- **Integrity versus political and personal pressure:** the minister's "advice" and the consultant's inducement.
- **Institutional independence versus executive deference.**
- **Certainty versus precaution:** one disputed sample against multiple corroborating signals.

## 3. Evaluation of options

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Renew unconditionally (yield to pressure)** | Avoids short-term job loss and political friction | Violates law; continued harm to villagers; sets a precedent of impunity; invites NGT or court action; amounts to a conflict of interest and possible corruption |
| **B. Refuse renewal and order immediate closure** | Upholds the law and the polluter-pays principle; protects health | Sudden loss of thousands of jobs; social unrest; possible legal challenge on the disputed sample; punishes workers for owners' lapses |
| **C. Conditional, time-bound renewal with a strict compliance roadmap** | Balances health and livelihood; technically feasible; legally defensible; keeps pressure on industry | Risk of weak enforcement if not monitored; may be seen as leniency |
| **D. Escalate or delay to a higher authority or the court** | Shifts accountability | Abdicates statutory duty; delay prolongs harm |

## 4. Recommended course of action

Choose a **firm, conditional, and transparent version of Option C**. This is not a soft option.

1. **Decide independently and on record.** Base the decision on technical evidence, with written reasons. Do not act on the phone call.
2. **Strengthen the evidence.** Re-sample at the disputed point in the presence of industry representatives, with an NABL-accredited lab and third-party validation. Rely on the undisputed samples for the interim decision.
3. **Issue a show-cause notice and impose environmental compensation** under the polluter-pays principle.
4. **Grant a short-duration conditional consent (for example, 3–6 months)**, tied to measurable milestones: an immediate cut in discharge, restored CETP operation, online continuous effluent monitoring (OCEMS) linked to the Board, and a bank guarantee. Non-compliance triggers automatic closure of the defaulting units.
5. **Protect the affected people.** Arrange safe drinking water through the district administration, health camps, and compensation or remediation for affected farmers.
6. **Protect workers.** Insist that wages continue during any shutdown of defaulting units and coordinate with the labour department on safeguards.
7. **Disclose publicly.** Put the inspection findings, conditions and monitoring data in the public domain, and engage the NGO and gram sabhas.
8. **Handle the pressure and inducement properly.** Politely but firmly decline the minister's advice, putting on record that the decision is statutory and evidence-based. Reject the inducement, disclose the approach to the competent authority or vigilance, and recuse from any matter involving a conflict of interest.

**Justification:** The harm to villagers is health-related and partly irreversible, so the precautionary principle applies. The harm to workers is real but can be reduced through conditions and safeguards. The roadmap protects both, and it keeps the decision within the law. A transparent record also protects you from allegations of favour or victimisation.

## 5. Values and principles tested

Integrity, objectivity, impartiality, courage of conviction, accountability, transparency, compassion, rule of law, sustainable development, polluter-pays and precautionary principles, and intergenerational equity.

## 6. Thinkers and sources to cite

- **Gandhi:** the "talisman" test, asking whether a decision helps the poorest and weakest person. Here it supports the villagers' and workers' claims alike, and argues against a decision that sacrifices either.
- **Kant:** duty ethics; the law and the public trust must be followed regardless of inducements. Treat people as ends, not as means to economic output.
- **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles of Public Life:** selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership. These map directly to the pressure and inducement here.
- **Supreme Court principles:** M.C. Mehta cases (polluter-pays, absolute liability), the Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum case (precautionary principle, sustainable development) and the right to a healthy environment under Art. 21.

## 7. Answer-writing tips

- Open with a brief statement of the central conflict, then structure the answer under the questions asked.
- Avoid one-sided answers. UPSC rewards **balanced, practical, implementable** solutions and penalises both extremes: "close everything" and "yield quietly".
- Show **who does what, and by when**. Concrete administrative steps score better than general statements.
- Close with a value-based line that ties the decision back to constitutional morality and public trust.
