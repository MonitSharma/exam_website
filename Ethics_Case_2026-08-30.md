## Case Study

You are the Head of Compliance at a fast-growing digital lending company that offers small, instant loans through a mobile app to low-income borrowers, gig workers and small shopkeepers. The company is three months away from a large funding round that the founders describe as "make or break" for the survival of the firm and the jobs of its 900 employees.

Two findings land on your desk in the same week.

First, an internal review shows that the third-party recovery agencies engaged by the company have been using the app's permissions to access borrowers' phone contacts, and are calling relatives and employers to shame defaulters into paying. There have been 41 written complaints in six months. One borrower, a young delivery worker, attempted suicide after such calls; the matter was quietly settled with his family and never reported.

Second, the "risk score" that decides loan eligibility appears to systematically reject applicants from certain postal codes — areas that happen to be predominantly poor and minority-dominated. The data team says this is "just what the model learnt" from past repayment data, and that fixing it would raise default rates by an estimated 1.8 per cent.

The Chief Executive, who personally recruited you and has always backed your recommendations, asks you to record both matters as "process observations" for internal review after the funding round. He points out that no regulator has raised a query, that disclosure now would kill the round, and that you can fix everything with more money in the bank. Your compliance report to the Board is due in ten days.

---

## Questions *(Total: 25 marks | Suggested length: ~250–300 words overall, proportionate to sub-question marks)*

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved in this case and the stakeholders affected by your decision. *(5 marks)*

**(b)** Evaluate the following options available to you, bringing out their merits and demerits:
 (i) Accept the CEO's suggestion and defer both matters to the post-funding review.
 (ii) Record both findings fully in your Board report and simultaneously report the recovery practices to the regulator.
 (iii) Report the recovery-agent abuse immediately but treat the algorithmic bias as a technical matter for later remediation.
 (iv) Resign in protest and disclose the matter publicly. *(10 marks)*

**(c)** What course of action would you adopt, and why? Justify your choice with reference to the responsibilities of a compliance function. *(6 marks)*

**(d)** "Corporate ethics is not merely about legal compliance." In the light of this case, discuss the values and principles that should guide a professional placed between institutional loyalty and public harm. *(4 marks)*

## Model Approach

> Read this **only after** writing your own answer.

### 1. Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Interest at stake |
|---|---|
| Borrowers (esp. the harassed defaulters and the injured worker's family) | Dignity, privacy, life and mental health, fair access to credit |
| Rejected applicants in flagged postal codes | Freedom from discrimination; equal opportunity in credit |
| You, as Head of Compliance | Professional integrity, statutory duty, personal career and livelihood |
| CEO and founders | Firm's survival, funding, reputation |
| 900 employees and their families | Job security |
| Investors in the funding round | Right to true and material disclosure |
| Regulator (RBI) and the wider financial system | Consumer protection, systemic trust in digital lending |
| Society at large | Fair, non-discriminatory access to formal credit |

### 2. Core ethical dilemmas

1. **Personal loyalty vs. professional duty** — the CEO recruited and backed you; the compliance role exists precisely to be independent of him.
2. **Utilitarian appeal vs. inviolable rights** — 900 jobs are real, but they are being weighed against harassment, privacy violation and a near-fatal harm already caused. Some harms cannot be traded off against aggregate benefit.
3. **Legality vs. ethicality** — "no regulator has asked" is an argument about detection, not about right conduct. Concealment of the suicide attempt is itself likely a suppression of material information.
4. **Algorithmic neutrality vs. substantive justice** — "the model only learnt from data" launders historical disadvantage into present exclusion. Responsibility cannot be outsourced to a model.
5. **Delegated harm** — the abuse is by third-party agents, but the company supplied the data access and the incentive structure. Outsourcing an activity does not outsource accountability.

### 3. Evaluation of options

**(i) Defer both matters.**
*Merits:* protects the funding round and jobs in the short run; avoids confrontation.
*Demerits:* ongoing harassment continues for another quarter; makes you complicit in concealment; misleads incoming investors on a material risk; if it surfaces later, the firm faces far worse regulatory and reputational consequences. Ethically indefensible — the "later" is bought with someone else's suffering.

**(ii) Full Board disclosure + immediate regulatory reporting.**
*Merits:* honest, lawful, stops the harm at once; discharges your fiduciary duty; the Board — not one executive — decides how to handle a material risk; protects investors from a mis-sold valuation.
*Demerits:* may jeopardise funding and jobs; may be seen as disproportionate on the bias issue before remediation is attempted; personal career risk.

**(iii) Report the harassment; defer the bias issue.**
*Merits:* stops the acute, life-threatening harm immediately; recognises that algorithmic remediation genuinely takes time and technical work.
*Demerits:* risks becoming a rationalisation for indefinite delay; discrimination is a continuing harm, not a dormant one. Acceptable **only** if the bias is fully disclosed to the Board with a time-bound, monitored remediation plan — not if it is quietly parked.

**(iv) Resign and go public.**
*Merits:* preserves personal integrity; may force change.
*Demerits:* abandons the very position from which you could stop the harm; removes the internal check; harms employees and borrowers without any guarantee of remedy. Whistle-blowing is a last resort, justified only after internal channels have genuinely failed.

### 4. Recommended course of action

A **graded but uncompromising** response, essentially (ii) with the sequencing sense of (iii):

1. **Immediately suspend** the offending recovery agencies and revoke their access to borrower contact data; direct that no contact be made with third parties.
2. **Disable the contact-list permission** in the app pending a privacy review — the underlying capability, not just its misuse, is the problem.
3. **Record both findings in full** in the Board report. Do not soften language. Add the concealed settlement of the suicide-attempt case as a separate governance failure.
4. **Report the recovery-agent conduct to the RBI** under the Digital Lending Guidelines and the Fair Practices Code, and reach out to the affected borrower's family with support — proactive disclosure is materially better than discovered concealment.
5. On the model, commission a **time-bound fairness audit** with disclosed metrics and an interim manual-review override for rejected applicants from flagged areas; place quarterly progress before the Board. Accept the 1.8 per cent cost as the price of a lawful product.
6. **Disclose the pending remediation to the investors** through the Board. Investors funding a firm on a concealed liability are being defrauded; a firm that discloses and fixes is more, not less, investable.
7. Keep a **written record** of your advice and of any instruction to the contrary. Resign only if the Board itself directs suppression — and then report to the regulator.

**Justification:** the compliance officer's client is not the CEO but the institution and the public interest embedded in its licence. Deferral is not neutral — every week of delay is another week of harassment. Disclosure with a credible remediation plan is also the option most likely to *save* the 900 jobs, because it removes the tail risk that would otherwise destroy the firm later.

### 5. Values and citations to deploy

- **Values:** integrity, moral courage, objectivity, non-maleficence (*primum non nocere*), respect for human dignity, transparency, accountability, empathy for the vulnerable.
- **Kant's categorical imperative / "humanity as an end":** borrowers are being used merely as means to a valuation. Publicity test — could you defend this decision if printed tomorrow?
- **Rawls's "veil of ignorance"** and the *difference principle*: a credit system must be justifiable to the applicant most disadvantaged by it — squarely applicable to the postal-code bias.
- **Amartya Sen's capability approach:** exclusion from formal credit forecloses real freedoms, not merely a transaction.
- **Gandhi's talisman** — recall the face of the poorest person you have seen and ask whether your decision helps them; also his warning against "commerce without morality."
- **The Second ARC (4th Report, *Ethics in Governance*)** on conflict of interest and the duty of disclosure; **RBI's Digital Lending Guidelines (2022)** on recovery conduct and data minimisation; **Article 14 and 21** as the constitutional backdrop for non-arbitrary treatment and dignity.
- **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles of Public Life** — selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership — travel well into corporate compliance.
