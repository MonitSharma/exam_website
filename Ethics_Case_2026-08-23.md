## Case Study

You are the Member Secretary of a State Pollution Control Board (SPCB). A large textile-dyeing cluster on the outskirts of a mid-sized town employs nearly 8,000 workers directly and supports thousands more through ancillary trades. It is the single largest source of livelihood and municipal revenue in the district.

Over the past year, your field inspectors have repeatedly flagged that several units in the cluster are discharging untreated effluent into a seasonal river that feeds irrigation channels and a downstream drinking-water intake. Independent testing confirms heavy-metal contamination well above permissible limits, and the district hospital has recorded a rise in skin and gastrointestinal complaints in three riverside villages.

Under the Water Act, you have clear grounds to issue closure directions. However, the units have applied for time to install a Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP), and a credible engineering assessment says commissioning will take at least ten months. A closure now would throw thousands out of work overnight during an economic downturn.

You are summoned by a senior political functionary who conveys that closure is "not an option" before the coming panchayat elections, and hints that a "phased, understanding approach" would be viewed favourably for your career. Simultaneously, a delegation of affected villagers, accompanied by a local journalist, submits a signed petition demanding immediate action. One of your own inspectors privately admits he was earlier pressured to soften a report. The contaminated water continues to flow while you deliberate.

---

## Questions

**(a)** Identify the key stakeholders in this case and the principal ethical issues involved. **(10 marks | ~150 words)**

**(b)** Evaluate the following options available to you, bringing out their merits and demerits:
&nbsp;&nbsp;(i) Issue immediate closure directions to all defaulting units;
&nbsp;&nbsp;(ii) Grant the units the full ten-month extension they seek without interim conditions;
&nbsp;&nbsp;(iii) Adopt a calibrated regulatory response with binding interim safeguards.
**(10 marks | ~150 words)**

**(c)** What course of action would you adopt as Member Secretary, and why? Justify your decision. **(15 marks | ~200 words)**

**(d)** Which core values and administrative principles are in tension in this case, and how should a civil servant reconcile them? **(10 marks | ~150 words)**

*(Total: 25 marks. Attempt in roughly 250–300 words overall, in the style of a GS-IV case study answer.)*

## Model Approach

*Read this only after you have written your own answer.*

### Stakeholders
- **Riverside villagers / downstream water-users** — right to health, clean water, life (Art. 21); the most vulnerable and least powerful.
- **8,000+ cluster workers and their families** — livelihood, economic security.
- **Unit owners / industry** — business continuity, but also legal duty to comply.
- **The political functionary** — electoral and populist interests.
- **The pressured inspector and the field staff** — professional integrity, institutional morale.
- **The SPCB as an institution and you as Member Secretary** — statutory duty, credibility, rule of law.
- **Wider public / future generations** — environmental sustainability, inter-generational equity.

### Core ethical dilemmas
- **Public health & rule of law vs. livelihood and economic hardship** — the classic regulation-vs-development tension.
- **Statutory duty & integrity vs. political pressure and career self-interest.**
- **Immediate compassion (jobs today) vs. long-term public interest (health, environment).**
- **Transparency and accountability vs. quiet accommodation** — including how to treat the inspector's admission of past pressure.

### Evaluation of options
- **(i) Immediate blanket closure.** *Merits:* upholds the law, protects health decisively, signals zero tolerance. *Demerits:* disproportionate and blunt; devastates livelihoods overnight; ignores the good-faith CETP applicants; may trigger backlash and non-compliance. Justice requires proportionality, not just severity.
- **(ii) Full extension, no conditions.** *Merits:* protects jobs and avoids confrontation; superficially "understanding." *Demerits:* abdication of statutory duty; contamination continues for ten months; capitulates to political pressure; erodes institutional integrity and public trust. Ethically indefensible — it trades away citizens' health for expediency.
- **(iii) Calibrated regulatory response (recommended).** A graded, conditional approach: issue show-cause and interim directions; mandate immediate stop-gap measures (tankering effluent to an existing CETP, halting the most toxic processes, providing safe drinking-water to affected villages); impose a legally binding, milestone-based CETP timeline with bank guarantees and monthly public monitoring; close only the units that refuse to comply. *Merits:* protects health without needless economic collapse, honours the law, is proportionate and defensible. *Demerits:* administratively demanding; requires courage to resist pressure and diligence to enforce milestones.

### Recommended course of action
Adopt option (iii). Place public health first and non-negotiably — arrange safe drinking water for the three villages at once and order the immediate cessation of untreated toxic discharge, since no development justification can override citizens' right to life. Then pursue a transparent, time-bound compliance framework with enforceable guarantees, retaining the power of closure for defiant units. Record your reasoning and decisions in writing and keep the process on the record — this protects both the public interest and your own accountability. Politely but firmly decline the political direction to shelve enforcement; put any such instruction in writing. On the inspector's admission, protect him, document the earlier pressure through proper channels, and strengthen internal safeguards rather than pursuing him.

This reconciles compassion with duty: no worker is punished for the owners' default, yet no citizen is asked to drink poison so an election can be won.

### Values and principles at stake
- **Objectivity, integrity, and courage of conviction** (Nolan Principles; Second ARC's emphasis on public-service ethics).
- **Proportionality and the rule of law** — regulation must be firm but graded.
- **The "public interest" and inter-generational equity** — sustainable development balances environment and economy rather than sacrificing one.
- **Accountability and transparency** — decisions recorded, monitoring made public.

**Thinkers/anchors to cite:** *Gandhi's Talisman* ("recall the face of the poorest and weakest… will this be of any use to him?") aptly centres the villagers; **Kautilya's Arthashastra** on the ruler's duty to protect subjects' welfare (*yogakshema*); and the **Second ARC report on Ethics in Governance** on insulating the civil servant from improper political interference. The **Precautionary Principle** and **Polluter Pays Principle** (upheld by the Supreme Court) give a firm legal-ethical footing.
