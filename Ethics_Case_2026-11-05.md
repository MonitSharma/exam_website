# Weekly Ethics Case Study — 5 November 2026

**Setting this week:** Environmental clearance / regulatory body

---

## Case Study

You are a member-secretary of a State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA) in a mineral-rich, tribal-dominated district. A public sector undertaking (PSU), partly owned by the State government, has applied for environmental clearance to expand an existing limestone mine and cement plant. The project promises 4,000 direct and indirect jobs in a district with high unemployment and a recent spate of migration-related distress. The State government has publicly described the project as "a flagship of regional development," and the Chief Secretary has indicated that the Chief Minister expects the clearance before the upcoming investor summit, two weeks away.

While scrutinising the file, you notice that:

- The Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) report, prepared by an accredited consultant, understates the project's water requirement, and does not account for the drying of two seasonal streams that sustain agriculture in four nearby villages.
- The public hearing was held in a town 40 km away, and the minutes record "unanimous support", although a village-level petition from the Gram Sabhas, signed by several hundred residents and raising concerns about groundwater, land and forest rights under the Forest Rights Act, was received but never annexed to the record.
- A senior colleague on the expert committee, who has been your mentor, privately tells you: "The project is good for the region overall. A few conditions in the clearance letter will take care of the rest. Don't be the one who delays it. Your next posting depends on goodwill."
- A local journalist, aware of the petition, asks you informally whether the file is "being pushed through".

The company's management assures you that a CSR programme worth several crores will compensate affected villagers.

---

## Questions

**Marks: 20 | Suggested length: ~250–300 words (answer all parts in a single, integrated response)**

1. Identify the ethical issues and the stakeholders involved in this case. **(5 marks)**
2. What are the options available to you? Evaluate the merits and demerits of each. **(8 marks)**
3. Which course of action would you adopt, and why? Explain how you would deal with pressure from your mentor and the government, and with the journalist's query. **(5 marks)**
4. Which ethical values and principles of public administration are at stake in this case? **(2 marks)**

# Model Approach

*(Attempt your own answer first; read this section only after you have written it.)*

## 1. Stakeholders

- **Affected villagers and Gram Sabhas** (farmers, tribal and forest-dependent communities): livelihoods, water, land and forest rights.
- **Prospective workers / unemployed youth of the district:** jobs and income.
- **PSU / project proponent:** commercial and developmental interests; reputational risk if clearance is later quashed.
- **State government and political executive:** development agenda, investor summit, electoral considerations.
- **You (member-secretary) and the SEIAA / expert committee:** statutory duty, institutional credibility, career.
- **Your mentor/senior colleague:** loyalty, pragmatism, career guidance.
- **EIA consultant:** professional integrity and accreditation.
- **Media and civil society:** public's right to know.
- **Future generations and the environment:** inter-generational equity, ecological balance.
- **Judiciary / NGT:** ultimate reviewer of flawed clearances.

## 2. Core ethical dilemmas

1. **Development vs. environment and community rights**, short-term jobs versus long-term water and livelihood security (sustainable development).
2. **Loyalty to mentor and political executive vs. integrity and duty to law**.
3. **Expediency vs. due process**: meeting a political deadline versus a complete, fair appraisal.
4. **Transparency vs. institutional confidentiality**: responding to the media while a file is under consideration.
5. **Utilitarian "greater good" vs. rights of a vulnerable minority** (tribal communities, who have constitutional safeguards under the Fifth Schedule, PESA and FRA).
6. **Compensation (CSR) vs. prevention**: money cannot substitute for the loss of a water source.

## 3. Options and evaluation

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Grant clearance on time with strict conditions** (as mentor advises) | Avoids conflict with government; jobs and investment proceed; career safe | Procedurally defective record; conditions are seldom enforced; legally vulnerable (NGT/High Court); violates Gram Sabha and FRA rights; ethically a compromise of integrity |
| **B. Reject the application outright** | Protects environment and community; strong moral stand | Premature without a full record; disregards genuine developmental needs; may be seen as arbitrary; ignores the possibility of a remedied proposal |
| **C. Defer the decision and return the file with specific deficiencies: revised water-balance and hydrology study, a fresh public hearing in the affected villages, annexing the petition, FRA compliance certificate** | Upholds due process and the rule of law; protects the affected; gives the project a legally durable path; fair to the proponent; defensible in court | Delays the clearance past the summit; political displeasure; possible personal career cost |
| **D. Escalate/inform the political leadership formally and put dissent on record** | Creates a transparent paper trail; shifts accountability to where decisions are made | Alone it does not fix the record; may look like passing the buck if not combined with C |
| **E. Leak concerns to the media** | Public pressure | Breach of service conduct and confidentiality; erodes institutional trust; not a legitimate first resort |

## 4. Recommended course of action

**Adopt Option C, supported by D.**

- Place a detailed, objective note on the file recording the deficiencies: the understated water requirement, the missing Gram Sabha petition, the distant public hearing venue and the FRA/PESA status. Recommend that the proposal be returned to the proponent for correction, as the Environment Impact Assessment Notification itself allows, with a time-bound list of required actions. The Authority should not be seen as obstructing the project; it should be seen as making it legally sound.
- Present the matter to the committee and, if needed, to the Chief Secretary in writing. Make the point that a clearance obtained on a flawed record is likely to be quashed later, which would damage both the PSU and the government far more than a short delay. This turns the "delay" into the "safer route", a respectful way of resisting pressure without confrontation.
- **With the mentor:** thank him, acknowledge his concern for your career, but explain courteously that your statutory duty is non-delegable and a decision taken on an incomplete record would be mine to defend. Gratitude does not mean compliance; personal loyalty cannot override public duty.
- **With the journalist:** do not discuss the merits or leak the file's contents. Confirm that the application is under appraisal, that the process is being followed and that all documents, including the public hearing record, will be available under the proactive disclosure provisions and the RTI Act. This respects both confidentiality and the public's right to information.
- Explore, with the proponent, genuine mitigation measures: water-use efficiency, rainwater recharge, alternative water sources, and local-hire commitments, so that development and environment are reconciled rather than traded off against each other. CSR may supplement mitigation but must never replace it.

**Justification:** Option C satisfies the tests of legality, proportionality, fairness and public interest. It treats the vulnerable community as a stakeholder with rights, not as a cost to be compensated. It follows the precautionary principle and the principle of intergenerational equity, and it protects the institution's credibility, which is its real asset.

## 5. Values and principles at stake

Integrity, objectivity, impartiality, courage of conviction, accountability, transparency, compassion toward the vulnerable, rule of law, and sustainable development.

## 6. Thinkers and ideas to cite (1–2)

- **Mahatma Gandhi:** "The earth provides enough to satisfy every man's need but not every man's greed", and his talisman on the interests of the last person. Apply it to the affected tribal villagers.
- **Kant's categorical imperative / duty ethics:** act according to principles that can be universalised; a regulator who bends the record for one powerful project would sanction doing so for all.
- **Administrative values:** the **Nolan principles** (selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness) and the **precautionary principle** and **inter-generational equity** from the Rio Declaration, accepted in Indian jurisprudence (*Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum*).
