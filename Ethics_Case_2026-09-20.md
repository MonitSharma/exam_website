# Weekly Ethics Case Study — 20 September 2026

*UPSC CSE Mains — General Studies Paper IV (Ethics, Integrity & Aptitude)*
*For written practice. Setting this week: **Urban local body / Smart City SPV — surveillance technology, data privacy and institutional transparency.***
*(Deliberate change of setting: recent weeks covered environmental regulation, drug licensing, private-sector compliance, disaster relief and sub-divisional law and order. This week the conflict is privacy versus security, and disclosure versus institutional reputation.)*

---

## Case Study

**(Marks: 25 | Suggested length: ~250–300 words in total across the sub-answers)**

You are the Chief Executive Officer of a Smart City Special Purpose Vehicle in a city of two million. Your Integrated Command and Control Centre runs 6,000 cameras with facial recognition, sanctioned by the municipal board for three purposes: traffic enforcement, garbage-truck tracking, and tracing missing children. The system is built and operated by a private vendor under a five-year contract.

Three matters reach you in one week.

First, the Police Commissioner writes seeking bulk access to the facial-recognition database to identify participants in a peaceful protest against a proposed waste-to-energy plant. He cites law-and-order apprehension. No warrant is enclosed; the request falls outside the three sanctioned purposes, though the contract's language is loose enough to be read either way.

Second, your technical head reports that the vendor stored eleven months of biometric templates on an offshore server without authorisation, that this storage was briefly exposed, and that a sample of records appears to have been downloaded by an unknown party. The vendor asks for thirty days of quiet to "patch and verify," warning that publicity would collapse the project and invite penalties that could sink its small Indian subsidiary and its 200 employees.

Third, a junior data analyst who first noticed the exposure has already sent an anonymous note to a civil society group, which now seeks your comment. The Mayor, whose flagship achievement this project is, tells you the breach is "an internal IT matter," asks you to identify the leaker, and reminds you that your deputation ends in five months.

The Police Commissioner wants an answer by Friday.

---

## Sub-Questions

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved in this case and list the stakeholders, indicating the interest of each. **(5 marks)**

**(b)** Evaluate the following options available to you, bringing out their merits and demerits: **(10 marks)**
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(i) Grant the police the access sought, defer breach disclosure by thirty days as the vendor requests, and initiate an inquiry to identify the analyst.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(ii) Refuse police access without lawful authority, disclose the breach immediately to those affected and to the board, and protect the analyst from action.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(iii) Suspend the facial-recognition module entirely, pending an independent audit and a fresh purpose-limitation framework approved by the municipal board.

**(c)** What course of action would you adopt, and why? Justify your decision with reference to the principles that guide it. **(7 marks)**

**(d)** The analyst bypassed you and went outside the organisation. Was his conduct ethically justified? What does his decision suggest about the institution you head, and what would you change? **(3 marks)**

# Model Approach

> **Do not read this section until you have written your own answer.**

## 1. Stakeholders and their interests

| Stakeholder | Interest at stake |
|---|---|
| **Citizens whose biometric data was collected** | Privacy, bodily and informational autonomy, protection from identity misuse; the right to know their data was exposed |
| **The protestors** | Freedom of speech, assembly and dissent without surveillance-chilling; equality before law |
| **The Police Commissioner / law-and-order machinery** | Public order, crime prevention; legitimate but bounded |
| **The vendor and its 200 employees** | Commercial survival, livelihoods; also a contractual and legal duty they have already breached |
| **The junior analyst** | Protection from retaliation; conscience |
| **The Mayor and the municipal board** | Political reputation, project continuity; the board is also the sanctioning authority whose mandate was exceeded |
| **You, as CEO** | Integrity, legal liability, career; but your primary loyalty is to the office, not the occupant of another one |
| **Future public-technology projects** | Public trust in digital governance — the asset most easily destroyed and slowest to rebuild |

## 2. Core ethical dilemmas

1. **Purpose limitation versus operational convenience.** Data collected for traffic, garbage and missing children is being sought for a fourth, unsanctioned purpose. Consent — such as it was — did not extend to this. Function creep is the defining ethical failure of state surveillance.
2. **Security versus privacy, and the false framing of that trade-off.** The request concerns a *peaceful* protest. Identifying peaceful protestors is not public order; it is the chilling of a constitutional freedom.
3. **Transparency versus institutional and commercial reputation.** Those whose biometrics leaked have a claim to be told. Thirty days of silence serves the vendor and the Mayor, not them.
4. **Loyalty versus conscience.** The Mayor's request to hunt the leaker converts a governance failure into a personnel hunt — loyalty to a person displacing accountability to the public.
5. **Accountability in outsourced governance.** The State cannot contract away its duty of care. A vendor's non-compliance is the SPV's failure of oversight.

## 3. Evaluation of the options

**Option (i): Grant access, delay disclosure, hunt the leaker.**
*Merits:* Smooth relations with the police, Mayor and vendor; protects the project and possibly 200 jobs in the short run; avoids immediate public panic.
*Demerits:* Ultra vires the board's sanction and constitutionally suspect (*K.S. Puttaswamy* requires legality, necessity, proportionality and procedural safeguards for any privacy intrusion). Conceals a breach from victims who cannot protect themselves without knowing. Punishing the analyst sets a chilling precedent and is unlawful victimisation. The concealment will almost certainly surface — the civil society group already has the note — converting a manageable incident into a scandal. Morally indefensible and practically fragile.

**Option (ii): Refuse access, disclose at once, protect the analyst.**
*Merits:* Legally and constitutionally correct; honours purpose limitation; respects the rights of data principals; preserves the moral standing of the office; protects a conscientious employee. Enables affected citizens to take precautions.
*Demerits:* Immediate political friction; possible transfer; vendor and project disruption; the police may (with justification in some cases) complain of obstruction; abrupt disclosure without a remediation plan can cause avoidable alarm.

**Option (iii): Suspend the module pending independent audit and a fresh framework.**
*Merits:* Addresses the systemic cause rather than the three symptoms; converts a crisis into institutional reform; gives the board — the legitimate authority — the decision that is properly theirs.
*Demerits:* Slow; does nothing by itself about Friday's deadline or about the citizens whose data is already out; risks appearing as a technocratic deferral if not paired with immediate action.

*The options are not mutually exclusive: (ii) is the immediate response and (iii) is the structural one. A strong answer says so explicitly rather than picking one and ignoring the rest.*

## 4. Recommended course of action

1. **Answer the police in writing, courteously and on record.** Bulk access outside sanctioned purposes cannot be granted. Offer the lawful channel instead: a specific, written, warrant-backed or statutorily authorised request identifying named suspects and a cognisable offence will be processed promptly. Cite the board's resolution and *Puttaswamy*'s proportionality test. A written refusal protects both of you; an oral one protects neither.
2. **Disclose the breach — now, and properly.** Inform the municipal board and the state IT secretary the same day; notify the Data Protection Board and affected citizens under the DPDP Act's breach-intimation obligation; publish a factual advisory of what was exposed and what people should do. Bad news does not improve with age, and a thirty-day delay would make you a participant in the concealment rather than its discoverer.
3. **Act against the vendor institutionally.** Freeze offshore transfers, invoke contractual penalties and the data-localisation clause, commission an independent forensic audit at the vendor's cost, and place the contract's continuation before the board. The 200 employees are a reason for a graduated, fair process — not a reason to suppress a breach that harms millions.
4. **Protect the analyst, and say so publicly within the organisation.** Refuse to open a leak inquiry. Record in writing to the Mayor that the employee's disclosure concerned a matter of public safety and attracts whistleblower protection in principle; that identifying him would be retaliation; and that the correct response is to fix the channel he could not use. Counsel him privately that internal escalation should come first next time — while acknowledging that the institution gave him no credible route.
5. **Suspend facial recognition until the framework is fixed**, and place before the board a purpose-limitation policy: enumerated permitted uses, mandatory logging of every query, retention limits, an independent oversight committee with a non-official member, and a standing procedure for police requests.
6. **Accept the personal cost.** Your deputation ending in five months is a fact about your career, not an input into the decision. Record every step on file so the decision is institutional rather than personal.

## 5. Values and thinkers to cite

- **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)** — privacy is a fundamental right; any intrusion must satisfy legality, legitimate aim, necessity and proportionality. The single most cite-able authority here.
- **Purpose limitation and data minimisation** — the core principles of the DPDP Act, 2023; the Srikrishna Committee's framing of the citizen as a *data principal*, not a data subject to be processed.
- **Constitutional morality and the chilling effect** — surveillance of peaceful dissent injures Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b) even when no one is arrested. Dissent is, as the Supreme Court has observed, a safety valve of democracy.
- **Second ARC, 4th Report (*Ethics in Governance*)** — objectivity, transparency, accountability; and its recommendation that whistle-blowers be protected rather than pursued.
- **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles** — openness and accountability are breached by a thirty-day silence, however convenient.
- **Kant** — citizens surveilled to secure a political project are treated as means. Note also that the utilitarian defence (200 jobs, project survival) fails on its own terms once the destruction of public trust in digital governance is priced in.
- **Onora O'Neill on trust** — trustworthiness is demonstrated by disclosure that is costly to the discloser; secrecy purchased for reputation destroys the very reputation it protects.
- **Gandhi's Talisman** — the weakest person here is the citizen whose face was captured, stored abroad, and exposed, and who does not know it.

**One-line spine for the answer:** *Data collected for one purpose may not be turned to another; the citizen whose data leaked has a right to be told today; the vendor and the framework are fixed institutionally, and the employee who spoke is protected, not pursued.*

## Before You Move On

1. **Write your own answer first** — by hand, timed at about 30 minutes for 25 marks, roughly 250–300 words across the four sub-parts. Do not read the Model Approach until your sheet is done.
2. Then run the **"mains-answer-feedback"** task on your written answer to get it scored against UPSC's evaluation criteria.
3. Common traps in this case: writing a general essay on "privacy in the digital age" instead of deciding three concrete matters; treating the police request as a routine inter-departmental courtesy; offering the vendor a "compromise" of partial disclosure; and, most often, forgetting sub-question (d) entirely or answering it as a disciplinary question rather than an institutional one.
4. Sub-part (d) is where most candidates lose easy marks. The expected line: his conduct was *understandable and substantially justified* given the absence of a safe internal channel and the ongoing harm to citizens, though bypassing internal escalation was not ideal — and the deficiency is the institution's, not his. Then say what you would build: a protected disclosure mechanism with a time-bound response.
