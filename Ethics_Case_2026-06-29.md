*Setting this week: Regulatory body / Environmental governance*

## Case Study

You are the Member Secretary of a State Pollution Control Board (SPCB). For the past four years, a large textile-dyeing cluster on the outskirts of a fast-growing industrial town has been the backbone of the local economy, employing close to 12,000 mostly informal workers and accounting for nearly a third of the district's tax revenue.

During a routine audit, your technical team finds that the cluster's Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP) has been bypassing treatment at night and discharging untreated effluent into a river that feeds drinking-water intakes for three downstream villages. Sampling confirms heavy-metal levels far above permissible limits. The data also suggests that two field officers of your Board accepted the manipulated self-monitoring reports without verification, possibly knowingly.

When you prepare a closure notice, you receive a call from a senior official in the state government urging "restraint," citing the upcoming investment summit and the risk of 12,000 job losses. The cluster's association offers, in writing, to upgrade the CETP within eight months if allowed to continue operating in the interim. Meanwhile, a downstream Panchayat has petitioned you about rising cases of skin ailments and is threatening to approach the High Court. A local journalist has separately sought your "official comment" on the leaked audit.

You are due to retire in fourteen months. Acting decisively may invite political displeasure and possibly transfer; acting cautiously may prolong a genuine public-health risk and signal that regulatory capture pays. The file is on your desk, awaiting your decision.

---

## Questions *(Total: 25 marks)*

**(a)** Identify the key stakeholders in this case and the principal ethical issues involved. *(5 marks; ~120 words)*

**(b)** Evaluate the following options available to you, bringing out their merits and demerits:

1. Issue an immediate closure order for the cluster.
2. Permit interim operation against the association's eight-month upgrade commitment, with strict conditions.
3. Refer the matter upward and await government instructions before acting.

*(10 marks; ~250 words)*

**(c)** What course of action would you finally adopt, and why? Justify your decision on ethical and administrative grounds. *(7 marks; ~200 words)*

**(d)** Which core values and principles of public service are in tension in this case, and how should a conscientious civil servant reconcile them? *(3 marks; ~80 words)*

## Model Approach

*(Read this only after attempting your own answer.)*

### Stakeholders
- **Downstream villagers / Panchayat** — right to clean water and health; most vulnerable, least powerful.
- **Workers (≈12,000)** — livelihood and dignity of labour; would bear the cost of abrupt closure.
- **Cluster owners / industry association** — economic interest; also the party in default.
- **State government / political executive** — investment, revenue, electoral considerations.
- **The two field officers** — internal integrity question; possible collusion.
- **You, the regulator** — custodian of statutory duty and public trust.
- **Wider public, judiciary, media** — accountability and transparency.
- **The environment / river ecosystem** — a "silent stakeholder" and inter-generational interest.

### Core ethical dilemmas
- **Public health & rule of law vs. economic/livelihood pressure** — the central tension.
- **Statutory duty & integrity vs. loyalty to political superiors** (lawful vs. expedient).
- **Transparency vs. discretion** in dealing with the media and a sub-judice-bound matter.
- **Accountability vs. self-interest** — security of one's career so close to retirement.
- **Compassion for workers vs. justice for villagers** — two legitimate human concerns pitted against each other.

### Evaluation of options
- **Option 1 — Immediate closure.** *Merit:* upholds law, protects health, signals zero tolerance. *Demerit:* sudden mass unemployment and economic shock; arguably disproportionate when a phased, health-protective alternative exists; punishes workers more than owners.
- **Option 2 — Conditional interim operation with binding upgrade timeline.** *Merit:* balances health and livelihood; uses regulatory leverage to compel compliance; proportionate. *Demerit:* risk of continued harm and broken promises; can look like capitulation unless safeguards are genuinely enforceable and verifiable.
- **Option 3 — Refer upward and await orders.** *Merit:* procedurally cautious, shares responsibility. *Demerit:* abdication of statutory mandate vested in the Board, not the government; delay prolongs harm; effectively lets pressure decide a technical-legal question — the weakest option.

### Recommended course of action
Adopt a **calibrated version of Option 2, anchored in the law and made transparent.** Concretely:
1. **Stop the immediate harm first** — order night-time discharge to cease at once and divert the contaminated stretch / arrange interim safe drinking-water supply for the three villages in coordination with the district administration. Protecting health is non-negotiable and time-bound.
2. **Issue a show-cause cum directed-compliance order** under the relevant Water Act provisions: permit operation **only** subject to a real-time online effluent monitoring linkage, a third-party-verified, milestone-based CETP upgrade with a bank guarantee, and an undertaking that any breach triggers automatic closure — converting a vague promise into an enforceable instrument.
3. **Initiate departmental inquiry** against the two field officers; integrity within the regulator is a precondition for credibility outside it.
4. **Record the political call on file** and respond to superiors in writing, explaining that the closure decision is a statutory function of the Board; seek support, not silence. Put the reasoning on record to protect both the public interest and oneself.
5. **Handle the media factually and within rules** — confirm that action is being taken without prejudging the inquiry; do not leak, do not stonewall.

This path protects the most vulnerable, preserves the rule of law, uses proportionality, and refuses to let either career anxiety or political pressure substitute for professional judgement.

### Values & thinkers to cite
- **Constitutional/administrative values:** integrity, objectivity, impartiality, accountability, and **probity in governance** (2nd ARC, *Ethics in Governance*); the regulator as a **trustee of public interest**.
- **Proportionality and the public-interest test** in administrative action.
- **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles of Public Life** (selflessness, integrity, accountability, openness) for the framing of duty over expediency.
- **Kant's deontology** (duty and the categorical imperative — do not treat villagers' health merely as a means to economic ends) balanced with a **utilitarian** concern for the greatest welfare, reconciled through a **rights-protective, rule-of-law approach** rather than crude head-counting.
- **Gandhian talisman** — recall the face of the poorest, most vulnerable person (here, the downstream villager) and ask whether your step will be of any use to them.
