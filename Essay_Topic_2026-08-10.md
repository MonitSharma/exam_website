## Topic A — Philosophical / Abstract

> **"We do not inherit the earth from our ancestors; we borrow it from our children."**

*(An abstract, values-based topic on responsibility, time, and stewardship. You may interpret it broadly — it is not only about the environment.)*

**A possible thesis / interpretation:**
The proverb reframes ownership as trusteeship. What we call "ours" — the planet, institutions, cultures, wealth, knowledge — is held in custody for those not yet born. The measure of a civilisation is not what it consumes but what it conserves and passes on. Yet an over-literal reading risks paralysis; true stewardship balances present needs against future rights.

**Multidimensional points to consider (pick 6–8, don't list them mechanically):**

1. **Ethical / philosophical** — intergenerational justice; Rawls' "just savings principle"; the moral standing of future generations who cannot yet consent.
2. **Environmental** — climate change, biodiversity loss, groundwater depletion; the "tragedy of the commons"; carbon budgets as borrowed capital.
3. **Economic** — sustainable vs extractive growth; sovereign debt burdening future taxpayers; the idea of natural capital and green GDP.
4. **Political / governance** — short electoral cycles vs long-term policy; who speaks for the unborn? Constitutional duties (Art. 48A, 51A(g)) and the doctrine of Public Trust.
5. **Cultural / civilisational** — Indian traditions of *dharma* and trusteeship (Gandhi), indigenous stewardship, "seventh-generation" thinking.
6. **Individual / psychological** — deferred gratification, legacy, the human tendency to discount the distant future (hyperbolic discounting).
7. **Scientific / technological** — how tech can extend or exhaust the inheritance (nuclear waste vs renewables; AI and data as inheritance).
8. **International** — climate equity, "common but differentiated responsibilities," the Global South's right to develop.

**Quotes / examples / thinkers to weave in:**
- Gandhi: *"The earth provides enough to satisfy every man's needs, but not every man's greed."*
- The idea of **trusteeship** (Gandhi) and the **Public Trust Doctrine** (upheld by the Indian Supreme Court).
- The Iroquois **"Seventh Generation" principle**; Rawls' *A Theory of Justice*.
- Concrete anchors: the Aral Sea's disappearance; India's groundwater crisis; the reforestation of the loess plateau; sovereign debt passed to future generations.

## Topic B — Issue-based / Contemporary

> **"Artificial intelligence will deepen inequality before it dissolves it."**

*(A contemporary, argument-driven topic on technology, economy, and society. You are free to agree, disagree, or take a nuanced middle position — a good essay usually does the latter.)*

**A possible thesis / interpretation:**
AI is a general-purpose technology whose benefits concentrate first among those who own capital, data, and skills, widening gaps in the short run — across workers, firms, and nations. Whether it eventually broadens opportunity depends not on the technology but on deliberate choices: education, regulation, access, and redistribution. The essay can argue that inequality is a policy outcome, not a technological destiny.

**Multidimensional points to consider (pick 6–8):**

1. **Economic** — capital-vs-labour returns; automation of routine white- and blue-collar work; productivity gains accruing to a few "superstar" firms; the "digital divide" becoming an "AI divide."
2. **Social** — reskilling gaps; urban–rural and gender divides in access; algorithmic bias entrenching existing discrimination.
3. **Political / governance** — regulation (EU AI Act, India's evolving DPDP framework and AI advisories); data as the new factor of production; state capacity to tax and redistribute.
4. **Educational** — AI as a great leveller for learners (personalised tutoring, translation) vs a moat for the already-privileged.
5. **International** — a widening gap between compute-rich and compute-poor nations; "AI colonialism" and dependence on a handful of chipmakers and model providers.
6. **Ethical** — accountability for automated decisions; surveillance; consent and ownership of one's own data.
7. **Historical parallels** — the Industrial Revolution and the printing press: disruption first, broad gains later, but only with institutions (labour laws, public schooling).
8. **Optimistic counter-current** — AI lowering costs of health, legal, and education services for the poor; India's digital public infrastructure (Aadhaar, UPI, ONDC) as a model for democratised access.

**Quotes / examples / thinkers to weave in:**
- Erik Brynjolfsson & Andrew McAfee, *The Second Machine Age* — technology racing ahead of shared prosperity.
- India's **Digital Public Infrastructure** (UPI, Aadhaar, DigiLocker, ONDC) as evidence that design choices democratise technology.
- The **EU AI Act** and India's **DPDP Act, 2023** as attempts to govern the transition.
- Historical anchor: the Luddites and 19th-century weavers — displacement was real, but so were the eventual gains once institutions caught up.
- Kuznets curve framing: inequality rising then falling — but only conditionally.

## How to attempt this

- **Plan first (10 min):** decide your thesis and pick a logical order for 5–7 dimensions. A clear line of argument beats a scattergun of points.
- **Structure:** an engaging introduction (anecdote, question, or definition) → body paragraphs each carrying one dimension and evidence → a forward-looking conclusion that returns to your thesis.
- **Balance:** show multiple perspectives, then take a reasoned stand. Examiners reward nuance and coherence over one-sidedness.
- **Language:** simple, precise, and readable. Use examples and the odd quote — but don't force them.
