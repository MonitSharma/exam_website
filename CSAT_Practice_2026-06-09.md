## Section A — Reading Comprehension (6 questions, 2 passages)

### Passage 1 (analytical / economic)

> Economists have long assumed that as nations grow wealthier, the informal economy — those enterprises and workers operating beyond the reach of taxation and regulation — will gradually shrink, absorbed into the formal sector with its contracts, social protections, and audited accounts. Yet across much of the developing world this transition has stalled. Informality has proved not a transitional stage but a durable equilibrium, sustained by the very institutions meant to dissolve it. Where registration is costly, where tax compliance demands literacy and capital that small operators lack, and where the benefits of formalization — credit, courts, public contracts — remain practically inaccessible, the rational firm stays informal. The cost of visibility exceeds its reward.
>
> This inverts the conventional causality. Policymakers have treated informality as a symptom of underdevelopment, to be cured by growth. But informality may instead be a response to how the state is constructed: a tax on formality, levied through bureaucracy, that the poor decline to pay. Reforms that merely simplify registration, without altering the underlying calculus of costs and benefits, produce a brief surge of new registrations followed by silent attrition, as firms discover that being seen confers liabilities without commensurate rights.
>
> The implication is uncomfortable for those who equate measurement with progress. A government that succeeds in formalizing its economy may have done so not by enriching the poor but by making informality more painful than compliance — through enforcement rather than enticement. Genuine formalization, by contrast, requires that the state make itself worth joining: that the visible firm gain protections the invisible one cannot. Until then, the informal sector will remain not a margin awaiting erasure, but a verdict on the institutions that surround it.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Informal economies inevitably disappear once a nation crosses a threshold of wealth.
- (b) Informality persists as a rational response to the costs that formal institutions impose on small operators.
- (c) Governments should rely chiefly on enforcement to compel firms into the formal sector.
- (d) Accurate measurement of the economy is the most important goal of development policy.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that a reform which only simplifies business registration, leaving the cost–benefit calculus unchanged, will most likely:

- (a) permanently enlarge the formal sector.
- (b) deliver lasting gains in tax revenue.
- (c) cause a short-lived rise in registrations that subsequently erodes.
- (d) eliminate the informal economy within a generation.

**Q3.** The author's attitude toward a government that reports "success" in formalizing its economy is best described as:

- (a) celebratory
- (b) skeptical
- (c) indifferent
- (d) bewildered

### Passage 2 (abstract / philosophical)

> We tend to treat certainty as the natural reward of careful thought, and doubt as the residue of incomplete reasoning — a temporary discomfort to be dispelled by further inquiry. Yet there is a tradition, older than it is fashionable, that reverses this hierarchy. On this view, doubt is not the absence of knowledge but its most honest form, and certainty is less an achievement than a kind of forgetting: the moment at which a mind ceases to register the grounds of its own belief and mistakes habit for insight.
>
> The person of unshakeable conviction is admired for resolve, but resolve is cheap when it costs nothing to maintain. What is demanded of the doubter is harder: to hold a position while remaining answerable to the evidence that might overturn it, to act without the anaesthetic of certainty. Such a person is often mistaken for the weak or the indecisive, when in fact they bear a heavier burden — the burden of keeping the question open while others have closed it for comfort.
>
> This is not a counsel of paralysis. To doubt well is not to refuse all judgement but to judge provisionally, treating one's conclusions as the best available rather than the final word. The danger is not that we will believe too little, but that we will believe too easily — that we will purchase the peace of conviction at the price of the truth it was meant to serve. A civilization, like a mind, is healthiest not when its certainties are most numerous, but when it retains the capacity to be surprised by its own errors.

**Q4.** The passage primarily argues that:

- (a) certainty is always a superior basis for action than doubt.
- (b) doubt, properly practised, is a more honest and more demanding stance than certainty.
- (c) one should refrain from forming any firm judgements at all.
- (d) conviction is the highest achievement of careful reasoning.

**Q5.** The author's argument rests most directly on which unstated assumption?

- (b) Beliefs ought to remain responsive to evidence that could overturn them.
- (c) A civilization inevitably declines once it becomes certain of anything.
- (d) Indecisiveness and reasoned doubt are essentially the same thing.

**Q6.** Based on the passage, the author would most likely regard a person who revises a firmly held view after encountering strong contrary evidence as:

- (a) weak and indecisive, and deserving of criticism.
- (b) intellectually honest, since sound judgement stays answerable to evidence.
- (c) lacking the resolve the author most admires.
- (d) guilty of succumbing to "the anaesthetic of certainty".

# Answers

**Q1 — (b).** *Main idea.* The passage's thesis is that informality is a durable, rational equilibrium produced by institutional cost–benefit structures, not a passing symptom of poverty. (a) and (d) are positions the author rejects; (c) is the cynical method the author warns against, not the central claim.

**Q2 — (c).** *Inference.* The text states such reforms "produce a brief surge of new registrations followed by silent attrition" — i.e., a temporary rise that later erodes.

**Q3 — (b).** *Tone.* Words like "uncomfortable" and the claim that success may come "not by enriching the poor but by making informality more painful" signal a skeptical, critical stance toward claimed success.

**Q4 — (b).** *Main idea.* The passage elevates well-practised doubt over certainty as the more honest and demanding intellectual posture.

**Q5 — (b).** *Assumption.* The argument that doubt is superior because it keeps one "answerable to the evidence that might overturn it" presupposes that beliefs *ought* to be revisable in light of evidence. (d) is explicitly denied by the passage.

**Q6 — (b).** *Inference.* The author praises remaining "answerable to the evidence"; revising a view on strong contrary evidence is exactly the intellectual honesty admired. (d) is a trap — the "anaesthetic of certainty" describes *refusing* to revise, the opposite of this person.

**Q7 — (c). Both follow.**
- Conclusion I: "All poets are dreamers" + "No dreamer is a realist" ⇒ no poet can be a realist. **Valid.**
- Conclusion II: "Some realists are wealthy" identifies persons who are realists *and* wealthy; "No dreamer is a realist" means those realists are not dreamers. So some wealthy persons are not dreamers. **Valid.**
- Both follow ⇒ (c).

**Q8 — (b) Maternal uncle.** "The only daughter of my mother" = the man's sister (the man, being male, is not a daughter). So the woman's mother is the man's sister, which makes the woman the man's niece and the man her **maternal uncle**.

**Q9 — (a) 7 km East.** Place the start at (0,0), facing north.
- North 5 → (0, 5).
- Turn right (east) 3 → (3, 5).
- Turn right (south) 5 → (3, 0).
- Turn left (east) 4 → (7, 0).
- Final point (7, 0): due **East, 7 km** from start.

**Q10 — (c) 153.** The rule is ×2 then +1, +2, +3, +4, +5:
3×2+1 = 7; 7×2+2 = 16; 16×2+3 = 35; 35×2+4 = 74; **74×2+5 = 153.**

**Q11 — (b) Q and S.** P is 3rd from left (position 3); R immediately right of P ⇒ position 4; T immediately left of P ⇒ position 2; S at right end ⇒ position 5; the remaining person Q takes position 1. Order: **Q, T, P, R, S.** The two ends are positions 1 and 5 ⇒ **Q and S.**

**Q12 — (a) BQQMF.** The code shifts each letter forward by one (M→N, A→B, N→O, G→H, O→P gives NBOHP). Applying +1 to APPLE: A→B, P→Q, P→Q, L→M, E→F ⇒ **BQQMF.**

**Q13 — (a) Centre A.** Pass %: A = 900/1200 = **75.0%**; B = 1050/1500 = 70.0%; C = 580/800 = 72.5%; D = 1300/2000 = 65.0%. Highest is **A**. *Trap:* (d) Centre D has the most passes in absolute terms (1,300) but the lowest rate.

**Q14 — (b) 70%.** Total appeared = 1200+1500+800+2000 = 5,500; total passed = 900+1050+580+1300 = 3,830. Overall = 3,830 / 5,500 = 69.64% ≈ **70%.** *Trap:* (d) 75% is Centre A's individual rate, not the aggregate.

**Q15 — (b) 150.** 80% of 1,500 = 1,200 would pass next year; this year 1,050 passed; difference = 1,200 − 1,050 = **150.** *Trap:* (d) 450 = appeared − passed (1,500 − 1,050), an irrelevant subtraction.

**Q16 — (b) 133.3%.** Failed at D = 2000 − 1300 = 700; failed at A = 1200 − 900 = 300. Percent more = (700 − 300)/300 × 100 = 400/300 × 100 = **133.3%.** *Trap:* (d) 233.3% comes from 700/300 × 100, which answers "what percent *of*," not "percent *more* than."

**Q20 — (b) 10 seconds.** Opposite directions ⇒ relative speed = 60 + 48 = 108 km/h = 108 × 5/18 = 30 m/s. Combined length to clear = 140 + 160 = 300 m. Time = 300 / 30 = **10 s.**

**Q21 — (a) 4/9.** Total ways to pick 2 of 9 = C(9,2) = 36. Both red = C(4,2) = 6; both blue = C(5,2) = 10; same colour = 16. Probability = 16/36 = **4/9.** *Trap:* (b) 5/9 = 20/36 is the probability of *different* colours.

**Q22 — (b) 20%.** New price = 1.25× old. To hold spending constant, new consumption must be 1/1.25 = 0.8 of old, a reduction of 20%. Formula: reduction % = increase/(100 + increase) × 100 = 25/125 × 100 = **20%.** *Trap:* (a) 25% naively mirrors the price rise but ignores the new, higher base.

### Timing & strategy tip

**Open with the 3 Decision-Making questions (Q17–Q19) and the Data Interpretation set (Q13–Q16) before touching Reading Comprehension.** Decision-making carries no negative marking and costs almost no time, so it is free, low-risk marks to bank first; the DI caselet here is a single table, so all four questions share one setup — solve the percentages once and harvest them. Then do reasoning (Q7–Q12), where your speed is high and traps are visible. Save the two RC passages for last and budget a hard ~4 minutes each: read the passage once for the argument, then answer — and if a single RC inference question is eating more than 90 seconds, mark your best guess and move on rather than letting it drain the clock. Since CSAT only needs ~66/200, accuracy on the easy two-thirds beats heroics on the hardest one or two items.
