## Case Study

**(Marks: 25 | Suggested length: ~250–300 words in total across sub-answers)**

You are the District Magistrate of a hill district struck by a cloudburst and flash floods four days ago. Nineteen bodies have been recovered, roughly 4,000 people are in relief camps, and two blocks remain cut off. You have invoked emergency procurement powers, which allow you to buy relief material without tender in a declared disaster.

Three problems arrive on the same morning.

First, your Additional District Magistrate recommends a single supplier for 10,000 tarpaulin sheets and dry-ration kits — the only firm able to deliver within forty-eight hours from a nearby town. You learn informally that the firm belongs to the brother-in-law of a sitting legislator, and that its quoted rate is about 22 per cent above the last known rate contract. Every alternative supplier needs at least a week.

Second, an audit note shows that a young Tehsildar, widely respected for working three nights without rest, diverted 300 ration kits meant for a listed camp to a hamlet of seasonal migrant workers that does not appear in any revenue record and is therefore ineligible. He did not seek approval and made no entry for two days. The hamlet was genuinely marooned.

Third, the state relief commissioner's office has asked you to "reconcile" the casualty figure at fourteen, treating five recovered bodies as "missing, verification pending", to avoid a mismatch with the earlier official statement. Local volunteers who carried those bodies down have already spoken to reporters, and a television crew is seeking your on-camera confirmation of the toll this evening.

Relief columns move at first light tomorrow. Every decision you defer costs a day.

---

## Sub-Questions

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved in the above case and list the stakeholders whose interests you must weigh. **(5 marks)**

**(b)** Evaluate the options available to you on the emergency procurement decision, bringing out the merits and demerits of each. **(7 marks)**

**(c)** How would you deal with the Tehsildar's action? Would your response differ if the hamlet had suffered no casualties? Justify. **(6 marks)**

**(d)** What course of action would you adopt on the casualty figure, and why? Which values guide your choice? **(7 marks)**

# Model Approach

*(Read only after you have written your own answer.)*

## 1. Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Interest at stake |
|---|---|
| Flood-affected survivors in camps | Immediate shelter, food, dignity, timely relief |
| Marooned migrant workers | Survival; recognition as rights-holders despite no revenue entry |
| Families of the deceased | Truthful acknowledgement, compensation, closure |
| The Tehsildar and field staff | Fair treatment; clarity on the limits of discretion; morale |
| The ADM and procurement staff | Protection from later audit and vigilance liability |
| State government / relief commissioner | Administrative consistency, public confidence, political optics |
| The supplier and other vendors | Fair opportunity; freedom from arbitrary favouritism |
| Media, volunteers, general public | Accurate information; accountability of the administration |
| You, as DM | Integrity, legal defensibility of decisions, effectiveness |

## 2. Core ethical dilemmas

1. **Urgency vs probity in procurement** — speed saves lives now; an inflated, politically connected single-source award corrodes public trust and invites capture later.
2. **Rule vs compassion** — the Tehsildar broke procedure to serve the substantive purpose of relief. Do you punish good ends achieved by irregular means?
3. **Truthfulness vs administrative loyalty** — the demand to under-report the toll pits honesty and the families' dignity against hierarchical conformity and the government's image.
4. **Equity in relief** — whether entitlement follows revenue records or actual human need.

## 3. Evaluation of options

### (b) Procurement

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| Award to the single supplier as recommended, unexamined | Fastest; relief moves tomorrow | 22% premium is unjustified; conflict of interest unaddressed; sets a precedent of favouritism; vigilance exposure |
| Refuse and wait for competitive bids | Procedurally clean | People sleep without shelter for a week; defeats the very purpose of emergency powers; a defensible file is not a defensible outcome |
| **Award only what is time-critical, at a negotiated rate, on a fully documented file (recommended)** | Relief is not delayed; premium reduced through negotiation and rate justification; disclosure of the ownership link recorded in writing; balance quantity tendered immediately | Requires a few hours of effort; may still attract criticism |
| Requisition from other districts / SDRF stocks and NGOs in parallel | Reduces dependence on one vendor; builds redundancy | Uncertain quantity and timing; cannot be relied on alone |

**Recommended action:** Procure the minimum quantity genuinely needed for the next 72 hours from the available supplier, but (i) record the conflict of interest and the justification for single-sourcing on the file, (ii) negotiate the rate against the last rate contract and take a written price-reasonableness certificate, (iii) simultaneously float a short-notice quotation for the remaining requirement, (iv) place a procurement committee with the ADM and the district treasury officer on record, and (v) report the deviation to the state government proactively. Emergency powers are meant to be *used*, not abused — the test is whether each element of the decision would survive publication.

### (c) The Tehsildar

- Relief is owed to people, not to entries in a revenue register; the Disaster Management Act's duty is to affected persons. The **substantive end was right; the procedural lapse is real but minor and correctable.**
- Response: **regularise the diversion ex post facto** by a written order recognising the hamlet as an affected habitation, direct immediate enumeration for further relief, and record in writing why post-facto approval was justified. Counsel the Tehsildar — privately, not punitively — that discretion exercised in emergencies must be *reported within hours*, not concealed for two days, and issue a district-wide instruction creating a same-day reporting channel for such deviations. Recognise his effort publicly.
- **If there had been no casualties:** the answer would not change on the entitlement question — need, not death toll, is the trigger for relief. What would change is the urgency defence for skipping approval: with no life-threatening emergency, a routine approval was feasible, and a firmer written caution would be warranted.
- Note the systemic fix: the real failure is an exclusion error in the relief database, not one officer's judgement.

### (d) Casualty figure

- Options are (i) comply and report fourteen; (ii) refuse outright and say so publicly; (iii) report facts accurately through official channels while declining to be the public spokesperson on a disputed number.
- Under-reporting a death toll is not "reconciliation" — it is falsification of a public record. It denies five families their compensation and their acknowledgement, misleads relief planning, and, once volunteers and media contradict it, destroys the administration's credibility precisely when public compliance with evacuation orders depends on it.
- **Recommended course:** Report nineteen. Send a written communication to the relief commissioner explaining the verification basis (recovery memos, panchnamas, post-mortem records) and offering to explain the earlier discrepancy as a natural update of a still-evolving figure — which is entirely defensible in a disaster. Take the earliest possible written instruction, but do not wait for it to state the truth. In the evening briefing, give the verified number with the verification method, and avoid speculation on those still missing. If the pressure is repeated in writing, comply with the *record*, not the request, and escalate.

## 4. Values and principles at stake

- **Integrity and objectivity** — Second ARC's *Ethics in Governance*; the Nolan Principles of selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty and leadership.
- **Public interest over procedural convenience** — emergency powers exist to serve people; probity is the condition of their use, not an obstacle to it.
- **Compassion and empathy** — the migrant hamlet has no revenue entry, but has an equal claim; *Antyodaya*, and Gandhi's talisman: recall the face of the poorest person you have seen and ask whether your decision helps them.
- **Courage of conviction and truthfulness** — as in Article 51A(h) and the civil servant's duty to render fearless, honest advice; Kautilya's *Arthashastra* holds the ruler's happiness in the happiness of the subjects.
- **Discretion with accountability** — Max Weber's warning that bureaucracy must serve substantive rationality, tempered by Chester Barnard's view that an executive's real function is to make defensible moral choices where codes conflict.
- **Transparency** — the "sunlight test": any decision you would be unwilling to see published is a decision you should not take.
