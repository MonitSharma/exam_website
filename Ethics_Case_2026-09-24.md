## Case Study

**(Marks: 20 | Suggested length: ~250–300 words in total across the sub-answers)**

You are the Divisional Forest Officer (DFO) of a forest division in a mineral-rich state, responsible for the Stage-I forest clearance recommendation under the Forest (Conservation) Act for a proposed bauxite mine. The project would divert 640 hectares of dense forest that is home to a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group settlement of about 1,200 people across eleven hamlets. The company projects roughly 3,200 direct and indirect jobs and significant royalty for the state over a fifteen-year lease.

Under the Forest Rights Act, your recommendation cannot go forward until individual and community forest rights are settled and the Gram Sabhas of the affected hamlets have given prior, informed consent. Your field survey finds that only four of the eleven Gram Sabhas have passed valid consent resolutions; the other seven never achieved quorum. The district's tribal welfare officer tells you, off the record, that in at least two of the "consenting" villages, company-funded transport brought outside labourers who are not Gram Sabha members to inflate attendance and pass the resolution.

The state's Additional Chief Secretary summons you, stresses the project's importance ahead of an investor summit, and suggests you "forward the file on the paperwork available," since any litigation can be handled later. Your contract field surveyor, who first flagged the discrepancy, threatens to resign and speak to the press if the file goes forward unverified. The police separately cite unrest in a nearby Naxal-affected pocket as reason to "close the matter quickly, one way or another." Your recommendation is due in five days.

---

## Questions

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues and the stakeholders involved in this case. *(5 marks)*

**(b)** What options are available to you? Examine the merits and demerits of each. *(7 marks)*

**(c)** What course of action would you adopt, and why? *(5 marks)*

**(d)** What values and administrative principles are at stake in this situation? *(3 marks)*

*(Suggested length for the full answer: ~250–300 words. Attempt it as you would in the actual exam — in your own hand or typed, within 20–25 minutes, before reading further.)*

## Model Approach

*(Do not read this until you have written your own answer.)*

**Stakeholders:** the DFO (self); the eleven tribal hamlets and the PVTG community, especially the seven villages without valid consent; the mining company and its workforce/investment plans; the Additional Chief Secretary and the state government's economic priorities; the contract field surveyor/tribal welfare officer (a potential whistle-blower); the local police and law-and-order apparatus; the judiciary and statutory bodies (NGT, courts) who will review the file if challenged; the broader public interest in lawful, transparent forest governance.

**Core ethical dilemmas:**
- Rule of law and statutory compliance versus administrative and political expediency.
- Genuine, substantive consent versus manufactured, procedural "paper consent."
- Loyalty and responsiveness to a superior's directive versus objectivity, conscience and accountability to the law.
- Protecting a subordinate who raises an uncomfortable truth versus institutional pressure to stay quiet.
- Short-term law-and-order optics versus long-term legitimacy and trust in the state.

**Evaluation of realistic options:**
1. *Forward the file as instructed, relying only on the paperwork submitted.* Merits: avoids immediate confrontation with the ACS, meets the investor-summit timeline. Demerits: knowingly violates the Forest Rights Act, invites near-certain NGT/judicial challenge (as in the Niyamgiri precedent, *Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment & Forests*, 2013, where the Supreme Court held Gram Sabha consent to be determinative), damages the DFO's own integrity and accountability, and normalises manufactured consent as institutional practice.
2. *Refuse to process the file at all, citing the violations.* Merits: firmly protects tribal rights and is legally the safest position. Demerits: may exceed the DFO's role (the decision on rejection versus revision typically lies higher up), offers no constructive path, and could be read as obstruction rather than diligence.
3. *Submit an honest, disaggregated report — recommending clearance only for the four hamlets with verified genuine consent, flagging the seven vitiated ones, and proposing a fixed, independently-monitored timeline (e.g., 30 days) for fresh Gram Sabha meetings there.* Merits: legally defensible, protects the vulnerable community, keeps the project moving in a lawful and time-bound manner, and puts the actual facts on record for the ACS and any future review. Demerits: slower than the ACS wants, and requires the DFO to withstand real pressure.

**Recommended course of action:** Option 3. Record the field findings exactly as verified — which Gram Sabhas gave valid consent and which did not, and why — and submit this as the formal recommendation rather than a diluted one. Communicate to the Additional Chief Secretary in writing, citing the Forest Rights Act and the Niyamgiri judgment, that partial, time-bound clearance is both faster and more defensible than a blanket approval likely to be stayed by courts. Request police protection for the verification process itself (not for suppressing dissent), and take written responsibility for the surveyor's findings so the individual is shielded from retaliation. This path serves the state's genuine interest — a project that survives legal scrutiny — better than a shortcut that risks being struck down after work has already begun.

**Values and administrative principles / thinkers to cite:** *Rule of law and constitutional morality* — obligations under a statute cannot be set aside by executive convenience. *Rawlsian justice as fairness* — decisions should be tested against their effect on the least-advantaged party, here the PVTG hamlets. Gandhi's talisman — "recall the face of the poorest and weakest man" — is directly applicable as a decision-test. *Courage of conviction and accountability* — the DFO's duty runs to the law and the public, not merely to the immediate superior; moral courage here means putting unwelcome facts on record rather than editing them away.
