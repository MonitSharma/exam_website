## Answer key with explanations

**1.** (c) — Article 368 in Part XX lays down the procedure for amendment of the Constitution and the two special powers of Parliament in this regard. Certain amendments outside its scope can be made by simple majority. *[Polity · Easy]*

**2.** (b) — In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) the Supreme Court, overruling Golaknath, held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its 'basic structure'. *[Polity · Easy]*

**3.** (b) — India adopted the British Westminster model, marked by a nominal head of state, a real executive answerable to the legislature, and collective responsibility. The U.S. follows a presidential system with separation of powers. *[Polity · Easy]*

**4.** (a) — Federal provisions — the election of the President, Union–state distribution of powers (Seventh Schedule), representation of states in Parliament, the Supreme Court and High Courts, and Article 368 itself — require ratification by half the states. Fundamental Rights are amended by special majority alone, without state ratification. *[Polity · Moderate]*

**5.** (a) — Golaknath (1967) → Kesavananda Bharati (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980) → I.R. Coelho (2007). Coelho held that even Ninth Schedule laws enacted after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge if they violate the basic structure. *[Polity · Moderate]*

**6.** (c) — The 42nd Amendment added clauses (4) and (5) to Article 368 to give Parliament unlimited amending power and bar judicial review. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court struck these down, ruling that limited amending power and judicial review are basic features. *[Polity · Moderate]*

**7.** (a) — Single citizenship, All-India Services, and a centrally appointed Governor are unitary features. India has a single integrated judiciary (not a dual court system as in the U.S.), which is also a unitary feature; statement 4 mis-describes it, so it is excluded. *[Polity · Moderate]*

**8.** (b) — Article 75(3) provides that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Article 74 deals with the Council aiding and advising the President; Article 78 with the Prime Minister's duties towards the President. *[Polity · Moderate]*

**9.** (d) — All four — new states (Arts 2–4), creation/abolition of legislative councils (Art 169), the Second Schedule, and citizenship provisions — can be altered by ordinary law passed by simple majority, and are expressly excluded from the definition of 'amendment' under Article 368. *[Polity · Hard]*

**10.** (d) — All three are correct. Article 1 calls India a 'Union of States' (an indestructible union of destructible states); K.C. Wheare called it 'quasi-federal'; and under Article 3 Parliament can reorganise states without their consent, the President only referring the bill to the state legislature for its views. *[Polity · Hard]*

**11.** (b) — Article 112 requires the President to lay before Parliament an Annual Financial Statement of estimated receipts and expenditure for each financial year — this is the Union Budget. Article 110 defines a Money Bill; Article 265 bars tax without authority of law. *[Economy · Easy]*

**12.** (b) — Article 267 provides for the Contingency Fund of India, operated by the President through the executive to meet urgent unforeseen expenditure pending authorisation by Parliament. The Consolidated Fund and Public Account fall under Article 266. *[Economy · Easy]*

**13.** (b) — Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts excluding borrowings. It represents the total borrowing requirement of the government. Revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts is the revenue deficit; fiscal deficit minus interest payments is the primary deficit. *[Economy · Easy]*

**14.** (d) — All three are correct. The 'effective revenue deficit', introduced in 2011–12, nets out grants given to states/bodies for creating capital assets, since such grants — though revenue expenditure for the Centre — finance asset creation. *[Economy · Moderate]*

**15.** (a) — Borrowings, recovery of loans, and disinvestment proceeds either create a liability or reduce an asset, so they are capital receipts. Interest received is a revenue receipt because it neither creates a liability nor reduces an asset. *[Economy · Moderate]*

**16.** (d) — All three are correct. The FRBM Act was enacted in 2003 (effective 2004); it mandates statements laid with the Budget including the Medium-Term Fiscal Policy and Macro-Economic Framework Statements; and, following the N.K. Singh Committee, it provides an escape clause for deviations. *[Economy · Moderate]*

**17.** (b) — Charged expenditure includes the President's emoluments, salaries of Supreme Court and High Court judges, and debt/interest charges — these are non-votable. The Speaker's salary is votable; it is not charged expenditure. *[Economy · Moderate]*

**18.** (c) — Both are correct. The Appropriation Bill (under Article 114) legalises withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund to meet grants and charged expenditure, while the Finance Bill enacts the taxation proposals of the Budget. *[Economy · Moderate]*

**19.** (b) — The N.K. Singh Committee recommended a general-government debt anchor of 60% of GDP, split as 40% for the Centre and 20% for the States, with the fiscal deficit as the operational target on a glide path to 2.5% of GDP. *[Economy · Hard]*

**20.** (d) — All three are correct. The Finance Commission is constituted under Article 280; cesses and surcharges stay outside the divisible pool (a recurring point of Centre–state friction); and the 16th Finance Commission's award period begins 2026-27. *[Economy · Hard]*

**21.** (b) — The Champaran Satyagraha (1917, Bihar) was Gandhi's first satyagraha in India, against the tinkathia system that forced peasants to grow indigo on a portion of their land. Kheda and Ahmedabad followed in 1918. *[Modern History · Easy]*

**22.** (a) — On 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi day) General Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed gathering at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. The Hunter Commission was later appointed to inquire into the events. *[Modern History · Easy]*

**23.** (a) — Tilak founded his Home Rule League in April 1916 (Poona/Belgaum region) and Annie Besant hers in September 1916 (Adyar, Madras). The two divided the country between them to avoid friction. *[Modern History · Easy]*

**24.** (d) — All three are correct. At Lucknow (1916) the Moderates and Extremists reunited and the Congress and Muslim League concluded a joint scheme of reforms, with the Congress conceding separate electorates to the League. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**25.** (c) — Both are correct. In Kheda (Gujarat, 1918) the peasants, hit by crop failure, sought suspension of land revenue under the revenue code; Gandhi led the campaign with Vallabhbhai Patel and Indulal Yagnik among his associates. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**26.** (b) — The Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms) introduced dyarchy in the provinces, dividing subjects into 'reserved' and 'transferred'. Dyarchy in the provinces was abolished by the 1935 Act. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**27.** (d) — All three are correctly matched. The Ghadar Party was founded in 1913 on the U.S. west coast, with Lala Har Dayal among its leaders; the Komagata Maru, turned away from Canada in 1914, met tragedy at Budge Budge near Calcutta. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**28.** (c) — Both are correct. In the Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918) Gandhi intervened in a wage dispute between mill owners and workers and undertook his first hunger strike (fast) to hold the workers to their pledge; the matter went to arbitration. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**29.** (d) — All three are correct. The Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act) allowed detention without trial and was pushed through against the unanimous opposition of the Indian members; Gandhi responded with the Rowlatt Satyagraha, popularly summed up as 'no appeal, no argument, no advocate'. *[Modern History · Hard]*

**30.** (b) — The 20 August 1917 declaration was made in the House of Commons by Edwin Montagu, the Secretary of State for India. It formed the basis of the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms embodied in the Government of India Act, 1919. *[Modern History · Moderate]*

**31.** (b) — Lindeman's ten per cent law states that only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next; the rest is lost mainly as heat in respiration. This is why food chains rarely exceed four or five links. *[Environment · Easy]*

**32.** (b) — Eutrophication is nutrient (especially nitrogen and phosphorus) enrichment of water, triggering algal blooms; their decomposition depletes dissolved oxygen, harming aquatic life. It may be natural or accelerated by human activity (cultural eutrophication). *[Environment · Easy]*

**33.** (c) — Tropical rainforests, with high temperature, high rainfall and year-round growing conditions, support the greatest species diversity of any terrestrial biome. Tundra and taiga, being cold and seasonal, have relatively low diversity. *[Environment · Easy]*

**34.** (c) — Both are correct. 'Lentic' (from lenis, calm) systems are standing waters — lakes, ponds, wetlands; 'lotic' (from lotus, washed) systems are running waters — rivers, streams and springs. *[Environment · Moderate]*

**35.** (a) — Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Reef-building corals host symbiotic zooxanthellae and turn white (bleach) when they expel them under stress. Statement 3 is wrong: such corals need warm, shallow, clear, well-lit and normally saline waters — not cold, deep, nutrient-rich water. *[Environment · Moderate]*

**36.** (d) — All three are correct. Primary succession starts on lifeless surfaces (bare rock, new volcanic land) with pioneer species; secondary succession proceeds faster because soil remains (e.g., after fire); both progress toward a stable climax community. *[Environment · Moderate]*

**37.** (c) — Both are correct. The Ramsar Convention, an intergovernmental treaty for wetland conservation, was adopted in the Iranian city of Ramsar in 1971. Mangroves are halophytes with adaptations such as pneumatophores (breathing roots) and viviparous germination. *[Environment · Moderate]*

**38.** (c) — The profundal zone is the deep, open-water region below effective light penetration, where photosynthesis cannot exceed respiration. The littoral is the shallow near-shore zone, the limnetic is the sunlit open surface water, and the benthic is the bottom substratum. *[Environment · Hard]*

**39.** (d) — All three are correctly matched. The euphotic (photic) zone is the sunlit productive layer; the disphotic zone is the dimly-lit twilight layer where light is too weak for net photosynthesis; the aphotic zone below receives no effective light. *[Environment · Hard]*

**40.** (c) — Both are correct. GPP is the total organic matter fixed by producers; NPP = GPP − respiratory loss by the producers, and represents the biomass available to consumers (herbivores and decomposers). *[Environment · Hard]*

**41.** (a) — The 82°30' E meridian, taken as India's standard meridian, passes near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh. Indian Standard Time is set 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of Greenwich Mean Time based on this meridian. *[Geography · Easy]*

**42.** (c) — The Tropic of Cancer (roughly 23°30' N) passes through eight states: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram. *[Geography · Easy]*

**43.** (b) — The mainland of India extends from about 8°4' N (Kanyakumari) to 37°6' N. Counting the islands, the southernmost point (Indira Point in the Nicobar Islands) lies near 6°4' N. *[Geography · Easy]*

**44.** (d) — All three are correctly matched. Along the Shiwalik foothills lies the pebbly bhabar where streams sink; below it the marshy terai where they resurface. On the plains, bhangar is the older alluvium (with kankar) and khadar the newer, flood-renewed alluvium. *[Geography · Moderate]*

**45.** (a) — From south to north the Himalayas are divided into the Shiwaliks (Outer), then the Himachal or Lesser Himalayas, and finally the Himadri or Great Himalayas — the loftiest range with the highest peaks. *[Geography · Moderate]*

**46.** (a) — Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Himalayan rivers are perennial (rain plus snowmelt) while most Peninsular rivers are seasonal. Statement 3 is wrong — Himalayan rivers such as the Ganga and Brahmaputra have far larger basins than Peninsular rivers. *[Geography · Moderate]*

**47.** (d) — India's physiography is broadly divided into the Northern and North-eastern Mountains, the Northern Plains, the Peninsular Plateau, the Indian Desert, the Coastal Plains, and the Islands — all four listed groups are included. *[Geography · Moderate]*

**48.** (a) — The Yamuna and Ghaghara are major tributaries of the Ganga, and the Chambal is a tributary of the Yamuna (hence part of the Ganga system). The Godavari is an independent Peninsular river draining into the Bay of Bengal. *[Geography · Moderate]*

**49.** (c) — Both are correct. The Indus, Satluj and Brahmaputra are considered antecedent — they predate the Himalayan uplift and maintained their courses by cutting deep gorges as the mountains rose across their path. *[Geography · Hard]*

**50.** (b) — NCERT gives the coastline of the mainland plus the island groups as 7,516.6 km (of which the mainland accounts for about 6,100 km). Note: a 2024 government re-assessment using modern methods revised the total coastline upward to about 11,099 km, but the textbook figure remains 7,516.6 km. *[Geography · Hard]*
