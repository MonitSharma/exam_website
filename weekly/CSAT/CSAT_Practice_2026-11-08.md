## Section: Reading Comprehension

**Q1.** Passage: When electric motors began to replace steam engines in American factories in the 1880s, productivity statistics barely moved, and they did not move appreciably for nearly four decades. Economic historians who have studied this lag reject the simple explanation that the new technology was inferior; electricity was cheaper and more flexible than steam from early on. The difficulty lay elsewhere. Factories had been designed around a central steam engine that drove a single shaft, with machines clustered close to it and multi-storey buildings that minimised the length of the belts. Owners who merely swapped the engine for an electric motor kept this layout and therefore captured only marginal savings. The real gains came when a later generation of managers realised that each machine could carry its own small motor, which freed them to build single-storey plants arranged around the flow of production rather than the location of power. This reorganisation, not the motor, raised output. The episode is now invoked to explain why recent investments in information technology were also slow to show up in national statistics. The analogy is suggestive but should not be pressed too far. Electrification required replacing physical capital, which firms do slowly because plants are long-lived, whereas software can be altered quickly. On the other hand, the complementary assets that digital technology demands - new skills, new forms of organisation, trust in data - are intangible, and intangibles are poorly captured by conventional accounting, so measured productivity may understate real gains for years. A sceptic may therefore argue that the paradox is partly an artefact of measurement. Yet this reading is incomplete too, for it cannot account for the fact that many firms which adopted the technology early gained nothing. What the history suggests is less that technology takes time to mature than that its benefits are conditional on the willingness of organisations to unlearn the arrangements built around the technology it replaces.

**Q4.** Passage: We tend to speak of forgetting as a failure - a leak in the vessel of memory - and to measure the health of a mind by how much it retains. Yet a being that forgot nothing would not be a perfect thinker but an incapacitated one. Memory that preserves every detail of every experience offers no way to distinguish what matters from what merely happened; generalisation, which is the essence of thought, depends upon discarding particulars. The same holds for societies. Communities that remember every grievance with perfect fidelity cannot reconcile, and the durable peace that follows civil conflict has often rested on a tacit, collective agreement not to speak of certain things. It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that forgetting is simply to be welcomed. There is a difference between forgetting that occurs because experience has been absorbed and transformed, and forgetting that is manufactured by those who gain from silence. The first allows a past to recede; the second erases it. The victims of an atrocity who are told to move on are not being invited to forget but to forgo a claim, and the language of healing often disguises what is in truth a demand for convenience. What distinguishes the two cases is not how much is forgotten but who decides, and for whose benefit. A society that forgets by choice and with the participation of those who suffered has done something morally different from one whose forgetting has been engineered by its beneficiaries, even if the resulting silence is identical. The task, then, is neither to prize memory without limit nor to celebrate oblivion, but to ask of any forgetting whether it is something that has been done by people or merely done to them.

- (b) forgetting is a necessary virtue and should be encouraged after every conflict.
- (c) collective silence about the past is always evidence of oppression.
- (d) the moral worth of forgetting depends on who decides and for whose benefit, not on how much is forgotten.

**Q1. (b)** — (b) The passage's closing sentence says benefits are conditional on organisations unlearning old arrangements; (a) is rejected in para 1, (c) overstates the measurement point, (d) is contradicted by the caution against pressing the analogy. [Moderate]

**Q4. (d)** — (d) Stated in the penultimate and final sentences. (a) and (b) are one-sided; (c) overgeneralises. [Moderate]

**Q5. (b)** — (b) It is forgetting engineered from above, 'done to' people. The others involve consent, ordinary cognition or organic absorption. [Moderate]

**Q6. (c)** — (c) The author says such language 'often disguises a demand for convenience' yet still accepts consensual forgetting, so (b) is too extreme. [Moderate]

**Q7. (d)** — Method: B is 3rd from left. G at an end and two persons between G and D gives (G,D) = (1,4) or (7,4) [or (4,...) not possible]. Try G=1,D=4: remaining places 2,5,6,7 for A,C,E,F. 'FE' adjacent with A two places left of E: F=6,E=7,A=5, so C=2, and C is not next to D (2 vs 4). Check G=7, D=4: places 1,2,5,6 remain; 'FE' must be (1,2) or (5,6), putting E at 2 or 6, so A would be at 0 or 4 - impossible. Order: G C B D A F E. Middle (4th) = D. Answer (d). [Moderate]

**Q8. (a)** — Method: R=3; Q to the right of R and one person between P and Q gives Q=4, P=2 (Q=5 would put P at 3). S, T take 1 and 5 with S right of T, so T=1, S=5. Tea is adjacent to coffee (P=2) and not at an end, so position 3: R drinks tea. Milk is left of juice (Q=4) and positions 2, 3 are taken, so milk is at 1: T. Water is then S at the end. Order T P R Q S = milk, coffee, tea, juice, water. Answer (a). [Hard]

**Q9. (b)** — Method: M > N = O > P. So M > P (I true). P < N so P is not greater than N (II true). O = N < M so O = M is false (III false). Answer (b). [Moderate]

**Q10. (c)** — Method: Hour hand = 7 x 30 + 20 x 0.5 = 220°. Minute hand = 20 x 6 = 120°. Difference = 100°. Formula check: |30H - 5.5M| = |210 - 110| = 100°. Answer (c). [Easy]

**Q11. (d)** — Method: 5x1+1 = 6; 6x2+2 = 14; 14x3+3 = 45; 45x4+4 = 184; 184x5+5 = 925. Answer (d). [Moderate]

**Q12. (b)** — Method: Draw pens inside books; the 'files' circle overlaps books but is wholly outside registers. The books that are files are not registers, so I follows. IV is the simple conversion of 'Some books are files'. Pens may or may not touch files or registers, so II and III do not follow. Answer (b). [Moderate]

**Q13. (c)** — Method: Increases A 16, B 15, C 6, D 6, so A is highest in absolute terms. Growth: A 20%, B 60%, C 10%, D 20%, so B is highest in percentage. Trap: assuming the biggest gainer also grew fastest. Answer (c). [Moderate]
