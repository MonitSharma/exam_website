## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (science / methodology)

> When a scientific result fails to replicate, the instinct is to look for a culprit: a careless technician, a fudged spreadsheet, an ambitious researcher willing to bend the data. Fraud exists, but it is far too rare to explain the scale of the problem. A more troubling possibility is that the machinery of ordinary, honest science is itself capable of manufacturing false findings at an impressive rate.
>
> Consider the conventional threshold of statistical significance, which tolerates a one-in-twenty chance that an observed pattern is a fluke. Applied to a single, carefully pre-specified test, this is a modest risk. But a modern study is rarely a single test. Data can be sliced by sex, by age band, by season; outliers may be trimmed under one defensible rule or another; the decision to stop collecting data may itself be taken after a first glance at the numbers. Each such choice is individually reasonable, and each quietly multiplies the opportunities for a fluke to surface. The researcher who wanders this garden of forking paths need not intend to deceive anyone, and may not even be aware of what has happened; the false positive arrives looking exactly like a discovery.
>
> Publication then completes the work. Journals prefer the surprising to the null, so the findings that survive into print are systematically those that were luckiest. Replication, the traditional remedy, is expensive, unglamorous and rarely rewarded by hiring committees; a failed replication is often read as a comment on the replicator's competence rather than on the original claim.
>
> The remedies now proposed — pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans, compulsory sharing of data, journals that accept a paper on the strength of its method before any result is known — have one feature in common. They do not ask scientists to be more virtuous. They alter what the ordinary, self-interested pursuit of a career happens to produce.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Scientific fraud is far more widespread than the research community is willing to admit.
- (b) The one-in-twenty significance threshold is the single cause of unreliable science and should be replaced by a far stricter one.
- (c) The replication problem arises less from individual dishonesty than from routine analytical latitude and publication incentives, so the remedies must be structural rather than moral.
- (d) Replication studies are the only trustworthy means of establishing any scientific claim.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that the author would regard a researcher who produces a false positive by wandering the "garden of forking paths" as:

- (a) dishonest, though unlikely ever to be caught.
- (b) a rare aberration in a system that is otherwise self-correcting.
- (c) statistically incompetent, and in need of elementary methodological training.
- (d) typically unaware of having done anything questionable, and therefore not usefully addressed by appeals to personal integrity.

**Q3.** The author's confidence in pre-registration and results-blind acceptance rests most directly on the assumption that:

- (a) most researchers would behave dishonestly if given the opportunity.
- (b) the analytical choices that generate false positives are ordinarily made after the data have been seen, and can be committed to in advance.
- (c) journals will voluntarily abandon their preference for surprising findings.
- (d) replication studies will shortly become inexpensive enough to run routinely.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> Nostalgia has an unfortunate reputation. It is treated as a sentimental disorder, a failure of nerve before the present, and its sufferers are advised to look forward. The advice assumes that the nostalgic person believes something false — that the past was better — and that the cure is a corrected estimate. But this misreads what the feeling is about.
>
> What is longed for in nostalgia is very often not a period but a person one used to be: a self that could still become many things, standing in a morning that had not yet been spent. The kitchen, the school corridor, the particular quality of an afternoon's light — these are not valued for their properties. They are valued because they are the last places in which that self was seen. Told that the food was worse and the winters colder, the nostalgic person is unmoved, and is right to be; the objection answers a claim that was never made.
>
> Yet the feeling is not therefore innocent. Nostalgia edits. It removes from the remembered afternoon the boredom, the small humiliations, the low anxiety about what was coming, and returns a scene with the suffering taken out and the openness left in. What it offers is not the past but the past relieved of its future — a time viewed with the knowledge of how little of it remained, which is precisely the knowledge one did not possess while living it. Against such a scene no present moment can compete, because every present moment still carries its own unfinished dread.
>
> Perhaps the right attitude is neither indulgence nor suppression, but a certain wariness in the presence of one's own tenderness: an awareness that what moves us so reliably has been arranged to move us, and by an editor with interests of its own.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

- (a) Nostalgia is best understood as a longing for a former self rather than a former time, and its real danger lies in its editing rather than in any factual error.
- (b) Nostalgia is a harmless sentiment that its critics take far too seriously.
- (c) The past was in most respects worse than the present, and nostalgic people ought to be told so plainly.
- (d) Memory is so unreliable that no emotional response to the past can ever be justified.

**Q5.** It can be inferred that the author considers the standard rebuttal to nostalgia — that conditions were objectively worse in the past — to be:

- (a) decisive, though rarely persuasive in practice.
- (b) mistaken, since conditions in the past were in fact better in the respects that matter.
- (c) beside the point, because the nostalgic person is not advancing a comparative claim about conditions at all.
- (d) useful chiefly as consolation for those who cannot return.

**Q6.** The author's attitude towards nostalgia is best described as:

- (a) contemptuous, treating it as an indulgence to be outgrown.
- (b) detached and clinical, declining to evaluate the feeling at all.
- (c) enthusiastically approving, urging the reader to dwell in memory.
- (d) sympathetic to what the feeling expresses, but guarded about what it conceals.

## Answers & Worked Methods

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (c).** *Main idea.* The passage moves from "fraud… is far too rare to explain the scale of the problem" to analytical latitude plus publication incentives, and closes with remedies that "do not ask scientists to be more virtuous" but change what careerism produces — a structural diagnosis and a structural cure. (a) is explicitly denied; (b) inflates one contributing factor into the sole cause; (d) is never claimed.

**Q2 — (d).** *Inference.* "Need not intend to deceive anyone, and may not even be aware of what has happened" — hence moral exhortation is the wrong lever, which is exactly why the final paragraph praises reforms that bypass virtue. (a) and (c) both assign a fault the author is at pains to deny.

**Q3 — (b).** *Assumption.* Negation test: if the flexible choices were fixed before any data were seen, there would be nothing for pre-registration to lock down and the reform would be pointless. The argument therefore assumes the choices are post-hoc and pre-commitable. (c) is not needed — results-blind review is proposed precisely because journals will *not* change their taste; (a) is the moral framing the passage rejects.

**Q4 — (a).** *Main idea.* Two claims carry the passage: what is longed for is "not a period but a person one used to be", and "nostalgia edits". (b) and (c) are the shallow positions the author sets aside; (d) is an over-generalisation the text never makes.

**Q5 — (c).** *Inference.* "The objection answers a claim that was never made" — the rebuttal is not false, it is irrelevant, because nostalgia is not a comparative judgement about conditions. (b) is the trap: the author never says the past *was* better.

**Q6 — (d).** *Tone.* The author defends the nostalgic person ("is right to be" unmoved) yet warns of "an editor with interests of its own" and counsels "wariness" — sympathetic but guarded. Not contempt (a), not endorsement (c), and the closing recommendation rules out detachment (b).

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (a) U.** R = 1 (clue 4). T is odd and above 4 ⇒ T = 5 or 7. Clue 2 means exactly three people between T and P, i.e. |T − P| = 4. If T = 5 then P = 1 or 9 — 1 is taken by R and 9 does not exist, so **T = 7 and P = 3**. Floors left: 2, 4, 5, 6 for Q, S, U, V. Clue 3 (Q immediately above S) allows only (S = 4, Q = 5) or (S = 5, Q = 6). In the first case U and V take {2, 6}, but clue 5 needs U > V *and* U < Q = 5 — impossible. So **S = 5, Q = 6**, leaving {2, 4} for U and V with U > V ⇒ **U = 4, V = 2**. Final order (1→7): R, V, P, U, S, Q, T. Fourth floor = **U**.

**Q8 — (c) Both follow.** From "some files are documents" and "no document is a record": those file-documents cannot be records, so **some files are not records** — I follows. For II, records are disjoint from documents but nothing forbids every record from lying inside the non-document part of "files"; that arrangement is consistent with both statements, so "all records being files" is a genuine **possibility** — II follows.

**Q9 — (b) lo.** Sentences 1 and 2 share the code "ka" and the word "smart" ⇒ **ka = smart**. Sentences 1 and 3 share "pe" and the word "is" ⇒ **pe = is**. Sentences 2 and 3 share "ti" and the word "tall" ⇒ **ti = tall**. In sentence 2, ka and ti are used up, so the remaining code **lo = and**. (Also mo = she, zu = enough.)

**Q10 — (c) Mother.** Read right to left: "R − S" ⇒ R is S's sister; "Q × R" ⇒ Q is R's brother; so Q, R and S are siblings. "P ÷ Q" ⇒ P is Q's mother, and by the standard convention that siblings share parents, P is therefore the mother of S. **Mother.** (Trap: reading the expression left to right and stopping at Q gives no relation to S at all.)

**Q11 — (b) 5.** The top face is 1 in both positions, so Position II is Position I rotated about the vertical axis. In that rotation the old right face comes to the front: old right = 3 becomes the new front = 3 ✓, which confirms the rotation is one quarter-turn in that direction. The new right face (5) must then be the face that was at the **back** in Position I. Hence, with 1 on top, the side ring in order front → right → back → left is 2, 3, 5, — . Front and back are opposite, so **2 is opposite 5**. (The face opposite 3 stays undetermined — it is 4 or 6 — which is why the question asks only about 2.)

**Q12 — (d) Z26.** The numbers are the alphabet positions of the letters: B = 2, E = 5, J = 10, Q = 17. Differences: 3, 5, 7 — consecutive odd numbers — so the next difference is 9, giving 17 + 9 = **26 = Z**, and the tag repeats the position: **Z26**. (Equivalently the positions are n² + 1: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26.) Trap (a) Z25 keeps the right letter but the wrong number.

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (c) ₹960 crore.** ₹7,200 crore over 360° gives **₹20 crore per degree**. Infrastructure = 108 × 20 = ₹2,160 crore; Health = 60 × 20 = ₹1,200 crore; difference = 48° × 20 = **₹960 crore**. Trap (a) ₹48 crore treats the 48° gap as if it were rupees.

**Q14 — (a) 100°.** Current Education outlay = 90 × 20 = ₹1,800 crore. New outlay = 1,800 × 1.25 = ₹2,250 crore. New share = 2,250 / 8,100 = 0.2778, and 0.2778 × 360 = **100°**. Trap (b) 112.5° scales the *angle* by 25% (90 × 1.25) and forgets that the whole pie has also grown.

**Q15 — (d) D.** Back-calculate 2024 = 2025 ÷ (1 + change): A = 693 / 1.10 = **630**; B = 480 / 0.80 = **600**; C = 875 / 1.25 = **700**; D = 720 / 0.90 = **800**. Highest in 2024 = **D (800)**. Trap (c) C is the largest in *2025* (875) but grew 25%, so its 2024 base was smaller than D's.

**Q16 — (b) about 1.4%.** 2025 total = 693 + 480 + 875 + 720 = **2,768**. 2024 total = 630 + 600 + 700 + 800 = **2,730**. Increase = 38, so 38 / 2,730 = 0.0139 ≈ **1.4%**. Trap (a) 1.25% is the plain average of the four percentages (10 − 20 + 25 − 10) / 4 — invalid, because the divisions have different bases.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (d).** Balanced and rule-abiding: written disclosure plus recusal removes the conflict of interest, creates a record, and protects both the firm's legitimate bid and the integrity of the process. (a) recuses without transparency and leaves no record; (b) penalises a bidder who has broken no rule; (c) turns a conflict into a different kind of bias and is itself unfair.

**Q18 — (b).** Empathetic *and* accountable: it restores her agency and safety, stops the conduct now, and honours the reporting duty that a supervisor cannot lawfully waive — while limiting disclosure as far as the rules permit. (a) leaves the conduct running and may itself breach your obligations; (c) is a public confrontation that would expose her; (d) moves the person harmed rather than addressing the harm.

**Q19 — (c).** Proportionate enforcement: immediate action where the health risk is acute, a realistic compliance path with training and affordable licensing for the rest, and re-inspection before closure — protecting public health without destroying livelihoods. (a) is disproportionate and blind to consequences; (b) abdicates a statutory duty; (d) is buck-passing on a matter squarely within your own powers.

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (a) 108.** Cylinder volume = πr²h = π × 6² × 45 = **1,620π** cm³. Cone volume = ⅓πr²h = ⅓ × π × 3² × 5 = **15π** cm³. Number = 1,620π / 15π = **108**. Trap (b) 36 comes from using πr²h for the cone and forgetting the ⅓ factor (1,620 / 45).

**Q21 — (b) ₹36,000.** Capital × months: A = 40,000 × 12 = 4,80,000; B = 60,000 × 9 = 5,40,000; C = 90,000 × 6 = 5,40,000. Ratio = 480 : 540 : 540 = **8 : 9 : 9**, total 26 parts. One part = 1,04,000 / 26 = ₹4,000. C's share = 9 × 4,000 = **₹36,000**. (Check: A = ₹32,000, B = ₹36,000, C = ₹36,000; sum = ₹1,04,000 ✓.) Trap (a) ₹32,000 is A's share.

**Q22 — (c) 3 km/h.** Let u = upstream speed, d = downstream speed, and put x = 1/u, y = 1/d.
30x + 44y = 10 … (i)
40x + 55y = 13 … (ii)
(i) × 4: 120x + 176y = 40; (ii) × 3: 120x + 165y = 39. Subtracting: 11y = 1 ⇒ y = 1/11 ⇒ **d = 11 km/h**. Substituting in (i): 30x + 4 = 10 ⇒ x = 1/5 ⇒ **u = 5 km/h**. Stream speed = (d − u)/2 = (11 − 5)/2 = **3 km/h** (boat speed in still water = 8 km/h). Check: 30/5 + 44/11 = 6 + 4 = 10 ✓; 40/5 + 55/11 = 8 + 5 = 13 ✓.

### Timing / strategy tip

**Do the two RC passages last, but read the questions of each passage before the passage itself.** RC is where CSAT hides its 12–15 easiest marks, yet it is also where a slow reader bleeds ten minutes on a single abstract passage — so protect it by banking the reasoning, DI and decision-making marks first (roughly 20 minutes for Q7–Q22, which are mechanical for you), then spend the remaining 15 minutes on Q1–Q6 with the question stems already in mind. Knowing in advance that you are hunting for main idea, an inference and an assumption turns a second reading into a targeted scan instead of a re-read.
