## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (science / economic / analytical)

> Antibiotic resistance is usually presented as a biological problem to which the answer is better science. The framing is not wrong, but it is incomplete in a way that explains why forty years of warnings have produced so little. Resistance is, in the strict sense, a common-pool resource problem: the effectiveness of an antibiotic is a finite stock, held collectively, depleted by each use, and replenished — if at all — only slowly. A physician who prescribes a broad-spectrum drug for a probably viral infection captures the whole of the small benefit, while the cost, a marginal increase in the resistant fraction of the bacterial population, is dispersed across every future patient, in every country, in perpetuity. No individual actor in the chain faces the full price of the decision, and each is behaving reasonably given the price he does face.
>
> The supply side inverts the same logic. A newly discovered antibiotic is valuable precisely to the extent that it is withheld — reserved for the infections that nothing else will treat. A firm that has spent a decade and a great deal of money bringing such a drug to market is therefore rewarded, if it behaves as a good citizen, with negligible sales; and its patent, meanwhile, runs down whether the drug is used or not. It would be difficult to design an incentive more perfectly opposed to the public interest, and it is unsurprising that most large firms have quietly left the field.
>
> What follows is that stewardship campaigns and laboratory subsidies, however admirable, address the symptoms of a pricing failure. The remedies that have any prospect of working — subscription payments detached from volume, prizes awarded on entry rather than on sales, procurement that pays for availability rather than for doses — all share a single feature: they sever a company's revenue from the quantity of the drug consumed. Until that severance is made, the discovery of new antibiotics will remain an act of charity, and their conservation an appeal to virtue.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(a) Antibiotic resistance would be largely solved if physicians could be persuaded to stop prescribing broad-spectrum drugs for viral infections.
(b) The persistence of resistance is chiefly explained by the profit-seeking of pharmaceutical firms, which have abandoned a socially necessary field.
(c) Resistance persists because the costs and rewards facing both prescribers and producers are misaligned with the collective interest, so the remedies with any prospect of working are those that detach revenue from the volume consumed.
(d) The scientific difficulty of discovering new classes of antibiotics is the binding constraint, and public subsidy of laboratories is therefore the most urgent response.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that the author would regard a government scheme that pays a firm a generous price for *each dose sold* of a new reserve antibiotic as:

**Q3.** The author's claim that stewardship campaigns address only "the symptoms of a pricing failure" rests most directly on the assumption that:

(a) physicians are generally unaware of the consequences of over-prescription.
(b) the conduct of prescribers and firms responds primarily to the incentives they face rather than to exhortation.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> There is something faintly magical about a promise. A person utters a form of words, and an obligation exists where a moment before there was none. Nothing has been transferred; no one has been injured; the world is materially as it was. Yet afterwards the speaker is bound, and the hearer may complain if the binding fails. Philosophers have long been uneasy about how a mere act of will can conjure a duty out of air, and their attempts to dissolve the mystery are instructive chiefly in their failure.
>
> The most popular attempt makes promising a device for creating expectations: to break a promise is wrong because someone has relied upon it and will be worse off. This has the merit of grounding the duty in harm, which is familiar terrain. But it explains too little. A promise on which the hearer has not yet acted is not, for that reason, less binding; and a promise I fail to keep, in circumstances where the failure is never discovered and costs no one anything, still feels like something I have broken rather than something that has quietly lapsed. Reliance, moreover, reverses the direction of explanation. We are not bound because others rely on us; they rely on us because we are bound.
>
> A better account begins by noticing what promising is *for*. Human beings need a way of making their future selves available to one another — of placing some part of what one will do beyond the reach of one's own later reasoning about it. Without such a device, cooperation must be renegotiated at every step, and can extend no further than the parties' present interests happen to run together. On this view the obligation is not an awkward cost of the practice but its entire point: a form of words that did not bind would leave us exactly where we were, each of us alone with his own revisable intentions.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(d) Promises ought to be given the force of legal contracts, since informal assurances are too easily revised.

## Answers & Worked Methods

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (c).** *Main idea.* The passage runs demand-side externality → supply-side inversion → the single feature shared by workable remedies ("they sever a company's revenue from the quantity consumed"). (a) and (d) are the partial framings the passage says are "incomplete"; (b) is ruled out by the text's insistence that each actor "is behaving reasonably given the price he does face" — the diagnosis is structural, not moral.

**Q2 — (c).** *Inference.* The drug "is valuable precisely to the extent that it is withheld"; a per-dose reward therefore pays the firm for the very consumption that destroys the asset. (a) is the trap: it treats price level, not price *structure*, as the problem — the passage's remedies are all volume-detached.

**Q3 — (b).** *Assumption.* Negation test: if conduct responded principally to exhortation rather than to incentives, stewardship campaigns would be a cure rather than a symptom-treatment and the claim collapses. (a) is about knowledge, not incentives; (c) and (d) are outside the argument.

**Q4 — (c).** *Main idea.* The third paragraph is the thesis — promising exists to place part of one's future conduct "beyond the reach of one's own later reasoning", and "the obligation is not an awkward cost of the practice but its entire point". (b) is the account the passage rejects; (a) is contradicted by the fact that the author offers an explanation; (d) is never proposed.

**Q5 — (b).** *Inference.* Directly from "cooperation must be renegotiated at every step, and can extend no further than the parties' present interests happen to run together." (a) is the trap — the author's point is that revisability is a loss, not a gain in honesty.

**Q6 — (c).** *Attitude.* He credits the account with "the merit of grounding the duty in harm, which is familiar terrain", then says it "explains too little" and "reverses the direction of explanation" — respectful but decidedly rejecting. Not contempt (a); he clearly takes a side, so not undecided (b); and he does not adopt it (d).

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (c) C.** Number the days 1 (Mon) to 5 (Fri). Clue 3, "exactly two days between", means |D − B| = 3; with clue 2 (B ∈ {2, 3, 4}) the only possibilities are (B, D) = (2, 5) or (4, 1).

- **(B, D) = (4, 1):** days 2, 3, 5 remain for E, C, A with E < C < A, forcing E = 2, C = 3, **A = 5** — barred by clue 4.
- **(B, D) = (2, 5):** days 1, 3, 4 remain, and E < C < A gives E = 1, C = 3, A = 4 ✓ (A ≠ 5 ✓).

Unique arrangement: **Mon E, Tue B, Wed C, Thu A, Fri D**. Wednesday = **C**. Trap (d): D is fixed only in the surviving case, and is on Friday, not Wednesday.

**Q8 — (b) Only II follows.** For **I**: "some readers are critics" says the overlap lies *somewhere* in readers; scholars are a sub-region of readers, and nothing forces the overlap into it — a diagram in which every critic-reader lies outside the scholar circle satisfies all three statements, so I does **not** follow. For **II**: take the readers who are critics (statement 2, non-empty); no critic is a poet (statement 3), so those readers are not poets ⇒ **some readers are not poets** follows. Trap (a): I is the more tempting conclusion precisely because statements 1 and 2 share the middle term "readers", but the shared term is undistributed.

**Q9 — (a) VEZIY.** Opposite letters (position → 27 − position): B(2) → Y(25); R(18) → I(9); A(1) → Z(26); V(22) → E(5); E(5) → V(22) ⇒ **YIZEV**. Reversing gives **VEZIY**. (Check the example: M(13) → N(14), I(9) → R(18), N(14) → M(13), D(4) → W(23) ⇒ NRMW, reversed WMRN ✓.) Trap (b) YIZEV is the answer for anyone who omits the second step.

**Q10 — (b) 15.** Inclusion–exclusion: |S ∪ E ∪ H| = 65 + 58 + 42 − 28 − 20 − 22 + 10 = 165 − 70 + 10 = **105**. Students studying none = 120 − 105 = **15**. Trap (c) 25 comes from stopping at 165 − 70 = 95 and forgetting to add the triple overlap back once.

**Q11 — (b) North-west.** Measure bearings clockwise from north. Start south-west = 225°. Turn 90° anticlockwise: 225 − 90 = 135° = south-east. Turn 225° clockwise: 135 + 225 = 360° ≡ 0° = north. Turn 45° anticlockwise: 0 − 45 = 315° = **north-west**. Trap (d) south-east is the intermediate position after the first turn only.

**Q12 — (d) 95.** The rule is III = I² − II², i.e. (I − II)(I + II). Row 1: 36 − 16 = 20 ✓ (or 2 × 10). Row 2: 81 − 25 = 56 ✓ (4 × 14). Row 3: 144 − 49 = **95** (5 × 19). Trap (a) 84 is the plain product 12 × 7; (c) 119 is 144 − 25, mixing rows.

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (a) 26 : 19.** Split each total in its given ratio:

| College | Total | Ratio | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 840 | 4 : 3 (7 parts of 120) | 480 | 360 |
| Q | 720 | 5 : 4 (9 parts of 80) | 400 | 320 |
| R | 960 | 7 : 5 (12 parts of 80) | 560 | 400 |
| S | 630 | 5 : 4 (9 parts of 70) | 350 | 280 |
| T | 900 | 11 : 7 (18 parts of 50) | 550 | 350 |

Boys = 480 + 400 + 560 + 350 + 550 = **2,340**; girls = 360 + 320 + 400 + 280 + 350 = **1,710** (sum 4,050 = the sum of the totals ✓). Ratio = 2,340 : 1,710 = **26 : 19** (divide by 90). Trap (b) 27 : 20 is what you get by averaging the five ratios instead of pooling the counts — invalid, because the colleges differ in size.

**Q14 — (b) 61.3%.** Girls in P and Q = 360 + 320 = **680**. Boys in R and T = 560 + 550 = **1,110**. 680 / 1,110 = 0.6126 ≈ **61.3%**. Trap (d) 163.2% is the reciprocal ratio (1,110 / 680), i.e. reading the question the other way round.

**Q15 — (d) 1,056.** Surviving copies = 8,000 × 0.88 = **7,040**. Retail 65% + online 20% = 85%, so review copies = 15% of 7,040 = **1,056**. Trap (a) 1,200 applies the 15% to the original 8,000 and ignores the destroyed stock.

**Q16 — (a) ₹18.72 lakh.**
- Retail: 65% × 7,040 = **4,576** copies; publisher's receipt per copy = 450 × 0.70 = ₹315 ⇒ 4,576 × 315 = **₹14,41,440**.
- Online: 20% × 7,040 = **1,408** copies; discounted price = 450 × 0.80 = ₹360; publisher's share = 360 × 0.85 = ₹306 ⇒ 1,408 × 306 = **₹4,30,848**.
- Total = 14,41,440 + 4,30,848 = **₹18,72,288 ≈ ₹18.72 lakh**.

Trap (d) ₹25.66 lakh is gross sales value (4,576 × 450 + 1,408 × 360), forgetting that the publisher keeps only a share; trap (b) ₹21.28 lakh runs the correct share calculation on 8,000 copies instead of 7,040; trap (c) ₹14.41 lakh is the retail leg alone.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (c).** A structural-safety finding is not negotiable against a ceremony date. (c) refuses the false certificate, puts the position on record the same day with everyone who needs to know, offers a workable alternative so the event is not simply obstructed, and fixes the verification and the cost on the contractor. (a) accepts a substandard structure on the contractor's assurance; (b) is the classic self-protective file note — it records the risk while still certifying it; (d) is an unauthorised leak that substitutes publicity for the responsibility the engineer is required to exercise himself.

**Q18 — (c).** Balanced and rule-abiding: it converts an irregular absence into the leave he is entitled to (so the record is correct and he is protected), shares the load explicitly rather than by default, and respects his privacy while giving the colleagues a reason for the change and an end-date. (a) leaves the record irregular and the resentment growing; (b) applies the rules without using the relief they themselves provide; (d) moves the person rather than managing the situation, and marks his file for a temporary difficulty.

**Q19 — (b).** The donor is an entity whose contract your office supervises, so accepting anything from it creates a live conflict of interest regardless of value or branding. (b) removes the conflict, and — importantly — does not leave the genuine shortage unaddressed: it escalates it properly with a costed case. (a) accepts a benefit from a current contractor; (c) removes the publicity but not the obligation, which is the part that matters; (d) is refusal by inaction and solves nothing.

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (a) 4 : 38 2/11.** At 4 o'clock the hour hand is at 120° and the minute hand at 0°. After *m* minutes the minute hand has gained 5.5*m* degrees on the hour hand, so the angle between them is |5.5m − 120|.

- Right angle the **first** time: 5.5m − 120 = −90 ⇒ 5.5m = 30 ⇒ m = 60/11 = 5 5/11.
- Right angle the **second** time: 5.5m − 120 = +90 ⇒ 5.5m = 210 ⇒ m = 420/11 = **38 2/11**.

So the answer is **4 : 38 2/11**. Trap (b) is the *first* right angle; trap (c) 36 4/11 solves 5.5m = 200, i.e. it takes the hour hand's 4 o'clock lead as 110° (one hour-space too few) instead of 120°.

**Q21 — (a) 108 km/h.** Combined length = 180 + 220 = **400 m**.
- Opposite directions: relative speed = 400 / 8 = **50 m/s** = *u* + *v*.
- Same direction: relative speed = 400 / 40 = **10 m/s** = *u* − *v*.

Adding: 2u = 60 ⇒ *u* = **30 m/s** = 30 × 18/5 = **108 km/h** (the slower train is 20 m/s = 72 km/h, which is trap (b)). Check: 108 − 72 = 36 km/h = 10 m/s ✓, and 108 + 72 = 180 km/h = 50 m/s ✓.

**Q22 — (a) ₹40,000.** For 3 years, CI − SI = P[(1 + r)³ − 1 − 3r]. With r = 0.10: 1.331 − 1 − 0.30 = **0.031**. So 0.031P = 1,240 ⇒ P = **₹40,000**. Verification: SI = 40,000 × 0.3 = ₹12,000; CI = 40,000(1.331 − 1) = ₹13,240; difference = ₹1,240 ✓. Trap (b) ₹1,24,000 comes from using the **two-year** formula, difference = P·r² = 0.01P.

## Timing / strategy tip

Give yourself a hard **12-minute cap on Section A**, and spend the first 90 seconds of each passage reading *only* for structure — where the author states his own position and where he states someone else's. Every question in this set (main idea, inference, assumption, tone) is answered from that map, and none from a detail hunt; candidates lose Paper II not by getting RC wrong but by re-reading passages for facts the questions never ask about. If a passage still feels opaque after one careful read, answer the main-idea question, mark the other two, and move — Section D is worth the same marks with no negative marking and takes a third of the time.
