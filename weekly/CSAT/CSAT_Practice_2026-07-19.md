## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (science / policy)

> For most of the twentieth century a newly discovered antibiotic was received as a permanent addition to medicine's arsenal — a lock fitted to a door that would stay shut. The metaphor was comforting and wrong. Bacteria reproduce in minutes, carry their genes casually across species on small loops of DNA, and exist in numbers that make the improbable routine. Within a population of billions, a mutation that blunts a drug's effect will almost certainly already be present before the drug is ever administered. The antibiotic does not create that variant; it merely removes its competitors and hands it an empty field. Resistance, in other words, is not an accident that befalls a good drug but the predictable consequence of using one.
>
> This reframing matters because it changes what counts as a solution. If resistance were a design flaw, better chemistry would answer it. But if resistance is the shadow cast by use itself, then every new compound arrives with an expiry date written into it, and the only variable we control is how quickly that date arrives. A drug reserved for the cases that truly require it may remain useful for decades; the same drug poured into livestock feed and dispensed for untroubled coughs may be exhausted in a few years. The scarce resource is not the molecule but its effectiveness, and effectiveness is spent every time the molecule is used.
>
> Public policy has been slow to absorb this, partly because the costs are diffuse and delayed while the benefits of prescribing are immediate and personal. A physician who withholds an unnecessary course protects a stranger, years hence, from an infection that will never be traced back to that restraint. It is an unpromising basis for individual virtue, and a strong argument for treating antibiotic effectiveness the way we treat any commons that no single user has reason to conserve.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(a) Pharmaceutical research has stagnated, and no genuinely new classes of antibiotics are being discovered.
(b) Because resistance is the predictable consequence of use rather than a defect of particular drugs, preserving effectiveness is a problem of governing shared use rather than of better chemistry.
(c) Physicians who over-prescribe are the principal cause of the resistance crisis and should bear the cost of it.
(d) Bacteria mutate deliberately in response to the drugs they encounter, which makes any medical countermeasure futile.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that the author would regard the discovery of a powerful new antibiotic as:

(a) A lasting solution, provided the compound is chemically distinct from existing classes.
(b) Genuinely valuable, but self-consuming — its useful life determined less by its chemistry than by how sparingly it is deployed.
(c) Largely irrelevant, since resistance will emerge at the same rate regardless of how the drug is used.
(d) Harmful on balance, because any new drug accelerates the spread of resistance genes across species.

**Q3.** The argument in the final paragraph rests most directly on the assumption that:

(a) Physicians are generally indifferent to the welfare of their patients.
(b) Individuals cannot be relied upon to restrain their own use of a resource when the costs of that use are delayed and fall largely on others.
(c) Agricultural use of antibiotics is the single largest driver of resistance worldwide.
(d) Resistant infections can, with sufficient effort, be traced back to the particular prescriptions that produced them.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> A promise is a strange instrument. At the moment it is made, one person binds another person — himself, later — who has not been consulted and cannot object. The self who promised on Monday to rise early on Friday is not quite the self who must obey on Friday: he has different appetites, different information, perhaps different judgment. On what authority does the earlier bind the later?
>
> One answer is that no such authority exists, and that keeping promises is merely prudent — we honour them because we wish to be trusted, and trust pays. But this account explains too little. It cannot say why a promise made to the dying, whose knowledge of our conduct has ended, retains its grip; nor why we feel the pull of promises whose breach no one would ever discover. Whatever binds us in these cases is not the anticipation of being seen.
>
> A more demanding view reverses the order of explanation. It is not that a continuous self exists first and then acquires the power to make promises; rather, the practice of holding oneself to yesterday's word is part of what makes a life something more than a sequence of loosely related episodes. To be a person, on this account, is largely to be answerable — to be the sort of creature whose past commitments have standing against present preference. The promise does not report a continuity already there; it manufactures it.
>
> This has an uncomfortable implication. Each broken promise is not only a wrong done to another but a small dissolution of the promiser, a vote cast for the view that Monday's intentions have no claim on Friday. Fidelity, so understood, is less a decoration upon character than the material of which character is made — and unreliability less a lapse of will than a way of failing to be anyone in particular.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(a) Promises are binding only to the extent that breaking them would damage one's reputation for trustworthiness.
(b) Since the self changes over time, obligations incurred in the past cannot reasonably bind a person in the present.
(c) Keeping promises does not merely presuppose a continuous self but helps constitute one, which is why a breach injures the promiser as well as the promisee.
(d) Moral philosophy has overstated the importance of promise-keeping relative to other virtues.

**Q5.** The author's mention of promises made to the dying, and of breaches no one would ever discover, functions chiefly to:

(a) Illustrate the practical difficulty of enforcing promises in the absence of witnesses.
(b) Show that the prudential account of promising cannot explain the binding force we in fact experience.
(c) Argue that promises made under emotional strain deserve special moral weight.
(d) Concede that some promises may legitimately be broken without wronging anyone.

**Q6.** The tone of the passage is best described as:

(a) Indignant and censorious.
(b) Detached and sceptical.
(c) Reflective and quietly demanding.
(d) Nostalgic and resigned.

## Answers & Worked Methods

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (b).** *Main idea.* The passage's pivot is "resistance is not an accident that befalls a good drug but the predictable consequence of using one", and its conclusion is that effectiveness should be treated as "a commons". (a) is never claimed; (c) is one contributing factor, not the thesis; (d) misstates the biology — the passage says the mutation pre-exists and the drug merely clears its competitors.

**Q2 — (b).** *Inference.* "Every new compound arrives with an expiry date written into it, and the only variable we control is how quickly that date arrives" — so a new drug is real but perishable, its life set by usage discipline. (c) contradicts the reserved-vs-poured contrast; (d) overstates.

**Q3 — (b).** *Assumption.* Negation test: if individuals *could* be relied on to restrain use despite diffuse, delayed costs, then the "unpromising basis for individual virtue" line and the call for commons-style governance collapse. (d) is in fact denied by the text ("never be traced back"); (a) and (c) are not asserted.

**Q4 — (c).** *Main idea.* The third and fourth paragraphs carry the thesis: the promise "does not report a continuity already there; it manufactures it", hence a breach is "a small dissolution of the promiser". (a) is the prudential view the author rejects; (b) is the puzzle posed, not the answer.

**Q5 — (b).** *Function / inference.* Both examples are cases where reputation is irrelevant, introduced immediately after "But this account explains too little" — they are counter-examples to the prudential account.

**Q6 — (c).** *Tone.* The author reasons through a puzzle rather than denouncing anyone (rules out (a)), reaches firm moral conclusions rather than withholding judgement (rules out (b)), and looks forward, not back (rules out (d)).

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (b).** Number the days Mon = 1 … Fri = 5. S = 3, so the consecutive pair (R, T) must sit in {1,2} or {4,5}.
- If R = 4, T = 5, then P and Q take days 1 and 2. P ≠ 1, so P = 2 and Q = 1 — but that violates P earlier than Q. Rejected.
- If R = 1, T = 2, then P and Q take days 4 and 5, and P < Q gives P = 4, Q = 5. All clues satisfied.

Unique arrangement: **R–T–S–P–Q** (Mon–Fri). Friday = **Q**.

**Q8 — (b).** Test A.
- Suppose **A is truthful** → B is a liar → B's claim ("A and C are the same type") is false → C differs from A → C is a liar → C's claim ("B is a truth-teller") is false → B is a liar. ✔ Fully consistent.
- Suppose **A is a liar** → B is truthful → A and C are the same type → C is a liar → C's claim is false → B is a liar. ✘ Contradicts B being truthful.

So A alone is truthful: exactly **1**.

**Q9 — (a).** Statement 2 guarantees at least one painter who is a banker; by Statement 1 no banker is a poet, so that painter is not a poet — **I follows**. II claims *no* painter is a poet, but painters outside the banker overlap may freely be poets, so II is only a possibility, not a certainty.

**Q10 — (a).** Shift each letter +3: M→P, A→D, S→V, T→W, E→H, R→**U**. Only U is a vowel, so it becomes '#': **PDVWH#**. (b) forgets the exception; (c) wrongly converts P (the shift of M) as if the *original* letter mattered; (d) applies the rule to the wrong position.

**Q11 — (b).** Inclusion–exclusion:
|F ∪ G ∪ S| = 70 + 55 + 40 − 25 − 18 − 22 + 10 = 165 − 65 + 10 = **110**.
None = 120 − 110 = **10**. (Trap (d) 20 comes from omitting the final "+ all three" term.)

**Q12 — (a).** Chain the first two rules: published before 1980 → in reference section → cannot be borrowed. So (a) must hold. (b) is the converse error — the third rule explicitly supplies non-borrowable books published after 2000. (c) and (d) directly contradict the third rule.

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (d).** Average per deal = total ÷ deals:
Fintech 2400/40 = 60; Healthtech 1750/25 = 70; Edtech 1200/30 = 40; Agritech 1050/15 = 70; Logistics 1600/20 = **80**. Highest is **Logistics**. (Trap: Fintech has the largest *total* funding but only a mid-range average.)

**Q14 — (c).** Overall total funding = 2400 + 1750 + 1200 + 1050 + 1600 = ₹8,000 crore; total deals = 40 + 25 + 30 + 15 + 20 = 130. Overall average = 8000/130 ≈ **₹61.5 crore per deal**. Sectors above it: Healthtech (70), Agritech (70), Logistics (80) → **3**. Fintech at 60 falls just below — the item that decides this question.

**Q15 — (b).** Department sizes: General Medicine 40% × 4500 = 1800; Orthopaedics 24% × 4500 = 1080; Paediatrics 20% × 4500 = 900; Dermatology = remaining 16% × 4500 = 720. (Check: 1800 + 1080 + 900 + 720 = 4500 ✔)
Referrals: 1800 × ¼ = 450; 1080 × ⅓ = 360; 900 × ⅕ = 180; 720 × ⅜ = 270.
Total = 450 + 360 + 180 + 270 = **1,260**.

**Q16 — (a).** From the same computation, the referral counts are 450, 360, 180 and 270 — the largest is **General Medicine**. The trap is Dermatology, which has the highest referral *rate* (⅜ = 37.5%, against 33.3%, 25% and 20%) but the smallest patient base, so it contributes only 270. Rate ≠ volume.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (b).** Balanced and rule-abiding: it removes the conflict of interest, preserves the integrity of the bill assessment, creates a written record, and still leaves a legitimate route for the school to benefit — without personal association. (a) accepts a benefit from a party with a pending claim; (c) is punitive and misuses inspection powers; (d) merely disguises the conflict.

**Q18 — (c).** Empathetic *and* accountable: it addresses the human situation privately, protects the deliverable through consented redistribution, and keeps management honestly informed rather than concealing risk. (a) hides a known delivery risk; (b) is rigid and uncaring; (d) outsources a first-line manager's responsibility.

**Q19 — (c).** Verify first, then communicate with facts, engage the aggrieved directly, and invite independent scrutiny — this restores trust rather than merely asserting innocence. (a) lets a falsehood harden; (b) is intimidatory and worsens perception; (d) is defensive posturing without verification and abdicates the officer's own responsibility.

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (b).** Opposite directions → speeds add: 54 + 36 = 90 km/h = 90 × 5/18 = **25 m/s**. Distance to clear = 180 + 120 = 300 m. Time = 300/25 = **12 seconds**. (Using the *difference* of speeds — the same-direction case — would give 5 m/s and 60 s, which is not on offer; (d) is the bait for a 300/15 slip.)

**Q21 — (b).** For 3 years at rate r, difference = P[(1+r)³ − 1 − 3r]. With r = 0.10:
(1.1)³ = 1.331, so difference = P(1.331 − 1 − 0.3) = **0.031P**.
0.031P = 930 → P = 930/0.031 = **₹30,000**.
Verification: SI = 30000 × 0.3 = ₹9,000; CI = 30000(1.331) − 30000 = ₹9,930; difference = ₹930 ✔. (Trap (c) ₹31,000 comes from using the 2-year formula factor 0.03.)

**Q22 — (b).** Take total work = LCM(12, 18) = 36 units. A does 3 units/day, B does 2 units/day; one A+B pair of days = 5 units.
7 pairs = 14 days → 35 units done, 1 unit left.
Day 15 is A's turn (3 units/day), so the last unit needs 1/3 of a day.
Total = 14 + ⅓ = **14⅓ days**. (Note the order matters: had B started, the remainder would have taken ½ day.)

## Timing & strategy tip

**Spend your first pass only on questions whose answer is decided by one clean computation or one clean rule — and physically skip anything that needs a second reading.** Concretely: open with the three Decision-Making items (Q17–Q19, no negative marking, ~3 minutes total), then the two DI sets, where a *single* setup pays for two questions each (the five per-deal averages settle both Q13 and Q14; the four department counts settle both Q15 and Q16 — compute them once, on paper, and don't recompute). Take the reasoning block next: Q9, Q10 and Q12 are pure one-rule items worth under a minute each, while Q7 and Q8 need a short case-split — cap those at two minutes apiece. Leave the two RC passages for last at ~4 minutes each: read once for the *argument*, not the details, since every question here is main-idea, inference, assumption or tone, and none rewards hunting for a sentence. Given that ~66/200 qualifies, the cheapest 14–15 correct answers in this set already clear the bar with room to spare — so a question you abandon at the 90-second mark costs you nothing, while one you stubbornly grind through can cost you three easier ones.
