## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (economic / analytical)

> A string quartet playing a Beethoven composition in 1830 required four musicians and roughly forty minutes; the same performance today requires exactly the same four musicians and the same forty minutes. Over the intervening two centuries the productivity of a weaver, a farmer or a steelworker has multiplied many times over, but the productivity of the quartet has not moved at all. From this unremarkable observation the economist William Baumol drew a conclusion that continues to unsettle policy debate: in an economy where some sectors grow steadily more productive and others cannot, the costs of the stagnant sectors must rise relative to everything else, and must go on rising indefinitely.
>
> The mechanism is not mysterious. Musicians, nurses and schoolteachers are not sealed off from the wider labour market; if wages in manufacturing climb with output per worker, the stagnant sectors must eventually match those wages or lose their people to other work. But they cannot pay for higher wages out of higher productivity, because there is none to be had. The bill therefore appears as price inflation in the affected services — which is to say, in health, education, live performance, policing and personal care.
>
> Two conclusions are commonly drawn, and only one of them survives scrutiny. The first is that these sectors have become inefficient or badly managed, and that the remedy is discipline. The second is that they have become expensive precisely because the rest of the economy has become rich, and that a society growing wealthier should expect to spend an ever larger share of its income on the things machines cannot do faster. The first invites an endless search for waste that will not be found; the second invites an uncomfortable political question about what we are willing to fund. To complain that teaching costs more than it once did is, on this view, another way of saying that we have become more productive at everything else.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(a) Public services such as health and education have grown wasteful and require stronger management discipline.
(b) Rising costs in labour-intensive services reflect productivity growth elsewhere in the economy rather than inefficiency within those services.
(c) Technology will eventually raise productivity in music, teaching and nursing just as it did in manufacturing.
(d) Wages in the stagnant sectors should be restrained in order to prevent inflation in essential services.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that a government which succeeded in freezing teachers' salaries while manufacturing wages continued to rise would most likely:

(a) permanently eliminate the cost pressure the passage describes, since wages are the channel through which it operates.
(b) find it increasingly difficult to retain teachers, because the passage's mechanism runs through competition for labour between sectors.
(c) raise productivity in teaching, since a tighter budget forces more efficient use of resources.
(d) show that Baumol's argument holds only for privately provided services.

**Q3.** The claim that costs in the stagnant sectors "must go on rising indefinitely" rests most directly on the assumption that:

(a) governments will continue to finance health and education largely from public revenue.
(b) productivity in those sectors will not be substantially raised by future technology.
(c) workers value income more highly than the intrinsic rewards of teaching or nursing.
(d) productivity growth in manufacturing will slow down in the coming decades.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> Two drivers leave the same party equally drunk and drive home by the same route at the same speed. One arrives without incident and sleeps it off. The other, through no difference in judgement, skill or intention, rounds a corner at the moment a child steps into the road, and kills her. In law and in ordinary moral life we treat these two very differently: the first will be reproached, if at all, briefly; the second may be imprisoned, and will certainly be altered for the rest of his life. Yet the only difference between them lies in something entirely outside the control of either.
>
> This is uncomfortable, because it collides with a principle most of us hold firmly: that people are properly praised or blamed only for what is up to them. If the child's presence on the road was up to neither driver, then the moral distance between them ought to be nothing at all. Reflection seems to demand that we assess both men by the quality of their decision to drive, and that we then find them identical.
>
> But this is a conclusion almost nobody can actually inhabit. The second driver's remorse is not a confusion to be argued away; if he shrugged and observed that he had been no more reckless than his companion, we would find something monstrous in him, and we would be right to. The agent who has done harm stands in a relation to that harm which no accounting of intentions can dissolve.
>
> What follows is not that the control principle is false, but that our moral thinking is not a single system. It contains a demand for fairness that looks only at the will, and a response to what has actually happened that refuses to look away from the world. Neither can be surrendered, and the two do not reconcile.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(a) Legal systems ought to punish the two drivers identically, since their intentions were identical.
(b) The second driver's remorse is irrational and should be dispelled by philosophical argument.
(c) Moral thought contains two commitments — judging by the will, and responding to what was actually done — neither of which can be reduced to the other.
(d) Because luck governs so much of human life, blame is never really justified.

**Q5.** It can be inferred that the author regards the second driver's remorse as:

(a) a psychological reaction carrying no moral significance.
(b) an appropriate response that a purely will-based account of blame cannot accommodate.
(c) evidence that he was, after all, more reckless than the first driver.
(d) a form of self-punishment that a humane society should discourage.

**Q6.** The author's attitude towards the control principle is best described as:

(a) dismissive — the principle is exposed as an illusion.
(b) wholly endorsing — the principle settles the case.
(c) respectful but qualified — the principle is retained, yet denied sole authority.
(d) indifferent — the author declines to take any position on it.

## Answers & Worked Methods

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (b).** *Main idea.* The passage's whole architecture — the quartet, the wage-competition mechanism, the two rival conclusions — drives to the second conclusion: these services grow costly "precisely because the rest of the economy has become rich". (a) is the diagnosis the passage explicitly rejects ("waste that will not be found"); (c) contradicts the premise of stagnant productivity; (d) is a policy the passage never recommends.

**Q2 — (b).** *Inference.* The stated mechanism is labour mobility: the stagnant sectors "must eventually match those wages **or lose their people to other work**." Freezing pay simply selects the second branch. (a) is the trap — it suppresses the *measured* cost while triggering the very attrition the passage warns of.

**Q3 — (b).** *Assumption.* Negation test: if technology were to raise productivity in nursing, teaching or performance, the sectors would cease to be stagnant and the "indefinite" rise would stop. That is the load-bearing premise. (c) is close but weaker — the passage needs no claim about workers' *preferences*, only about the market for their labour; (a) and (d) are irrelevant to the cost logic.

**Q4 — (c).** *Main idea.* The final paragraph is the thesis: "our moral thinking is not a single system… Neither can be surrendered, and the two do not reconcile." (a) is the position reflection *seems* to demand but which the author says nobody can inhabit; (b) is explicitly denied ("not a confusion to be argued away"); (d) is never claimed.

**Q5 — (b).** *Inference.* The author calls the shrug "monstrous" and says we would be "right" to see it so — the remorse is fitting; yet it cannot be justified by intentions, which were identical in both drivers. Hence a will-only account has no room for it.

**Q6 — (c).** *Tone / attitude.* "What follows is **not** that the control principle is false" — the principle survives; but it is one of two irreducible demands, not the whole of morality. That is respect with a limit, not dismissal (a), endorsement (b), or indifference (d).

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (a) A.** Facing **outward** reverses the usual convention: each person's **right is clockwise**, left is counter-clockwise. Number the seats 1–6 clockwise and put **A = 1**.
- (i) A is third to the *left* of B ⇒ moving 3 seats counter-clockwise from B lands on A ⇒ B = A + 3 = **4**.
- (ii) C is second to the *right* of A ⇒ C = A + 2 = **3**.
- (iii) D neighbours B(4): seats 3 or 5; seat 3 is C, so **D = 5**.
- (iv) Remaining seats 2 and 6 for E and F; C(3)'s neighbours are 2 and 4, and E must avoid them ⇒ **E = 6, F = 2**.
Final (clockwise): 1-A, 2-F, 3-C, 4-B, 5-D, 6-E. Second to the *right* of D = clockwise two from seat 5 → 6 → 1 = **A**.
*Trap:* using the facing-inward convention gives F.

**Q8 — (a) Only I follows.** "Only a few pens are markers" means **some pens are markers** *and* **some pens are not markers**. From "some pens are markers" + "all markers are inks", those pens are inks ⇒ **I follows**. For II, the pens that are not markers may still be inks (nothing rules it out), so "some pens are not inks" is only a possibility ⇒ II does not follow. (c) is the bait: I and II are a complementary pair, but the "either/or" rule applies only when *neither* conclusion follows on its own — here I is definite.

**Q9 — (a) MQJMH.** Shift by position: L(12)+1 = 13 = **M**; O(15)+2 = 17 = **Q**; G(7)+3 = 10 = **J**; I(9)+4 = 13 = **M**; C(3)+5 = 8 = **H** → **MQJMH**. (Verify the example: C+1 = D, A+2 = C, T+3 = W → DCW ✓.) Trap (b) shifts the last letter by 4 instead of 5.

**Q10 — (a) Niece.** A (male) is married to B, so B is female. C is B's brother; D is C's only son and E is C's daughter (D's sister). E is therefore the daughter of A's wife's brother — i.e., A's **niece**. F (B's sister) is a distractor and plays no part in this relation.

**Q11 — (a) 10 km, due south.** Take O = (0, 0), north = +y, east = +x.
Ravi: N 5 → (0, 5); turns *right* (north → east) 12 → **(12, 5)**.
Meera: S 9 → (0, −9); turns *left* (south → east) 12 → (12, −9); turns *left* again (east → north) 4 → **(12, −5)**.
Same x-coordinate, so the distance is 5 − (−5) = **10 km**, and Meera is **due south** of Ravi. *Trap:* (c) 14 km comes from adding 5 + 9 and forgetting Meera's final 4 km north.

**Q12 — (c) 36.** The rule is ×2 then add a running count: 3×2+1 = 7; 7×2+2 = 16; 16×2+3 = **35**, not 36 — so **36 is the wrong term**. Everything after it is consistent with the correct value: 35×2+4 = 74 ✓; 74×2+5 = 153 ✓; 153×2+6 = 312 ✓. (Equivalent check: the differences of the true series are 4, 9, 19, 39, 79, 159 — each roughly doubling.)

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (a) B.** NPA amount = loans × rate:
A = 1,400 × 4.0% = **56**; B = 900 × 6.5% = **58.5**; C = 1,600 × 3.5% = **56**; D = 700 × 8.0% = **56**; E = 1,100 × 5.0% = **55**. Largest = **B (₹58.5 crore)**.
*Traps:* C has the largest loan book and D the highest NPA *rate*, but neither has the largest NPA amount — three branches tie at exactly 56, so the arithmetic has to be done.

**Q14 — (a) 4.9%.** Combined NPA = 56 + 58.5 + 56 + 56 + 55 = **281.5**; combined loans = 1,400 + 900 + 1,600 + 700 + 1,100 = **5,700**. 281.5 / 5,700 = 0.0494 ≈ **4.9%**.
*Trap (b) 5.4%* is the plain average of the five rates, (4.0 + 6.5 + 3.5 + 8.0 + 5.0)/5 = 5.4 — invalid, because the branches have very different loan bases and the two largest books carry the two lowest rates.

**Q15 — (a) 4,320.** Votes cast = 75% × 60,000 = **45,000**. Invalid = 4% × 45,000 = 1,800 ⇒ valid = **43,200**. X = 45% × 43,200 = **19,440**; Y = 35% × 43,200 = **15,120**. Margin = 19,440 − 15,120 = **4,320**. (Shortcut: the 10-percentage-point gap × 43,200 = 4,320.)
*Trap (b) 4,500* applies the 10-point gap to the votes *cast* (45,000) instead of the *valid* votes.

**Q16 — (b) 32.4%.** X polled 19,440 out of 60,000 registered voters ⇒ 19,440 / 60,000 = 0.324 = **32.4%**. (Chain check: 0.75 × 0.96 × 0.45 = 0.324 ✓.)
*Trap (c) 33.75%* forgets the 4% invalid votes (0.75 × 0.45); *trap (a) 45%* is X's share of valid votes, a different base.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (c).** During an election the DEO answers to the Election Commission, and the correct instinct is documentation plus verification rather than either accommodation or informal defiance. (c) rejects an unsupported request, creates a written record, routes it up the constitutionally correct channel, and still leaves room for evidence to change the decision. (a) yields to pressure and risks disenfranchising voters; (b) is right in substance but leaves no record and no reporting — fatal if the pressure escalates; (d) abdicates a statutory function to the very government seeking the change.

**Q18 — (c).** Two distinct issues must be separated: a genuine procedural breach (unauthorised disclosure) and a genuine substantive fact (the scheme is late). (c) handles the first by due process rather than punitive haste, and the second by fixing the underlying problem instead of the messenger. (a) pre-judges and punishes before inquiry; (b) waives a rule a supervisor has no authority to waive; (d) adds a false public statement to a real delay — the worst option.

**Q19 — (d).** Rule-abiding and constructive: the deviation goes on record, the ex-post-facto route provided by the rules is used rather than invented, the process is corrected prospectively, and the senior is informed transparently rather than ambushed. (a) makes you complicit in an irregularity you have now noticed; (b) bypasses every legitimate channel; (c) is the most damaging — it uses your own signature to erase evidence of someone else's lapse.

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (a) 105.** Alligation on the averages: boys 68, girls 82, mean 76.
Boys : girls = |82 − 76| : |76 − 68| = 6 : 8 = **3 : 4**.
Girls exceed boys by 1 part = 15 students ⇒ 1 part = 15 ⇒ boys = 45, girls = 60, total = **105**.
*Check:* 45 × 68 = 3,060; 60 × 82 = 4,920; total 7,980; 7,980 / 105 = **76** ✓.
*Trap (d) 75* comes from reading the ratio the wrong way round (4 : 3, giving boys = 60, girls = 45 — inconsistent with "15 more girls").

**Q21 — (b) 8.** Find the cycle mod 13: 5¹ = 5, 5² = 25 ≡ 12, 5³ = 60 ≡ **8**, 5⁴ = 40 ≡ **1** (mod 13). So the order is 4. Now 99 = 4 × 24 + 3, hence 5⁹⁹ ≡ 5³ ≡ **8** (mod 13).
*Direct check:* 5⁴ = 625 = 13 × 48 + 1 ✓ and 5³ = 125 = 13 × 9 + 8 ✓.

**Q22 — (a) 84.** Let the man's rate be *m* steps per second, the escalator's *e* steps per second, and let *N* be the total number of steps. Put k = e/m.
- Walking **down** (escalator helps): he contributes 60 steps and the escalator contributes 60k in the same time ⇒ N = 60(1 + k).
- Walking **up** (escalator opposes): he contributes 140 steps while the escalator takes back 140k ⇒ N = 140(1 − k).
Equate: 60 + 60k = 140 − 140k ⇒ 200k = 80 ⇒ **k = 0.4**.
N = 60 × 1.4 = **84** (cross-check: 140 × 0.6 = 84 ✓).
*Trap (b) 100* is the plain average of 60 and 140; the correct combination is the harmonic-style weighting above — note that 84 is exactly the harmonic mean 2 × 60 × 140 / (60 + 140).

## Timing & strategy tip

**Bank the DI before you bank the quant.** In this set, Q13–Q16 are four marks available on nothing harder than percentage arithmetic — but each carries a deliberately attractive wrong answer (a simple average of rates, the wrong denominator, the wrong base). The discipline that pays: before you pick an option in DI, say out loud what the denominator is. In Q14 the denominator is *total loans*, not *five branches*; in Q16 it is *registered voters*, not *valid votes*. Roughly a third of CSAT DI errors are base errors, not arithmetic errors, and they cost you a mark you had already earned. Give DI 7 minutes, and if a quant item like Q22 hasn't yielded a clean equation in 90 seconds, mark it and move on — you are chasing 66, not 200.
