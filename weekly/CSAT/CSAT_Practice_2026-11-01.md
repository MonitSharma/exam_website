### Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

**Q1.** Read the passage and answer the question that follows.

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. This aphorism, usually attributed to the economist Charles Goodhart, captures a difficulty that public administrators meet repeatedly. An indicator is valuable because it correlates with something we care about but cannot observe directly: the number of patients treated within four hours stands in for the quality of emergency care, examination pass rates for learning, arrests for public safety. The correlation, however, holds only so long as nobody has a reason to manipulate the indicator. Once rewards or penalties are attached to it, those who are measured will optimise the number rather than the underlying reality, and the statistical relationship that made the number informative begins to dissolve. Hospitals reclassify waiting areas as treatment bays; schools coach for the test and discourage weak candidates from sitting it; police records quietly downgrade offences.

The standard remedy is to multiply indicators, but this is a partial answer at best. A larger dashboard raises the cost of gaming each item, yet it also invites a different distortion: effort drifts towards whatever is most easily counted, and the unmeasured dimensions of the work, which are often the most valuable, are starved. Nor is the solution to abandon measurement; judgement unaided by data is hostage to anecdote and favouritism. A more modest lesson is that indicators serve best as prompts for inquiry rather than as verdicts. A sudden improvement in a figure should trigger scrutiny of how it was achieved, and those who are measured should be involved in choosing indicators, since they know best where the figure and the reality are likely to part company. Used in this spirit, a metric remains a flashlight; used as a leash, it becomes an incentive to hide what the light would reveal.

Q. Which one of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Public indicators are inherently unreliable and administrators should replace them with expert judgement.
- (b) Adding more indicators eliminates gaming and is the surest way to measure public services.
- (c) Indicators lose their reliability once rewards or penalties are tied to them, so they are best used as prompts for inquiry rather than final verdicts.
- (d) Those who are measured should design the indicators because administrators lack the data skills to do so.

**Q4.** Read the passage and answer the question that follows.

Nostalgia is commonly dismissed as a failure of memory: the nostalgic person, it is said, recalls a past that never existed, scrubbed of its poverty, disease and petty tyrannies. There is truth in the charge, but it mistakes the object of the emotion. Rarely does anyone yearn for the past as a complete set of conditions; what is mourned is some particular good that the past happened to carry, such as an unhurried afternoon or a neighbourhood in which strangers nodded to one another, and that the present has failed to preserve. To say that the past was, on balance, worse is therefore not to refute the nostalgic; it merely changes the subject. A person can coherently believe both that the world has improved and that something of value has been lost in the improvement.

The real danger of nostalgia lies elsewhere. Because it isolates the lost good from the costs that once accompanied it, nostalgia invites the thought that the good can be recovered at no price, and it is on this thought that political movements of restoration feed. The remedy is not to forbid the feeling, which would be both futile and impoverishing, but to complete the accounting: to ask who paid for the unhurried afternoon, and whether the nodding neighbourhood was open to everyone. Such questions do not extinguish the longing; they convert it from a demand for return into a guide for what the future should try to contain. Seen in this way, nostalgia is less a verdict on history than an inventory of present deficiencies, drawn up in the only language available to those who feel them: the language of the lost.

Q. Which one of the following best expresses the central idea of the passage?

- (b) Because the past carried real goods, societies should work to restore as much of it as possible.
- (c) Political movements exploit nostalgia, so the feeling should be forbidden in public life.
- (d) Nostalgia usually points to a genuine present deficiency; it is best handled by counting the full costs of the lost good, not by suppressing it.

## Answers and Worked Solutions

**Q1 — (c)** · *Main idea - Goodhart's law and public indicators* · Moderate  
(c) combines both halves: the problem (targets corrupt measures) and the author's modest remedy (indicators as prompts). (a) is rejected explicitly ('Nor is the solution to abandon measurement'); (b) is called a 'partial answer at best'; (d) overstates a minor point.

**Q2 — (a)** · *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* · Moderate  
The passage says a sudden improvement 'should trigger scrutiny of how it was achieved'. (b) rewards a possibly gamed figure; (c) abandons measurement, which the author rejects; (d) is the 'partial answer' remedy.

**Q3 — (b)** · *Assumption - limited effort and the unmeasured* · Hard  
Negation test: if effort were unlimited, workers could meet every counted target and still serve the uncounted dimensions, so 'starving' would not follow. (a), (c), (d) are neither needed nor stated; the passage speaks of 'optimising' behaviour, not dishonesty.

**Q4 — (d)** · *Main idea - nostalgia and the lost good* · Moderate  
The passage rejects the 'mere distortion' view, warns against the restoration politics, rejects forbidding the feeling, and ends by treating nostalgia as an 'inventory of present deficiencies' to be sharpened by complete accounting. Only (d) holds all this.

**Q5 — (b)** · *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* · Moderate  
Directly from paragraph 1: showing the past was worse overall is not a refutation of mourning one lost good. (a) and (c) both take sides on the 'overall' question the author calls irrelevant; (d) contradicts the author's recommendation to 'complete the accounting'.

**Q6 — (a)** · *Tone and attitude - qualified sympathy* · Moderate  
The author defends the emotion's coherence and refuses to forbid it, yet warns of the 'real danger' of restoration politics: qualified sympathy.

**Q7 — (d)** · *Scheduling puzzle - five officers, five days* · Moderate  
Order: P < R < Q, and S,T consecutive (S then T). Q is not Friday. P and S not adjacent. Try blocks: the chain P-R-Q needs three days and ST needs two consecutive. Options for the pair ST: (Mon,Tue) leaves Wed-Fri for P,R,Q -> Q on Fri, rejected; (Tue,Wed) leaves Mon,Thu,Fri -> Q Fri, rejected; (Wed,Thu) leaves Mon,Tue,Fri -> Q Fri, rejected; (Thu,Fri) leaves Mon,Tue,Wed -> P Mon, R Tue, Q Wed, S Thu, T Fri; P(Mon) and S(Thu) are not consecutive. Unique schedule. Thursday = S. (Verified by brute force over all 120 permutations.)

**Q8 — (c)** · *Calendar - day of the week* · Moderate  
Days from 26 Jan to 15 Aug: 5 (rest of Jan) + 28 (Feb) + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 (Mar-Jul) + 15 = 201. 201 mod 7 = 5. Tuesday + 5 days = Sunday. (2027 is not a leap year.)

**Q9 — (a)** · *Coding-decoding - common word deduction* · Moderate  
'green' is common to statements 1 and 2 = ve; 'tea' is common to 2 and 3 = mi; 'leaves' is common to 1 and 3 = ro. Then 'fresh' = ta and 'fall' = ku. 'fresh tea leaves' = ta mi ro (in some order). (b) has ve=green, (c) has ku=fall, (d) has green+fall.

**Q10 — (a)** · *Critical reasoning - weakening a causal claim* · Moderate  
The conclusion is causal. (a) supplies an alternative explanation (regression to the mean after an abnormal spike, visible even where there were no cameras), so the fall may have occurred without CCTV. (b) is a minor exception to an average; (c) and (d) are irrelevant or mildly supportive.

**Q11 — (c)** · *Data sufficiency - divisibility* · Moderate  
I alone: 4, 8 are divisible by 4 but not by 12. II alone: 6 is divisible by 6 but not by 12. Together N is a multiple of both 4 and 6, i.e. of LCM(4,6) = 12. So both together suffice. (Trap: multiplying 4 x 6 = 24 is unnecessary; the LCM is what matters.)

**Q12 — (d)** · *Selection puzzle - committees under conditions* · Hard  
Case 1, E is selected: A must be in (iii); D is out (iv); B is out (i); C is out (ii needs D). Only F can be the third member, giving {A,E,F} - 1 committee. Case 2, E is not selected: choose 3 from A,B,C,D,F with no A+B pair and C only with D. Valid: ACD, ADF, BCD, BDF, CDF = 5 (ACF, BCF fail rule ii; any triple with A and B fails rule i). Total = 1 + 5 = 6. (Verified by enumerating all 20 triples.)

**Q13 — (a)** · *Table - percentage-point change vs absolute change (trap)* · Moderate  
Coverage 2020 -> 2025: Alpha 25% -> 50% (+25 pp); Beta 40% -> 50% (+10); Gamma 10% -> 40% (+30); Delta 50% -> 60% (+10). Largest = Gamma. Traps: Alpha and Delta add the most connections in absolute terms (+3.0 lakh each); Delta has the highest 2025 coverage.

**Q14 — (c)** · *Table - weighted vs simple average (trap)* · Hard  
2020: tap = 2+4+0.5+6 = 12.5; households = 8+10+5+12 = 35; coverage 35.7%. 2025: tap = 5+6+2.4+9 = 22.4; households = 10+12+6+15 = 43; coverage 52.1%. Rise = 52.1 - 35.7 = 16.4 pp. Trap (b): simple average of the four district rises = (25+10+30+10)/4 = 18.75, which ignores district sizes.

**Q15 — (b)** · *Table - ratios and percentages (compound), simple-average trap* · Moderate  
Tech staff: A 480, B 450, C 300, D 360 (total 1590). Women in tech: A 25% of 480 = 120; B 40% of 450 = 180; C 50% of 300 = 150; D 35% of 360 = 126. Total = 576. Trap (c): applying the simple average of the four percentages (37.5%) to 1590 gives about 596.

**Q16 — (d)** · *Table - proportion of subgroup (trap: count vs proportion)* · Hard  
Women employees: A 450, B 500, C 600, D 300. Women in tech: 120, 180, 150, 126. Proportions: A 26.7%, B 36.0%, C 25.0%, D 42.0%. Highest = D. Trap: B has the largest number of women in tech (180) but not the highest proportion.

**Q17 — (c)** · *Equity in relief distribution - resisting influence* · Moderate  
Best answer balances rule-following (objective criteria), equity, transparency and initiative (more stock). (a) is favouritism; (b) harms victims; (d) disadvantages the weakest who cannot reach the office.

**Q18 — (a)** · *Integrity and accountability - correcting a published error* · Moderate  
Public trust in official statistics depends on prompt, transparent correction. (b), (c) and (d) conceal a known error and discourage honest reporting by juniors.

**Q19 — (b)** · *Law vs custom - consultation and proportionate enforcement* · Moderate  
The balanced answer respects both the law and community sentiment: advance dialogue, an agreed compromise, then even-handed enforcement. (a) and (d) are heavy-handed; (c) abandons the rule and the students' interest.

**Q20 — (d)** · *Arithmetic progression with exclusion* · Hard  
Three-digit multiples of 7: 105, 112, ..., 994; count = (994-105)/7 + 1 = 128; sum = 128 x (105+994)/2 = 70336. Multiples of 35: 105, 140, ..., 980; count = 26; sum = 26 x (105+980)/2 = 14105. Required = 70336 - 14105 = 56231.

**Q21 — (a)** · *Profit and loss - successive discounts with false weight* · Hard  
Let cost per kg = 100. Marked price per kg = 140; after two 10% discounts = 140 x 0.9 x 0.9 = 113.4 per kg. The customer pays 113.4 but receives 900 g, which costs the seller 90. Profit = (113.4 - 90)/90 = 26%. (Trap: 13.4% ignores the false weight.)

**Q22 — (c)** · *Combinatorics - bounded integer solutions (inclusion-exclusion)* · Hard  
Without the bound: C(19,2) = 171. Subtract cases where a given variable is at least 11: put x = 10 + x' (x' at least 1), so x' + y + z = 10 has C(9,2) = 36 solutions; for 3 variables, 3 x 36 = 108. Two variables both at least 11 is impossible (22 > 20). Answer = 171 - 108 = 63. (Verified by brute force.)
