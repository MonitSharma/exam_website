## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage — Q1–Q3

> Economists once assumed that competition among technologies would, over time, select the best one. The notion of increasing returns complicates this comfortable picture. When a technology becomes more valuable the more widely it is adopted — because users learn it, complementary goods are built around it, or others expect it to prevail — small and essentially accidental early advantages can snowball. A rival that is superior on every engineering measure may then never find the foothold it needs: no producer will build for it until users exist, and no user will adopt it until producers do. The outcome is not necessarily the best one; it is merely the one that history happened to favour, and once reached it is difficult to leave, even if everyone would, in principle, prefer to move together.
> 
> It would be a mistake, however, to read this as a licence for gloom or for confident intervention. Lock-in is demonstrable in some cases but is often asserted rather than shown; the standard anecdotes are contested, and a technology that is widely used may be widely used because it is good. Moreover, the remedy is not obvious. A government that attempts to rescue the superior alternative must know which one is superior — precisely the knowledge that, under uncertainty, nobody possesses at the moment the choice is made. The more defensible lesson is procedural: the early stage of a technology's life is when choices are cheap to reverse and expensive to ignore, so that is when standards and public procurement deserve their greatest scrutiny, and when preserving several options is worth more than it appears.

### Passage — Q4–Q6

> It is a commonplace that translation is impossible, and a commonplace that it is done every day; the interesting question is why both are said with a straight face. The first claim treats a text as a sealed vessel whose contents must be poured intact into another; since no two languages divide the world identically, something always spills. The second claim treats the text as a message, and a message survives any change of container. Both pictures flatter the original by imagining it as a thing with a fixed content apart from the words in which it happens to be written. But a great poem or a legal clause does not have its meaning first and its wording afterwards; the wording is where the meaning happens. What the translator therefore transmits is not a content but a reading — an interpretation answerable to the original, but never identical with it.
> 
> This does not make fidelity meaningless. A translation can be tested against the original as a performance is tested against a score: a pianist who changes the notes is not offering an interpretation, he is changing the subject. Yet the score does not dictate a single rendition, and the disagreement among competent performers is not a symptom of failure but evidence that the work is rich enough to sustain more than one. The translator who hopes to settle the matter once and for all, producing the definitive version, misunderstands both the original and her own task. Each generation will need to translate again, not because earlier translators erred, but because the language into which they wrote has moved on and the original, being alive in a way that the translations are not, can be asked new questions.

# Answers & Worked Methods

**Q1 — (b)** · *Main idea - increasing returns and technological lock-in* · Moderate  
(b) covers both halves: the mechanism of lock-in and the cautious, procedural conclusion. (a) is the view the passage sets out to complicate. (c) is the 'confident intervention' the author warns against. (d) overstates: the author says a widely used technology may be so because it is good.

**Q2 — (c)** · *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* · Moderate  
The author says the early stage is when choices are cheap to reverse and options are worth preserving; hence (c). (a) assumes superiority is knowable. (b) ignores lock-in. (d) acts late, when switching is costly.

**Q3 — (a)** · *Assumption - chicken-and-egg adoption* · Hard  
Negation test: if producers and users did not wait for each other, the rival could start without the foothold and the 'never' collapses. (b), (c) and (d) are not needed for the stated mechanism.

**Q4 — (d)** · *Main idea - translation as interpretation* · Moderate  
The author rejects both the 'impossible' and the 'message survives' pictures and replaces them with the idea of a reading answerable to the original. (a) and (b) are the two commonplaces criticised; (c) contradicts the passage's insistence that fidelity can be tested.

**Q5 — (c)** · *Inference - author's likely judgement on cases* · Moderate  
The author says the translator seeking 'the definitive version' misunderstands her task. (a), (b) and (d) are all endorsed or implied: retranslation by each generation, testing against the original, and legitimate plurality.

**Q6 — (a)** · *Assumption - argument from the performance analogy* · Hard  
Negation test: if the differences arose because some performers ignored the score, disagreement would show carelessness, not richness. (b) contradicts the author; (c) and (d) are irrelevant.

**Q7 — (d)** · *Circular seating arrangement (8 persons, facing centre)* · Hard  
Fix A at position 0 and number positions clockwise (for a person facing the centre, 'left' is clockwise, 'right' is anticlockwise). C = 0−3 = 5. E opposite A = 4. B immediately right of E = 3. D second left of C = 5+2 = 7. Empty seats: 1, 2, 6 for F, G, H. F is not next to A (seats 1 and 7), so F = 2 or 6. G immediately right of F: F=2 gives G=1 (free); F=6 gives G=5 (taken, C). So F=2, G=1, H=6. Order clockwise: A, G, F, B, E, C, H, D. Second to the left of F (two seats clockwise from seat 2) = seat 4 = E.

**Q8 — (b)** · *Syllogism - chain with particular premise* · Moderate  
Pilots∩Doctors ≠ ∅, Doctors ⊂ Writers, Writers ∩ Singers = ∅. Those pilots who are doctors are writers, hence not singers, so I follows; and they are writers, so IV follows by conversion. II contradicts the third premise. III fails: only some pilots are known to be doctors, so nothing is said about the other pilots (trap). Answer: I and IV.

**Q9 — (c)** · *Blood relations - 'only daughter' trap* · Moderate  
Rekha's father's mother's only daughter = Rekha's father's sister, i.e. Rekha's paternal aunt (not her mother, which is the common slip). Her son is Rekha's paternal cousin. Answer: cousin.

**Q10 — (b)** · *Truth-teller / liar puzzle* · Moderate  
Suppose X is truthful: then Y lies, so Z is truthful (Y's claim 'Z is a liar' is false); but then Z's statement 'X is a liar' must be true — contradiction. So X lies, hence Y is truthful, so Z is a liar. Check Z: 'X and Y both liars' is false (Y is truthful) — consistent with Z lying. Only Y is truthful.

**Q11 — (a)** · *Coding-decoding - alternating shifts with wrap-around* · Moderate  
SILENT→TGMCOR: letters at odd positions shift +1 (S→T, L→M, N→O), letters at even positions shift −2 (I→G, E→C, T→R). DREAMS: D+1=E, R−2=P, E+1=F, A−2=Y (wraps round the alphabet: A→Z→Y), M+1=N, S−2=Q → EPFYNQ. Option (b) (EPFZNQ) forgets the wrap-around.

**Q12 — (d)** · *Direction sense - successive turns* · Moderate  
Take start (0,0). North 10 → (0,10). Right (east) 7 → (7,10). Right (south) 4 → (7,6). Left (facing south, left is east) 5 → (12,6). Right (facing east, right is south) 6 → (12,0). Net displacement: 12 km due East.

**Q13 — (b)** · *Table with percentage shares - growth vs share trap* · Moderate  
Convert shares to amounts. 2024: Edu 10,000; Health 4,800; Roads 7,200; Agri 6,000; Others 12,000. 2025: Edu 11,000; Health 7,000; Roads 10,000; Agri 6,000; Others 16,000. Growth: Edu 10%; Health 7,000/4,800 = +45.8%; Roads +38.9%; Agri 0%; Others +33.3%. Health is highest. Trap: Roads, Health and Others all gained 2 percentage points of share, which hides the very different growth rates.

**Q14 — (c)** · *Table with percentage shares - share-point vs percentage-change trap* · Moderate  
Education + Health: 14,800 (2024) → 18,000 (2025), growth = 3,200/14,800 = 21.6% > 20%, so the statement is correct. Agriculture stayed at ₹6,000 crore (a share fall of 3 points is 20% of its share, but the budget itself grew 25%) — a classic trap. Roads (10,000) < Education (11,000). Others: 12,000 → 16,000 = +33.3%.

**Q15 — (a)** · *Weighted pass percentages - simple average trap* · Moderate  
Girls: IX 80, X 150, XI 150 (total 380). Passed: 0.9×80 = 72; 0.8×150 = 120; 0.8×150 = 120 → 312. 312/380 = 82.1%. The simple average of 90, 80, 80 is 83.3% (trap) because the class sizes differ.

**Q16 — (d)** · *Conditional share - boys among those who failed* · Hard  
Boys: IX 120, X 150, XI 100 (total 370). Boys passed: 96 + 105 + 60 = 261 → boys failed 109. Total students 750; girls passed 312 + boys passed 261 = 573, so failed = 177. Boys among failed = 109/177 = 61.6%. Traps: 370/750 = 49.3% (boys' share of students); 261/370 = 70.5% (boys' pass rate).

**Q17 — (b)** · *Integrity - reporting safety violation* · Moderate  
Balanced, rule-abiding option: verify first, then use the official channel, protecting the informant. (a) colludes in concealment; (c) bypasses the due process and prejudges; (d) abdicates responsibility where public safety is at stake.

**Q18 — (c)** · *Law and order - rumour management* · Moderate  
A proportionate response: fact-check, prevent violence, involve community leaders, counter the rumour with credible information, and use extreme measures only within legal limits. (a) is passive; (b) is disproportionate and unconsulted; (d) is partisan and inflammatory.

**Q19 — (a)** · *Child labour at a government worksite - proportionality* · Moderate  
The law is non-negotiable, but the response is humane (rehabilitation, schooling) and keeps the public project moving. (b) is disproportionate and harms the school's beneficiaries; (c) compromises integrity; (d) tolerates an illegal practice.

**Q20 — (d)** · *Number theory - remainder using Euler's theorem* · Hard  
gcd(3,28)=1 and φ(28)=12, so 3^12 ≡ 1 (mod 28). 2026 = 12×168 + 10, so 3^2026 ≡ 3^10. Since 3^3 = 27 ≡ −1, 3^9 ≡ −1 and 3^10 ≡ −3 ≡ 25 (mod 28).

**Q21 — (c)** · *Boats and streams - simultaneous equations* · Hard  
Let upstream speed u and downstream speed d. 30/u + 44/d = 10 and 40/u + 55/d = 13. Put x = 1/u, y = 1/d: 30x + 44y = 10; 40x + 55y = 13. Multiply the first by 4 and the second by 3: 120x + 176y = 40; 120x + 165y = 39 → 11y = 1, y = 1/11, d = 11. Then 30x = 10 − 4 = 6, x = 1/5, u = 5. Still-water speed = (11 + 5)/2 = 8 km/h (stream = 3 km/h).

**Q22 — (a)** · *Probability - Bayes' theorem (light)* · Hard  
P(A)=P(B)=1/2. P(red|A)=3/5, P(red|B)=2/6=1/3. P(A|red) = (1/2·3/5)/(1/2·3/5 + 1/2·1/3) = (3/10)/(3/10 + 1/6) = (9/30)/(14/30) = 9/14. The trap is 3/5, which ignores that Box B is also a source of red balls.
