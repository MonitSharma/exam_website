## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (economic / analytical)

> Employers routinely pay more for job candidates holding a university degree than for those without one, even when the degree's subject bears no relation to the job's actual duties. The conventional explanation treats education as a factory that manufactures skill: years in a classroom build competence, and wages simply reward that competence. But a rival account treats the degree not as proof of what was learned but as evidence of what the holder was already capable of enduring. On this view, a demanding and lengthy course of study functions less as a training ground than as a filter: it is costly enough that only the more able, disciplined, or persistent candidates find it worth completing, and it is this pre-existing quality, not any skill imparted by the coursework, that employers are actually paying for.
>
> The two accounts are not easy to tell apart from the outside, since both predict the same correlation between credentials and wages. But they diverge sharply in their implications. If education is a factory, its value lies in the material taught, and shortening or cheapening the process would erode the very competence employers seek. If education is a filter, its value lies in the cost of passing through it: the arduousness must remain high enough to separate the able from the merely willing, whatever the syllabus actually contains. A short, easy, universally-awarded credential would signal nothing at all, however accurately it reflected some minimal literacy.
>
> This second account unsettles the usual case for expanding access to credentials. If a qualification's worth to the labour market rests substantially on its scarcity and difficulty, then making it easier to obtain, however admirable on grounds of fairness, may erode the very information employers rely on to distinguish candidates, forcing them to search for some costlier signal in its place. The problem, then, is not that credentials fail to inform employers, but that the information they carry may have little to do with the classroom at all.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Education always increases a worker's productive skill.
- (b) Employers pay more for degree-holders because degrees may signal pre-existing ability rather than impart it.
- (c) Universities should be shut down because they teach nothing of value.
- (d) Wages are determined solely by the difficulty of a course of study.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that, on the "filter" account, a credential made easier and cheaper to obtain would most likely:

- (a) necessarily raise the average productivity of workers who hold it.
- (b) lose its power to distinguish candidates, pushing employers to seek some costlier signal instead.
- (c) cause employers to stop caring about candidates' ability altogether.
- (d) raise wages for all degree-holders further.

**Q3.** The claim that "a short, easy, universally-awarded credential would signal nothing at all" rests most directly on which assumption?

- (a) A signal's value depends on its being costly enough to filter out those unwilling or unable to bear that cost.
- (b) All credentials are equally difficult to obtain.
- (c) Employers can never observe a worker's actual output.
- (d) Skill and ability are identical concepts.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> We are accustomed to defending privacy as a shield: a boundary that keeps unwanted eyes from matters we would rather keep to ourselves, useful chiefly to those with something to hide. On this account, the person with nothing embarrassing in their life loses little by being transparent, and privacy is simply a convenience that shrinks as our conduct grows more blameless. Yet this treats privacy as though its only function were concealment, and misses a subtler role it plays in the very formation of a self.
>
> To be watched is not merely to be seen; it is to perform. The presence of an observer, even an imagined one, alters what a person does, tempering experiment with the awareness that missteps will be recorded and judged. A self that is never unobserved has little room to try on a belief before endorsing it, to rehearse a version of itself that might be abandoned without cost, or to fail privately before succeeding publicly. Privacy, on this reading, is not the opposite of a full life but one of its preconditions: the unwatched interval in which a person drafts themselves before committing to a final version for others to see.
>
> This suggests that the harm of pervasive surveillance is not exhausted by the risk that some secret will be exposed. Even a population that is never actually punished for what is observed may still be subtly altered by the mere fact of being watched, converging toward whatever is safest to display and abandoning the tentative, the experimental, the not-yet-decided. A society that dissolves privacy in the name of having nothing to hide may thereby lose something it did not know it possessed: the private draft from which a considered self is written, long before it is fit to be shown.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

- (a) Privacy matters only to people who have something to hide.
- (b) Being watched, even without punishment, can narrow a person's room to experiment and form a self, so privacy is a precondition of that formation, not merely concealment.
- (c) Surveillance should be banned because it always leads to punishment.
- (d) A fully transparent society is always a freer society.

**Q5.** On the author's view, it can be inferred that a population watched constantly but never punished:

- (a) suffers no meaningful loss, since only punishment matters.
- (b) may still be altered, converging toward safe, "undrafted" behaviour and losing room to experiment privately.
- (c) becomes more creative because scrutiny sharpens judgement.
- (d) experiences privacy as irrelevant once punishment is absent.

**Q6.** The author's tone through the passage is best described as:

- (a) dismissive of privacy as a sentimental indulgence.
- (b) reflective and cautionary, defending privacy on grounds deeper than mere concealment.
- (c) alarmist, predicting imminent societal collapse.
- (d) neutral to the point of expressing no view at all.

## Answers & Explanations

**Q1. (b)** — The passage's central move is contrasting the "factory" (skill-building) account with the "filter" (signaling) account, and arguing employers may be paying for the signal, not the skill.

**Q2. (b)** — The passage states directly that a credential's value under the filter account depends on cost/difficulty; cheapening it "would erode the very information employers rely on," pushing them toward a costlier signal.

**Q3. (a)** — The sentence's logic only holds if a signal must be costly to be informative; that is the unstated premise.

**Q4. (b)** — The passage argues privacy is not just concealment but a precondition for a self to form via unwatched experimentation.

**Q5. (b)** — The passage explicitly says even an unpunished, watched population "may still be subtly altered," converging toward safe display and losing the tentative/experimental.

**Q6. (b)** — The measured, hedging language ("suggests," "may thereby lose") paired with a clear warning about surveillance marks the tone as reflective and cautionary, not alarmist or dismissive.

**Q7. (a)** — Positions (left→right): P is 3rd from left → P=3. Q is left of P with exactly one person between them → Q=1. T is immediately right of P → T=4. R is second to the right of P → R=5. The one remaining seat, position 2, goes to S by elimination. For facing: Q faces North (given). Exactly two face South and S is one; T and R face the same direction, opposite to P. Testing P=South: then T=R=North, so South-facers = {S, P} = exactly 2 ✓ (consistent). Testing P=North: then T=R=South, giving three South-facers {S,T,R}, contradicting "exactly two." So P=South, T=North, R=North. Left end = Q (North), right end = R (North) → option (a).

**Q8. (b)** — K=Doctor/Patna (clue 3). M=Delhi (clue 5). J is not Delhi/Pune (clue 1), and Patna is taken, so J=Kochi. The only city left, Pune, goes to L. The Engineer is from Kochi (clue 2) = J, so J=Engineer. Remaining professions Teacher/Lawyer go to M and L; M is not a Lawyer (clue 6), so M=Teacher and L=Lawyer. L is the Lawyer, from Pune → option (b).

**Q9. (b)** — "Some engineers are teachers" converts validly to "Some teachers are engineers" (a particular affirmative always converts) — so III always follows. I does not follow: the doctors (⊆ engineers) need not overlap with the specific engineers who are teachers. II does not follow: only the teacher-subset of engineers is known to be disjoint from painters; other engineers could still be painters. Hence only III follows.

**Q10. (a)** — The code shifts every letter forward by 2 positions in the alphabet (T→V, E→G, A→C, C→E, H→J, E→G, R→T, all confirmed +2). Applying the same shift to CHILDREN: C→E, H→J, I→K, L→N, D→F, R→T, E→G, N→P, giving EJKNFTGP.

**Q11. (b)** — "The only son of my father" is Reena's brother (her father has exactly one son, and Reena is his sibling). "He is the son of [that brother]" makes the man the brother's son — i.e., Reena's nephew.

**Q12. (a)** — Taking start at (0,0) facing North: walk 8 km North → (0,8). Turn right (North→East), walk 6 km → (6,8). Turn right again (East→South), walk 8 km → (6,0). Turn left (South→East), walk 4 km → (10,0). Straight-line distance from (0,0) to (10,0) = 10 km, due East.

**Q13. (b)** — Course S: (210−150)/150 × 100 = 60/150 × 100 = 40%. [Option (d) 60% is the trap — it uses the raw absolute increase as if it were the percentage.]

**Q14. (c)** — 2025 total = 300+150+400+210+240 = 1300.

**Q15. (b)** — Course Q is the only one whose enrollment fell (180→150). Percentage decline = 30/180 × 100 ≈ 16.7%. [Option (c) is the trap — it uses the raw drop of 30 as if it were the percentage.]

**Q16. (a)** — 2024 total = 240+180+320+150+210 = 1100. Ratio 1100:1300 = 11:13 (dividing both by 100).

**Q17. (b)** — Balances the immediate humanitarian need (children must eat now) with due process on the underlying financial dispute; doesn't pass the buck, grandstand, or leave the resolution to courts/parents alone.

**Q18. (b)** — Addresses the real interpersonal root cause privately and professionally, sets clear expectations, and reserves escalation for continued non-compliance — firm but not punitive or avoidant.

**Q19. (c)** — Restores fairness through a transparent, needs-based system under camp authority rather than either tolerating discrimination, overreacting by expelling a well-meaning NGO outright, or placing the burden of complaint on already-vulnerable families.

**Q20. (b)** — Combined rate for 4 hours: 4×(1/12+1/16) = 4×(4/48+3/48) = 4×7/48 = 28/48 = 7/12 of the tank filled. Remaining = 1 − 7/12 = 5/12. Pipe B alone: time = (5/12) ÷ (1/16) = 5/12 × 16 = 80/12 = 20/3 hours = 6 hours 40 minutes.

**Q21. (c)** — Let initial volume = V. Milk = 5V/8, water = 3V/8. Removing 16 L of mixture removes milk and water in the same 5:3 ratio: milk removed = 16×5/8 = 10, water removed = 16×3/8 = 6. After removal and adding 16 L pure water: milk = 5V/8 − 10, water = 3V/8 − 6 + 16 = 3V/8 + 10. New ratio: (5V/8 − 10)/(3V/8 + 10) = 5/7. Cross-multiplying: 7(5V/8 − 10) = 5(3V/8 + 10) → 35V/8 − 70 = 15V/8 + 50 → 20V/8 = 120 → V = 48. Check: milk=30, water=18; remove 16 L → milk=20, water=12; add 16 L water → water=28; ratio 20:28 = 5:7 ✓.

**Q22. (a)** — Total ways to pick 4 from 10 = C(10,4) = 210. At least 2 women = 2W+2M, 3W+1M, or 4W+0M: C(4,2)×C(6,2) = 6×15 = 90; C(4,3)×C(6,1) = 4×6 = 24; C(4,4)×C(6,0) = 1×1 = 1. Total favourable = 90+24+1 = 115. Probability = 115/210 = 23/42.
