## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (analytical / science)

Science is often described as self-correcting, and in the long run it is. But the long run can be long, and the mechanism of correction depends on the very thing that is most easily distorted: what gets published. Journals prefer findings that are new and striking; a study that finds an effect is more likely to be written up, accepted and cited than one that finds none. The consequence is not that individual researchers lie. It is that the visible literature becomes a filtered sample of everything that has been tried, in which positive results are over-represented relative to their true frequency.

The filter has a mathematical signature. When many small studies of the same question are plotted together, the imprecise ones scatter widely around the true value; if only those that happen to land far from zero are published, the average of the published studies overstates the effect, and the smaller the studies, the larger the overstatement. A reader who tallies the published papers and finds that most report a benefit may be counting nothing more than the survivors of the filter. Meta-analyses, which pool results, inherit the distortion unless they can reach the unpublished work.

The remedies proposed fall into two camps. One urges researchers to behave better: to report null results and to be sceptical of their own surprises. This is admirable, but it asks individuals to bear a cost that the system rewards them for avoiding. The other changes the incentive itself: studies are registered with their methods and analysis plan before the data are collected, and journals commit to publishing them on the strength of the question and the design, whatever the outcome. Under such registration a null result is no longer a failure to find something but a finding, and the filter has little left to act upon. The first approach appeals to virtue; the second makes virtue unnecessary, which is the more dependable foundation for an institution.

**Q1.** Which one of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(c) Meta-analyses should be abandoned because pooling results always magnifies the errors of individual studies.  
(d) Science is not self-correcting, since small studies are inherently less trustworthy than large ones.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

Most of what any of us knows we have not verified. That the earth is some four and a half billion years old, that a certain medicine is safe, that one's own birthday falls on a given date: each of these rests on the word of others. Philosophers who prize independent thought sometimes treat this dependence as an embarrassment, a concession to be minimised by the diligent. But the picture is mistaken in two ways. It is impossible, first, since no life is long enough to re-derive even a small part of what a single textbook contains. And it misdescribes the ideal, since an inquirer who trusted nothing would not be more rational than the rest of us; she would merely know less, and would have no means of correcting herself, for the instruments, the data and the very language in which she framed her doubts are all inherited.

Yet it would be equally hasty to conclude that trust needs no justification. To trust is to hand over to another a judgement one could not make oneself, and the handover can be misplaced. What distinguishes rational reliance from credulity is not that the rational person checks everything, but that she is sensitive to the signs that bear on whether checking is needed: whether the source has a stake in the outcome, whether others who might expose an error have had the chance to do so, whether the claim fits what she has independent reason to believe. Her trust is revocable, and she knows what would revoke it.

Seen thus, epistemic independence is less a starting point than a capacity exercised within dependence. The autonomous thinker is not the one who stands alone, but the one who knows on whom she leans, and why.

**Q4.** Which one of the following best expresses the central idea of the passage?

(b) Dependence on the testimony of others is unavoidable and legitimate; rationality lies in trust that is discriminating and revocable, not in checking everything.  
(c) Textbooks and experts deserve unquestioning trust because no individual can re-derive their contents.  
(d) Independent thought is an illusion and should be abandoned as an ideal.

**Q1. (b)** — *Main idea - publication bias and incentive design* (Moderate)  
The passage traces a filter in publication, its statistical effect, and argues for incentive redesign over appeals to virtue. (a) is rejected explicitly ('individual researchers do not lie'); (c) and (d) overstate.

**Q2. (c)** — *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* (Moderate)  
The author warns that a tally of published positives 'may be counting nothing more than the survivors of the filter'. Fraud is expressly not alleged; (d) reverses the passage's claim.

**Q3. (b)** — *Assumption - behaviour follows incentives* (Moderate)  
'The system rewards them for avoiding' the cost, so the author trusts incentive design over virtue; this presupposes that behaviour tracks incentives. (a) contradicts 'not that researchers lie'.

**Q4. (b)** — *Main idea - testimony and rational trust* (Moderate)  
The author rejects both total self-reliance and blind trust; the ideal is sensitivity to signs that bear on reliability. (c) and (d) overstate.

**Q5. (a)** — *Inference - credulity vs rational reliance* (Moderate)  
The manufacturer has a stake in the outcome and nobody has had the chance to expose an error: exactly the warning signs the author names. The others show sensitivity to such signs.

**Q6. (b)** — *Tone and attitude* (Easy)  
The author says the picture is 'mistaken in two ways' yet ends by redefining, not abolishing, independence. 'Dismissive' and 'cynical' go too far.

**Q7. (c)** — *Scheduling puzzle with case elimination* (Hard)  
Number days Mon=1..Sat=6. B=k, A=k+1; E<k so k>=2. k=2: E=1, F=4, D=5, C=6 breaks (iii). k=4: D=3, C,E must fill 1-2 but C cannot be 1/2 by (vi). k=5: D=4, remaining days 1-2 again need C - fails (vi). k=3 (B=Wed, A=Thu): F=1 or 5; with F=5, C and E fill Mon/Tue - fails (vi). So F=1, E=2, D=6, C=5. Order: F, E, B, A, C, D. Tuesday = E. (Brute-force checked: unique.)

**Q8. (b)** — *Ranking and position after exchange* (Moderate)  
After exchange Ravi sits where Sunil sat, so Sunil's original position is 20th from left. He is also 15th from right, so total = 20 + 15 - 1 = 34. (Trap: 12 + 15 - 1 = 26 ignores the exchange.)

**Q9. (c)** — *Interleaved geometric series* (Moderate)  
Two interleaved series. Odd places: 2, 6, 18, 54 (x3 each) so the next is 162. Even places: 3, 6, 12, 24 (x2 each). The 9th term is an odd place: 54 x 3 = 162.

**Q10. (d)** — *Constraint-based digit puzzle* (Moderate)  
Let the code be ABCD. A = 2D and A + B + C + D = 20 with B + C = A gives 2A + D = 20, so 5D = 20, D = 4, A = 8, B + C = 8. B even, B > C, B + C = 8 and C prime gives B = 6, C = 2 (the pair 5 and 3 fails because B must be even). Code 8624. Check: 8534 has odd B; 8714 has C = 1 (not prime); 8264 has B < C.

**Q11. (c)** — *Data sufficiency - ordering* (Moderate)  
Alone, Statement 1 leaves R and T unplaced and Statement 2 leaves P and Q unplaced. Together: S < P < Q and S < T < R, so S is shorter than all four others. S is the shortest.

**Q12. (b)** — *Critical reasoning - resolving a paradox* (Moderate)  
A paradox is resolved by a fact that shows how the ban could itself have raised the weight: substitution by heavier plastic products. (a), (c) and (d) do not bear on why weight rose.

**Q13. (a)** — *Trade table - absolute vs percentage deficit (trap)* (Moderate)  
Deficits: 12, 9, 9, 18, 9. As % of imports: 12/42 = 28.6%; 9/45 = 20.0%; 9/54 = 16.7%; 18/66 = 27.3%; 9/72 = 12.5%. Highest is 2021. Trap: 2024 has the largest absolute deficit (18) but a lower percentage.

**Q14. (a)** — *Trade table - evaluating statements on growth* (Moderate)  
2021 to 2025: exports +110% (30 to 63), imports +71.4% (42 to 72), so (a) is true. (b) false: deficits 12, 9, 9, 18, 9. (c) false: export growth 20%, 25%, 6.7%, 31.3% - highest in 2025, lowest in 2024. (d) false: in 2022 exports +20% vs imports +7.1%.

**Q15. (c)** — *Grouped data - weighted mean from class mid-points (trap)* (Moderate)  
Mid-points 10, 30, 50, 70, 90: sum = 10x10 + 30x30 + 70x50 + 60x70 + 30x90 = 100 + 900 + 3500 + 4200 + 2700 = 11,400. Mean = 11,400/200 = 57. Trap: 50 is the simple average of the class mid-points, ignoring frequencies.

**Q16. (c)** — *Grouped data - percentile cut-off by interpolation (trap)* (Hard)  
Top 25% = 50 candidates. The 80-100 class has only 30, so 20 more come from the 60-80 class of 60. Cut-off = 80 - (20/60) x 20 = 80 - 6.67 = 73.3. Trap: 66.7 counts 20 candidates up from the bottom (60 + 6.67) instead of down from the top.

**Q17. (b)** — *Political pressure in beneficiary selection - integrity* (Moderate)  
Balanced and rule-abiding: no illegal inclusion, no confrontation for its own sake, an avenue for genuine cases, and a written record and escalation to protect against retaliation.

**Q18. (c)** — *Subordinate's public criticism - conduct rules and fairness* (Moderate)  
Proportionate and fair: hears the officer, upholds conduct rules, offers a legitimate channel. Suspension is disproportionate, inaction condones a breach, and surveillance by colleagues damages trust.

**Q19. (b)** — *Disaster triage - urgency, vulnerability and communication* (Moderate)  
Prioritise by urgency and vulnerability, not numbers or pressure; use alternative access for A, seek reinforcements, and communicate transparently. Equal split or waiting delays rescue.

**Q20. (b)** — *Successive percentage changes* (Moderate)  
Net factor = 1.2 x 0.9 x 1.25 = 1.35. Original = 16,200/1.35 = 12,000. Check: 12,000 -> 14,400 -> 12,960 -> 16,200.

**Q21. (a)** — *Inclusion-exclusion with three divisors* (Hard)  
Counts: div by 4 = 125; by 6 = 83; by 10 = 50. Pairs: LCM(4,6)=12 gives 41; LCM(4,10)=20 gives 25; LCM(6,10)=30 gives 16. All three: LCM=60 gives 8. Union = 125 + 83 + 50 - 41 - 25 - 16 + 8 = 184. Neither = 500 - 184 = 316. (184 is the trap - the number divisible by at least one.)

**Q22. (c)** — *Races - chaining distance ratios* (Hard)  
When A runs 200, B runs 180, so A:B = 10:9 in distance. When B runs 100, C runs 90, so B:C = 10:9. Hence A:C = 100:81. When A runs 400, C runs 400 x 81/100 = 324, so A wins by 76 m. (Trap: 80 adds 40 + 40 naively.)
