## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (analytical / economic / science)

Engineers who improve the efficiency of a machine usually describe their achievement in terms of the fuel it will save. The calculation looks simple: if a lamp now delivers the same light for a quarter of the energy, then lighting a house should require a quarter of the energy it did before. Economists have long been sceptical of this arithmetic. Efficiency lowers the effective price of the service a device provides — light, warmth, distance travelled — and when the price of anything falls, people tend to buy more of it. Householders who once lit one room now light gardens, façades and corridors; freight firms whose lorries travel further on a litre of diesel find longer routes worth running. Part of the engineer's saving is spent rather than banked. Where the saving is large enough to lower production costs across an entire economy, the additional output it makes possible may consume more energy than the original improvement saved.

The size of this "rebound" is contested, and the dispute is less empirical than definitional. Measured narrowly — one household, one appliance, one year — rebound is usually modest, perhaps a fifth of the expected saving. Measured across an economy over decades, the effect of efficiency cannot be cleanly separated from everything else that changed in the same period, and the estimates diverge accordingly. What is not in doubt is the direction. Efficiency is a means of obtaining more service from a given quantity of energy; it is not, by itself, an instrument for obtaining less energy. Policies that treat the two as equivalent will be disappointed by their own success. If the object is to reduce consumption, the price of energy must be prevented from falling as efficiency rises — which is precisely what a carbon tax, a quantity cap or a rising standard is for. Efficiency then becomes what it can be: a way of making a constraint cheaper to bear, rather than a substitute for imposing one.

**Q1.** Which one of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(a) Estimates of the rebound effect are unreliable because economy-wide effects cannot be isolated from other changes occurring at the same time.
(b) Engineers systematically overstate the benefits of their work because they disregard how consumers behave.
(c) Efficiency increases the service obtained per unit of energy, and therefore reduces total energy use only when it is paired with a measure that stops energy services from becoming cheaper.
(d) Carbon taxes and quantity caps are superior to efficiency standards as instruments of energy policy.

**Q3.** The author's recommendation that efficiency be accompanied by a carbon tax, a cap or a rising standard rests on which one of the following assumptions?

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

Two drivers set out one evening equally careless, equally drunk, equally indifferent to the risk they impose on others. One reaches home; the other, on a road no more dangerous than the first, meets a child who has stepped off a kerb. We do not judge them alike. The second is a killer — tried, imprisoned and, more to the point, reviled. The first collects a fine and an insurance penalty and is, within a year, forgiven by everyone including himself. Yet the entire difference between them lies in a fact neither of them controlled. Whether a child stepped off a kerb was not something either driver decided.

This is uncomfortable, because morality is supposed to be the one domain that luck does not reach. We do not blame people for their height or their inheritance; we blame them for what they do, and what they do is meant to be theirs. But once the thought is followed through, the province of the truly voluntary contracts alarmingly. What a person is disposed to want, the temperament that makes courage easy or cruelty tempting, the situations that arise to test them — none of these are chosen either. Strip away everything that luck supplied and there may be nothing left over to praise or blame.

Two responses are available, and neither is comfortable. The first insists that our ordinary judgements are simply confused, and that the two drivers deserve identical censure; the practice of punishing outcomes is then an administrative convenience, not a moral truth. The second accepts that moral assessment never was a matter of assessing a person's inner will alone, but of responding to what people actually do in a world they do not control. The first response is tidy, and leaves our deepest practices looking like a mistake. The second saves the practices at the cost of the very idea that made them seem defensible.

**Q4.** Which one of the following best expresses the main idea of the passage?

(a) Drunk drivers who cause a death should receive exactly the same punishment as those who do not.
(b) Ordinary moral judgement lets outcomes matter even though outcomes depend on luck, and neither available way of resolving that tension is free of cost.
(c) Since almost nothing a person does is truly voluntary, praise and blame ought to be abandoned altogether.
(d) The law punishes outcomes for reasons of administrative convenience rather than for moral reasons.

**Q5.** A general orders an attack that is reckless by every professional standard; an accident of weather routs the enemy and he is decorated. In terms of the author's argument, this case is best described as

(a) an instance of the same phenomenon, with luck operating in the direction of praise rather than blame.
(b) a counter-example, since military judgement is not moral judgement.
(c) irrelevant, because the general's recklessness was not itself voluntary.
(d) evidence that our moral practices are, after all, internally consistent.

**Q6.** The tone of the passage is best described as

(a) sceptical of the idea that morality can be insulated from luck, while declining to settle the question.
(b) indignant at a legal system that punishes misfortune.
(c) confident that ordinary moral judgement is mistaken and should be reformed.
(d) detached and purely descriptive, taking no position of its own.

# Answers & Worked Methods

## Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (c).** The passage's arc is: efficiency lowers the effective price of an energy service → consumption of that service rises → efficiency alone is not an instrument for cutting energy use → it must be paired with something that keeps the price from falling. (a) and (b) are sub-points made in passing; (d) reverses the author's position, which is that the two are complements, not rivals.

**Q2 — (b).** Cheaper buses mean more bus-kilometres demanded, so part of the 40 per cent engineering saving is spent on extra travel. (a) ignores rebound entirely; (d) overreaches — the passage says economy-wide backfire *may* occur, not that it must; (c) contradicts the passage's premise that demand responds to price.

**Q3 — (a).** Apply the negation test: if consumers did *not* buy more of a service when its effective price fell, there would be no rebound and nothing for a tax or cap to offset — the recommendation would collapse. That makes (a) a necessary assumption. (c) is far stronger than the argument needs (the author says rebound is often about a fifth).

**Q4 — (b).** The passage sets up a conflict (outcomes matter to our judgements; outcomes are luck) and closes by noting that the tidy response discredits our practices while the other saves the practices by giving up their rationale. (a) and (d) are contents of the first response only; (c) is a possibility the author raises and does not endorse.

**Q5 — (a).** Moral luck cuts both ways: the general's reputation, like the second driver's, is fixed by an outcome he did not control. The author's point concerns assessment in general, so praise is as much affected as blame.

**Q6 — (a).** The author calls the situation "uncomfortable", says the voluntary contracts "alarmingly", and ends by saying both responses cost something — engaged and sceptical, but deliberately unresolved. (d) fails on the evaluative language; (c) mistakes one of the two responses for the author's own.

## Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (c) U.**
From (i) and (iv), Q is below V with exactly three persons between, so the floor numbers differ by 4: (Q, V) = (1, 5), (2, 6) or (3, 7). By (v), P is on floor 2, so (2, 6) is out.

*Case (Q = 3, V = 7):* by (iii) R is on floor 5 or 7; 7 is V, so R = 5. Floors left are 1, 4 and 6 for S, T and U — but (ii) needs S immediately above T, and no two of 1, 4, 6 are consecutive. Case rejected.

*Case (Q = 1, V = 5):* by (iii) R = 7 (floor 5 is V). Floors left are 3, 4 and 6; the only consecutive pair is (3, 4), so T = 3 and S = 4, leaving U = 6. Check (vi): U is not on floor 7. ✔

Final order, 1 → 7: **Q, P, T, S, V, U, R.** Immediately below R (floor 7) is floor 6 = **U**.

**Q8 — (c) Cousin.**
K % L → K is L's daughter. L $ M → L is M's sister, so L is female and M is her brother. M * O → M is O's father. Therefore K is the child of L and O is the child of M, where L and M are siblings — K and O are children of a brother and sister, i.e. **cousins**. O's gender is unknown, but "cousin" does not depend on it, so the relation is determinate.

**Q9 — (d) 1, 2 and 3.**
"Only a few engineers are writers" means *some engineers are writers* and *some engineers are not writers*.

- **Conclusion 1:** Some engineers are writers + all writers are readers ⇒ those engineers are readers ⇒ *some engineers are readers*. Follows.
- **Conclusion 2:** All writers are readers + no reader is a farmer ⇒ no writer can be a farmer. Follows.
- **Conclusion 3:** From Conclusion 1 there exist engineers who are readers; no reader is a farmer, so those engineers are not farmers ⇒ *some engineers are not farmers*. Follows.

All three hold, so (d).

**Q10 — (c) Both together.**
*Statement 1 alone:* two-digit numbers with digit sum 12 are 39, 48, 57, 66, 75, 84, 93 — not unique.
*Statement 2 alone:* tens = 2 × units gives 21, 42, 63, 84 — not unique.
*Together:* from the second list, digit sums are 3, 6, 9 and 12; only **84** has digit sum 12. Unique, so both together suffice and neither alone does.

**Q11 — (c) 67.**
Differences: 9 − 7 = 2, 14 − 9 = 5, 24 − 14 = 10, 41 − 24 = 17. These are 1² + 1, 2² + 1, 3² + 1, 4² + 1. The next difference is 5² + 1 = 26, so the next term is 41 + 26 = **67**. (Option (a) 62 comes from assuming the differences grow by a constant 4.)

**Q12 — (a).**
Chain the conditionals: urgent ⇒ cleared the same day (rule i); cleared the same day ⇒ signed by the Section Officer (rule ii, read as "cleared same day *only if* signed"); signed by the Section Officer ⇒ entered in the register (rule iii). Hence urgent ⇒ entered in the register. (c) is the converse of rule (iii) and does not follow; (d) contradicts rule (i); (b) introduces a person the rules never mention.

## Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (c) 2.61.**
Fill the blanks first: A's production = 120 × 2.5 = 300; B's area = 270 ÷ 3.0 = 90; C's yield = 420 ÷ 150 = 2.8; D's production = 80 × 2.0 = 160.

Total area = 120 + 90 + 150 + 80 = **440** thousand hectares.
Total production = 300 + 270 + 420 + 160 = **1150** thousand tonnes.
Overall yield = 1150 ÷ 440 = **2.6136 ≈ 2.61**.

*Trap:* option (b) 2.575 is the plain average of the four yields (2.5 + 3.0 + 2.8 + 2.0) ÷ 4. A yield is a ratio, so it must be weighted by area — the districts have unequal areas, so the plain average is wrong.

**Q14 — (c) 153 thousand tonnes.**
New area = 80 × 1.5 = 120 thousand hectares; new yield = 2.61.
New production = 120 × 2.61 = 313.2 thousand tonnes.
Old production = 160 thousand tonnes.
Increase = 313.2 − 160 = **153.2 ≈ 153**.
(Option (d) 313 is the new production itself, not the increase; (a) 49 comes from raising the yield but forgetting the area; (b) 80 from raising the area but forgetting the yield.)

**Q15 — (c) 2024.**
Run the stock forward, year by year: closing stock = opening + produced − dispatched.

| Year | Opening | + Produced | − Dispatched | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 20 | 180 | 165 | **35** |
| 2022 | 35 | 210 | 200 | **45** |
| 2023 | 45 | 195 | 215 | **25** |
| 2024 | 25 | 230 | 205 | **50** |
| 2025 | 50 | 240 | 250 | **40** |

The peak closing stock is 50 thousand units, at the end of **2024**. Stock is a cumulative quantity — reading off the year with the largest production (2025) gives the wrong answer, because 2025 is also the year of the largest dispatch.

**Q16 — (c) 98.1%.**
Total produced = 180 + 210 + 195 + 230 + 240 = 1055.
Total dispatched = 165 + 200 + 215 + 205 + 250 = 1035.
1035 ÷ 1055 = 0.9810 → **98.1%**.
*Trap:* option (d) 101.9% is 1055 ÷ 1035, the ratio taken the wrong way round. Dispatches were less than production over the period (stock rose from 20 to 40), so the answer must be below 100% — a five-second sanity check that kills (d) outright.

## Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (b) 20 hours.**
Combined filling rate of A and B = 1/12 + 1/15 = 5/60 + 4/60 = 9/60 = **3/20** tank per hour.
Observed net rate with the leak = **1/10** tank per hour (the tank fills in 10 hours).
Leak rate = 3/20 − 1/10 = 3/20 − 2/20 = **1/20** tank per hour.
So the leak alone empties a full tank in **20 hours**.

**Q21 — (b) 43.74 litres.**
Each replacement of 8 litres from 80 leaves the fraction (1 − 8/80) = 9/10 of the milk.
After three such operations: 80 × (0.9)³ = 80 × 0.729 = **58.32** litres of milk (volume still 80 litres).
Removing 20 litres of a *uniform* 80-litre mixture removes one quarter of everything, leaving 3/4:
58.32 × 0.75 = **43.74** litres. Topping up with water does not change the milk.
(Option (d) 58.32 is the answer if the final step is missed; (c) 48.60 comes from doing only two replacements before the final draw.)

**Q22 — (d) 43/66.**
Total ways to choose 4 from 11 = C(11, 4) = 330.
Favourable (at least 2 women):
- 2W, 2M: C(5,2) × C(6,2) = 10 × 15 = 150
- 3W, 1M: C(5,3) × C(6,1) = 10 × 6 = 60
- 4W, 0M: C(5,4) = 5
Total = 150 + 60 + 5 = **215**. Probability = 215/330 = **43/66**.
*Check by complement:* 0 women = C(6,4) = 15; exactly 1 woman = C(5,1) × C(6,3) = 5 × 20 = 100; 15 + 100 = 115, and 330 − 115 = 215. ✔

## Timing / strategy tip

**Spend ten seconds bounding the answer before you compute anything in DI.** Q16 is the model: total dispatches over five years must be *less* than total production, because stock rose from 20 to 40 thousand units — so the answer is below 100 per cent and option (d) dies before a single addition. Q13 is the same discipline in reverse: the districts have unequal areas, so the weighted yield must sit between 2.0 and 3.0 but nearer the heavier districts — that kills (d) and makes the 2.575/2.61 distinction the only real work. In a paper where the arithmetic is rarely hard but the clock always is, the mark is usually lost to a wrong *setup*, not a wrong sum. Get the direction and the ballpark first; you will often find you only have to distinguish two survivors, and sometimes none.
