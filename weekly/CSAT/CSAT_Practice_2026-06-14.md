**Scoring note:** CSAT is *qualifying* — you need ~33% (≈66/200) to clear. Each question is worth 2.5 marks with 1/3 negative for wrong answers (decision-making items traditionally carry **no** negative marking). Set a 35-minute timer, attempt in one sitting, then check the Answers section.

## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I (Q1–Q3)

> Markets are remarkably efficient at allocating resources for goods whose benefits accrue privately to those who pay for them. Yet they falter conspicuously when confronted with goods whose benefits spill over to society at large. Basic scientific research is the paradigmatic example. The firm that funds the discovery of a fundamental principle — say, the structure of a molecule or a theorem in number theory — cannot easily prevent rivals from exploiting that knowledge once it is published. Knowledge, unlike a loaf of bread, is non-rivalrous: my use of it does not diminish yours. It is also, absent patents, largely non-excludable. Consequently, the private return to the firm that bears the cost of discovery is only a fraction of the social return that the discovery ultimately generates. A rational, profit-maximising firm will therefore invest in such research only up to the point where its own marginal benefit equals its marginal cost, stopping far short of the level that would be optimal for society. The result is chronic under-investment in precisely those activities that drive long-run growth. This is why nearly every advanced economy supplements private enterprise with public funding for universities and national laboratories, and why the case for such subsidy rests not on charity but on the cold logic of market failure. Critics who decry state funding of "useless," curiosity-driven research mistake the nature of the problem: the very unpredictability of basic research — the fact that one cannot know in advance which inquiry will yield the transformative application — is what makes it unattractive to private capital and indispensable for public support. To demand that each grant justify itself by an identifiable commercial payoff is to misunderstand why the market left the task undone in the first place.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Basic research is worthless unless it produces a commercial application.
- (b) Public funding of basic research is justified because markets systematically under-invest in goods whose benefits spill over widely.
- (c) Private firms are, in fact, the most efficient funders of all scientific research.
- (d) Knowledge should never be protected by patents of any kind.

**Q2.** The author's argument rests primarily on which underlying assumption?

- (a) The social return from basic research generally exceeds the private return captured by its funder.
- (b) Governments always spend their research budgets efficiently.
- (c) Every scientific discovery is eventually patented.
- (d) Private firms conduct no basic research whatsoever.

**Q3.** The author's attitude toward those who criticise state funding of curiosity-driven research is best described as:

- (a) Sympathetic and accommodating.
- (b) Detached and indifferent.
- (c) Critical — holding that they misunderstand the underlying economics.
- (d) Apologetic and conciliatory.

### Passage II (Q4–Q6)

> We are inclined to think of the self as a thing we possess — a stable core that persists unchanged beneath the flux of experience, the same entity that woke this morning as fell asleep last night. Yet under scrutiny this conviction dissolves. The body's cells are continuously replaced; beliefs are revised; memories are not faithful recordings but reconstructions, edited anew each time they are recalled. What, then, secures the identity of a person across a lifetime? One influential answer holds that the self is not a substance at all but a story — a narrative we ceaselessly compose, casting the disconnected episodes of our days into a coherent plot with ourselves as protagonist. On this view, to have a self is to be engaged in an act of perpetual authorship, weaving the present into a remembered past and an anticipated future. The appeal of this account is considerable, for it explains how we can change profoundly and yet remain, in some recognisable sense, the same person: the thread is not material but narrative continuity. But the account carries an unsettling implication. If the self is authored rather than discovered, then it is also, in part, a fiction — and a fiction is something that can be told well or badly, truthfully or self-servingly. The danger is not that the story is invented, for that is unavoidable, but that we may mistake a flattering edition for the truth, or cling to a plot that no longer fits the life we are actually living. Self-knowledge, on this telling, is less the unveiling of a hidden essence than the difficult discipline of revising one's story honestly in the light of what one has done.

**Q4.** The passage is primarily concerned with:

- (a) Proving that personal identity is a baseless illusion.
- (b) Defending the view that the self is best understood as a continually constructed narrative, and exploring its implications.
- (c) Arguing that memories are accurate recordings of the past.
- (d) Demonstrating that the body's cells never change.

**Q5.** It can be inferred that the author regards self-knowledge as:

- (a) An automatic and effortless process.
- (b) The discovery of a fixed essence hidden within us.
- (c) An ongoing, demanding effort to revise one's self-narrative truthfully.
- (d) Something ultimately impossible to achieve.

**Q6.** The author's tone in the latter half of the passage is best described as:

- (a) Triumphant and celebratory.
- (b) Cautionary — alert to the risk of self-deception.
- (c) Dismissive and mocking.
- (d) Resigned and despairing.

# Answers

| Q | Ans | Q | Ans | Q | Ans | Q | Ans | Q | Ans |

| 1 | (b) | 6 | (b) | 11 | (a) | 16 | (b) | 21 | (b) |

| 4 | (b) | 9 | (a) | 14 | (a) | 19 | (c) | 24 | (b) |

### Reading Comprehension
**Q1 — (b).** The whole passage builds the case that knowledge's non-rivalrous, non-excludable nature creates under-investment (a market failure) that public funding corrects.
**Q2 — (a).** The argument only works if society gains more than the funder does; that gap *is* the market failure being described.
**Q3 — (c).** The author says critics "mistake the nature of the problem" and "misunderstand" — a clearly critical, corrective stance.
**Q4 — (b).** It presents and defends the "self as narrative" view, then draws out its consequence (risk of self-deception).
**Q5 — (c).** The closing line frames self-knowledge as "the difficult discipline of revising one's story honestly" — ongoing effort, not discovery of an essence.
**Q6 — (b).** Words like "unsettling," "danger," and "self-servingly" signal a warning, not despair or celebration.

### Logical Reasoning
**Q7 — (c) G.** Middle (pos 4) = C; E at pos 7; F second to left of E = pos 5; D neighbours C so D = pos 3 (pos 5 is taken). A–B with three between and A left: only (2, 6) fits the gaps → A = 2, B = 6. The last seat, pos 1, is **G**. Final row: G A D C F B E.
**Q8 — (b) Only II.** "Some books are pencils" converts to "some pencils are books" → **II valid**. "All pens are books" + "some books are pencils" cannot guarantee any pen is a pencil → **I invalid**. The converse "all books are pens" is never given → **III invalid**.
**Q9 — (a) MJPO.** Each letter shifts +1: T→U, I→J, G→H, E→F, R→S. So L→M, I→J, O→P, N→O = **MJPO**.
**Q10 — (b) Sister.** "Only son of my grandfather" = the man's own father; the father's daughter = the man's **sister**.
**Q11 — (a) 5 km East.** Start (0,0): N5 → (0,5); right→E 3 → (3,5); right→S 5 → (3,0); left→E 2 → (5,0). He is 5 km due **East**.
**Q12 — (b) 38.** Differences are +3, +5, +7, +9 (consecutive odd numbers); next difference +11 → 27 + 11 = **38**.
**Q13 — (b) B.** Bottom→top: C(1), F(2), D(3), B(4), A(5), E(6). (C bottom; B two boxes above C = pos 4; A immediately above B = 5; E above A = 6; D above F gives D = 3, F = 2.) The box immediately above D is **B**.

### Data Interpretation
**Q14 — (a) 580.** 2024 column: 150 + 180 + 120 + 130 = **580 thousand**. (Trap (b) 520 = the 2023 column total.)
**Q15 — (b) 50%.** Delhi 120 → 180; increase = 60; 60 ÷ 120 × 100 = **50%**. (Trap (c) 60% confuses the absolute rise of 60 with the percentage.)
**Q16 — (b) 2.** Delhi (120<150<180) ✓ and Chennai (90<120<150) ✓ rise every year. Mumbai dips in 2024 (200→180); Kolkata dips in 2025 (130→100). So **2 cities**.
**Q17 — (b) 120.** Chennai: (90 + 120 + 150) ÷ 3 = 360 ÷ 3 = **120 thousand**. (Trap (d) 360 is the three-year total, not the average.)

### Decision-Making
**Q18 — (c).** Balances empathy with responsibility — address the issue directly and supportively before escalating; ignoring it or going straight to discipline are both unbalanced.
**Q19 — (c).** Protects accuracy while respecting the senior's position by raising it privately, not embarrassing them publicly or acting unilaterally.
**Q20 — (b).** Stays composed, treats the citizen with respect, and resolves the actual problem — the rule-abiding, service-oriented choice.

### Quantitative Aptitude
**Q21 — (b) 20%.** Take original = 100 → after +25% = 125. Required decrease = 25 ÷ 125 × 100 = **20%**. (General rule: a rise of *x*% needs a fall of x/(100+x)×100.)
**Q22 — (c) 15.** Let numbers be 3x and 5x. (3x + 9)/(5x + 9) = 3/4 → 4(3x+9) = 3(5x+9) → 12x + 36 = 15x + 27 → 3x = 9 → x = 3. Numbers are 9 and 15; the larger is **15**. (Check: 18 : 24 = 3 : 4 ✓.)
**Q23 — (b) 25 s.** Total distance = train + platform = 150 + 350 = 500 m. Speed = 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s. Time = 500 ÷ 20 = **25 s**. (Trap (d) 17.5 s ignores the train's own length: 350 ÷ 20.)
**Q24 — (b) 48.** DELHI has 5 distinct letters; vowels {E, I}. Treat the vowel pair as one block → 4 units arrange in 4! = 24 ways; the two vowels swap internally in 2! = 2 ways → 24 × 2 = **48**.
**Q25 — (a) 2/15.** P(both red) = C(4,2)/C(10,2) = 6/45 = **2/15**. (Trap (c) 4/25 wrongly assumes replacement: (4/10)².)

## Timing & strategy tip

**Do reasoning and DI first, RC last.** In CSAT the reasoning, DI, and quant items have a definite right answer you can *verify* on the spot, so they reward your quantitative strength with near-certain marks — bank them in the first ~20 minutes. RC passages are slower and more interpretive, so they belong at the end where leftover time won't bleed into the high-yield questions. Within any section, if a single puzzle or DI sub-question hasn't yielded a foothold in ~90 seconds, mark it and move on — a stuck question costs you the three easy marks you could have earned elsewhere, plus the negative if you guess it wrong.
