## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3

> It is one of the more counter-intuitive results in economics that two parties can both gain from trade even when one of them is more efficient at producing everything. Intuition rebels: surely the party that is better at every task has nothing to gain from dealing with a less capable partner. Yet the decisive question is not who is better in absolute terms but who sacrifices less. A surgeon who also happens to type faster than any clerk in the hospital still does well to leave the typing to the clerk, for every hour she spends at the keyboard is an hour stolen from the operating theatre, where her advantage — and the value of her time — is far greater. The clerk, hopeless at surgery, gives up almost nothing by typing. Each, by concentrating on the task in which their relative sacrifice is smallest, frees the other to do likewise, and the joint output of both rises.
>
> What the principle exposes is that efficiency is never absolute but always measured against the road not taken. The cost of producing a thing is whatever else one might have produced with the same effort, and it is this hidden cost, not the visible one, that should govern the division of labour. A nation that can grow both wheat and wine more cheaply than its neighbour may still enrich itself by specialising in whichever it gives up least to make, and importing the rest. The gains do not depend on one party being poor or weak; they flow from difference itself.
>
> To miss this is to confuse being good at something with being the right one to do it — a confusion that has justified much wasted effort, in workshops and economies alike.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) A party that is more efficient at producing everything has nothing to gain from trading with a less efficient one.
- (b) Mutual gains from trade arise from differences in opportunity cost, so even an all-round more efficient party benefits by specialising where its relative sacrifice is least.
- (c) Trade benefits only those nations that are poor or technologically weak.
- (d) A skilled professional should never delegate a task she can perform better than anyone else.

**Q2.** It can be inferred from the passage that whether a person ought to perform a task they are good at depends primarily on:

- (a) whether they are the single most skilled person available for that task.
- (b) the absolute amount of time the task requires.
- (c) what else they could produce with the same time and effort.
- (d) whether anyone else is willing to do it instead.

**Q3.** The author's claim that a nation "better at both" goods can still gain by specialising rests most directly on the assumption that:

- (a) the two nations are equally wealthy.
- (b) the resources used to make one good could instead be redirected to making the other.
- (c) one nation is militarily stronger than the other.
- (d) trade between the nations is entirely free of transport costs.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6

> We are inclined to treat happiness as the final measure of a life — to suppose that, whatever else a person pursues, what they are ultimately after is to feel good, and that a life rich in contentment is a life that has gone well. On this view, suffering is simply a cost, valuable at most as a means to some later pleasure and otherwise to be minimised. Yet a moment's reflection on the lives we actually admire unsettles the equation. The parent who sits through sleepless years, the researcher who labours for a recognition that may never come, the reformer who courts hostility for a cause — these are not, hour by hour, the happiest of people, and we do not honour them in spite of recognising this but, in some measure, because of it.
>
> What such lives possess is not pleasure but meaning, and the two can come apart. Meaning seems to attach to engagements that reach beyond the self — to projects, persons and ends whose worth does not reduce to how they make us feel. A life can be agreeable yet hollow, full of comfort and empty of significance; and a life can be hard, even sorrowful, yet weighted with a sense that it mattered. The pursuit of happiness, single-mindedly undertaken, may even be self-defeating, for the goods that confer meaning — love, commitment, the long effort toward something difficult — typically demand precisely the exposure to loss and frustration that the pure seeker of contentment is counselled to avoid.
>
> None of this is to despise happiness, which is no small thing. It is only to deny that it is the whole of the good — to insist that a life be assessed not merely by how it felt from the inside, but by what it was spent upon.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

- (a) Happiness is worthless and should be actively avoided in favour of suffering.
- (b) A good life is best measured by the total quantity of pleasure it contains.
- (c) Meaning and happiness can diverge, and a life's worth turns not only on how it felt but on what it was devoted to.
- (d) The people we admire are reliably less happy than everyone else.

**Q5.** The author's claim that a single-minded pursuit of happiness "may even be self-defeating" depends most directly on the assumption that:

- (a) happiness and pleasure are entirely without value.
- (b) the sources of meaning often require accepting risks of loss and frustration that a pure happiness-seeker would shun.
- (c) no one has ever achieved both happiness and meaning together.
- (d) suffering is good in itself, independent of anything it produces.

**Q6.** The author's overall attitude toward happiness is best described as:

- (a) dismissive — regarding it as worthless.
- (b) appreciative but qualified — valuing it while denying that it is the whole of the good.
- (c) hostile — treating the desire for happiness as a moral failing.
- (d) wholly indifferent.

## Answers & Worked Methods

**Q1 — (b).** *Main idea.* The passage builds from the "counter-intuitive" opening to its thesis: gains from trade come from differences in *opportunity cost* (relative sacrifice), so even an all-round more efficient party gains by specialising where it gives up least. (a) is the intuition the author rejects; (c) is explicitly denied ("not depend on one party being poor or weak"); (d) is contradicted by the surgeon example.

**Q2 — (c).** *Inference.* The surgeon-typist case turns on what each gives up — "every hour… is an hour stolen from the operating theatre." The right question is the road not taken, i.e. what else could be produced with the same effort. (a) restates the very confusion the passage warns against ("good at something" vs "the right one to do it").

**Q3 — (b).** *Assumption.* Opportunity cost is only defined if the same resources/effort can be redirected from one good to the other; negate (b) — if wheat-resources could not be switched to wine — and "gives up least to make" has no meaning and the argument collapses. (a) and (d) are not required; (c) is irrelevant.

**Q4 — (c).** *Main idea.* The passage argues meaning and happiness can "come apart," and closes by insisting a life be judged "not merely by how it felt… but by what it was spent upon." (a)/(d) are over-statements the text never makes; (b) is the view being criticised.

**Q5 — (b).** *Assumption.* The self-defeat claim works only if meaning's sources (love, commitment, hard effort) require exposure to loss/frustration — the very thing the contentment-seeker avoids. Negate that link and avoiding loss costs nothing, so the argument fails. (a)/(d) are caricatures the author explicitly rejects ("no small thing").

**Q6 — (b).** *Tone.* "None of this is to despise happiness, which is no small thing… only to deny that it is the whole of the good" — appreciative but qualified, not dismissive (a), hostile (c) or indifferent (d).

**Q7 — (b) E.** B = seat 1. "Three places to the right of B" ⇒ A = seat 4. C immediately left of A ⇒ C = 3. Remaining seats {2, 5, 6} for F, D, E; D and E adjacent with D left of E ⇒ only (D = 5, E = 6) is adjacent, so F = 2 (and F is not at the right end ✓). Row (1→6): **B, F, C, A, D, E.** Right end = **E**.

**Q8 — (b) Only II follows.** "All teachers are graduates" + "some graduates are researchers" does **not** force any teacher to be a researcher (the researcher-graduates may all be non-teachers), so I fails. II is the valid conversion of "some graduates are researchers" ⇒ "some researchers are graduates." Hence only **II** follows.

**Q9 — (a) DCS.** Shift each letter by its position in the word: 1st letter C → +1 → **D**; 2nd letter A → +2 → **C**; 3rd letter P → +3 → **S**. CAP → **DCS**. (Distractors mis-shift one letter.)

**Q10 — (b) Cousin.** B and D are siblings (both children of A; D is "brother of B"). C is B's daughter and E is D's daughter — children of two siblings — so C and E are **cousins**. Because every link is stated explicitly, there is no paternal/maternal ambiguity.

**Q11 — (c) 126.** Differences are 1, 3, 9, 27 — a geometric chain (×3). The next difference is 81, so 45 + 81 = **126.** Check: 5 (+1) 6 (+3) 9 (+9) 18 (+27) 45 (+81) **126** ✓.

**Q12 — (a) U.** From the clues: U < R < P, T immediately before P, P < Q, and S last (6th). Testing P's slot, only P = 4 works: then T = 3, Q = 5, and U, R fill seats 1–2 with U < R ⇒ U = 1, R = 2. Full order: **U, R, T, P, Q, S.** Winner = **U.** (P = 5 forces Q into the last slot already taken by S; P = 3 leaves no room for both U and R ahead of it.)

**Q13 — (c) Solar.** Percentage rises: Thermal 60/600 = 10%; Hydro 50/250 = 20%; Solar 80/100 = **80%**; Wind 10/50 = 20%. Highest is **Solar.** Trap (a) Thermal has the largest *absolute* increase (60 MU) but only a 10% rise.

**Q14 — (b) 45%.** 2025 renewables = 300 + 180 + 60 = 540 MU; total 2025 = 1200. 540 / 1200 = **45%.** Trap (d) 54% comes from dividing 540 by the *2024* total of 1000.

**Q15 — (b) 372.** Engineering = 60% × 1200 = 720; Operations = 480. Women: 25% × 720 = 180 (Eng) and 40% × 480 = 192 (Ops). Total = 180 + 192 = **372.** Check: men = 540 + 288 = 828; 372 + 828 = 1200 ✓.

**Q16 — (b) 3 : 2.** Operations = 480; women = 192, so men = 288. Ratio men : women = 288 : 192 = **3 : 2.** Trap (a) 2 : 3 reverses the ratio.

**Q17 — (b).** Balanced and rule-abiding: keep the fair, need-based order and reassure the leader that everyone will be served — neither caves to pressure (a), punishes the public by stopping (c), nor escalates into a public confrontation (d).

**Q18 — (c).** Correct the error promptly and honestly with the client, then privately coach the junior — upholds integrity without a cover-up (a), avoids disproportionate punishment (b) and the irregular back-channel of (d).

**Q19 — (c).** Apply the rule consistently while addressing the high performer privately — fair to the team and respectful to the individual, unlike ignoring it (a), public humiliation (b) or abandoning the rule for everyone (d).

**Q20 — (a) 25/42.** Total ways = C(9,3) = 84. At least two red = exactly 2 + exactly 3 = C(5,2)·C(4,1) + C(5,3) = 40 + 10 = 50. Probability = 50/84 = **25/42.** Trap (b) 10/21 = 40/84 counts only "exactly two red."

**Q21 — (b) ₹6,500.** For 2 years, CI − SI = P·(r/100)² = P·(10/100)² = P/100. So P/100 = 65 ⇒ P = **₹6,500.** Check: SI = 6500 × 0.2 = 1300; CI = 6500 × (1.21 − 1) = 1365; difference = 65 ✓.

**Q22 — (b) 240.** LEADER has 6 letters with E repeated twice ⇒ total arrangements = 6!/2! = 360. Treating the two E's as one block: 5! = 120 arrangements with the E's together. Never together = 360 − 120 = **240.** Trap (c) 360 is the total; (a) 120 is the "together" count.
