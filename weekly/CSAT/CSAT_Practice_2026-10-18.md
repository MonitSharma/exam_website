## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

**Q1.** Read the passage and answer the question(s) that follow.

The parable of the common pasture holds that individuals sharing a resource will, each pursuing his own advantage, ruin it for all. The parable has proved durable partly because it is tidy: it requires only self-interest and an open field. Yet fieldwork on irrigation channels, village forests and inshore fisheries across several continents has recorded communities that sustained such resources for centuries without either a distant state or private titles. What distinguished them was neither virtue nor luck. Their users were clearly identified, so that outsiders could be excluded; the rules on extraction were fitted to local conditions rather than imported; those affected by the rules had a hand in revising them; monitors were drawn from, or answerable to, the users; and penalties began lightly and rose with repeated offence. Where these features were absent, the same communities were as capable of exhausting a resource as the parable predicts.

It would be a mistake to conclude that communal management is a universal remedy. The arrangements that worked were small, were embedded in dense relationships, and depended on a stable population that expected to share the resource for generations. When a market suddenly widens the demand for a fish or a timber, or when migration dilutes the web of mutual obligation, the old rules can collapse swiftly. Nor is state ownership vindicated; central agencies that replaced local rules with uniform prescriptions have often left the resource worse off, since they destroyed the local knowledge on which compliance had rested while lacking the means to police remote areas. The lesson, then, is less about which owner is best than about which conditions make cooperation cheaper than defection. Policy that treats institutions as interchangeable, handing a resource to the market, the state or the community according to ideological preference, misses precisely the variable that fieldwork shows to matter.

- (c) Whether a shared resource survives depends less on who owns it than on institutional conditions that make cooperation cheaper than defection.
- (d) Small communities have always managed shared resources better than markets or states, so control should be devolved to them.

**Q4.** Read the passage and answer the question(s) that follow.

We are inclined to hold that people should be judged only for what lies within their control. Two lorry drivers drive home after a long shift, equally tired and equally careless, and differ only in that a child runs into the road in front of one of them. We nevertheless judge the unlucky driver more harshly, and he himself, if he is decent, will feel a guilt his colleague never has to feel. The philosopher who insists that the two are morally identical must therefore explain away not only our verdicts but also the unlucky driver's own remorse, which seems to be a fitting response rather than a mistake.

One tempting resolution is to say that our reactions are merely confused and should be revised until they track only intentions and choices. But this revision proves hard to stop. What a person chooses depends upon his temperament, and temperament upon upbringing; the circumstances in which he is tested are no more his making than the child's path. Press the principle far enough and it dissolves the agent himself, leaving a bundle of causes to which neither praise nor blame can sensibly attach. A morality that survives only by ceasing to apply to anyone is a poor ally of the principle it was designed to protect.

It does not follow that we must simply accept outcomes as the measure of persons. A defensible middle course distinguishes the assessment of character from the assessment of responsibility for what happened. The careless drivers are equally defective in the former and unequally burdened in the latter, because to act is to stake oneself on the world's cooperation, and the stake is lost or won partly beyond one's control. Recognising this need not license cruelty to the unlucky; it may instead explain why the unlucky driver's sorrow is not self-indulgence, and why the lucky one has reason, though no one will demand it of him, for a quiet gratitude.

- (c) Moral philosophy cannot reach any settled view on blame, since every action is caused by prior circumstances.
- (d) The principle that only what lies in one's control counts morally cannot be sustained in full, yet a distinction between character and responsibility for outcomes preserves what is sound in it.

# Answers & Worked Methods

**Q1 — (c)** · *Main idea - governance of shared resources* · Moderate  
The closing sentences state the thesis: conditions, not owner type, matter. (a) and (d) are the single-owner remedies the author rejects; (b) overstates, since the passage says communal management is no universal remedy and that the same communities can exhaust resources.

**Q2 — (b)** · *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* · Moderate  
The passage faults policy that treats institutions as interchangeable. (a) and (d) are the over-claims the author avoids; (c) praises uniform state rules, which the passage criticises.

**Q3 — (a)** · *Assumption - why uniform state rules fail* · Hard  
Negation test: if compliance did not depend on local knowledge and stake, destroying that knowledge would not matter, and the author's explanation collapses. (c) and (d) are absolute claims the passage does not need.

**Q4 — (d)** · *Main idea - moral luck and responsibility* · Moderate  
The passage states the control principle, shows it dissolves the agent if pressed, then offers a middle course. (a) and (b) are the two extremes it rejects; (c) is the dissolution it treats as a reductio, not its conclusion.

**Q5 — (a)** · *Inference - author's likely judgement on a case* · Moderate  
The last sentence says the lucky driver has reason for a quiet gratitude. (b) and (c) contradict the middle course; (d) conflicts with the claim that the unlucky driver's sorrow is fitting.

**Q6 — (c)** · *Tone and attitude - towards the control principle* · Moderate  
The author grants the principle's pull ('tempting', 'protect') but shows it cannot be pressed to its end. Not dismissive (a), not agreeing (b), and clearly not neutral (d).

**Q7 — (a)** · *Puzzle - floors of a building* · Hard  
B is immediately above D, so (D,B) is (1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(4,5) or (5,6). A is three floors from B. E is odd and below D, so D is at least 2. Try (D,B)=(5,6): A=3. Remaining floors 1,2,4 for C,E,F: E odd and below D gives E=1; then C=F+2 means F=2, C=4. Check other (D,B) pairs: (4,5) gives A=2 (or 8, impossible); remaining 1,3,6 for C,E,F: C=F+2 needs F=1,C=3 leaving E=6, not odd, and E<D fails. (3,4): A=1 (A=7 impossible); remaining 2,5,6: E odd and below 3 impossible. (2,3): A=6; remaining 1,4,5, E=1 (odd, below 2), C=F+2 needs 4,5 differing by 1, fails. (1,2): D=1 leaves nothing below for E. So unique: E1, F2, A3, C4, D5, B6. Immediately above A (3) is C on 4.

**Q8 — (b)** · *Cubes - painted faces and cutting* · Moderate  
Red faces meet blue faces along 4 edges at the top and bottom (2 edges on the top, 2 on the bottom). Each edge has 5 - 2 = 3 non-corner cubes: 4 x 3 = 12. Corner cubes touch one red, one blue and one unpainted face, so every one of the 8 corners also has exactly two painted faces in different colours: +8. Total 20. Trap: forgetting the corners (12). Edges between blue and unpainted faces carry only one colour.

**Q9 — (d)** · *Blood relations - coded relation chain* · Moderate  
R is mother of S; S is brother of T, so T is male (as the husband of U) and a child of R; T is husband of U; U is sister of V. T is therefore the husband of V's sister: V's brother-in-law, whatever V's gender. 'Cousin' and 'son-in-law' are not supported.

**Q10 — (c)** · *Series - alternating number and letter pattern* · Moderate  
Numbers: 1^2+1, 2^2+1, 3^2+1, 4^2+1, 5^2+1, next 6^2+1 = 37. Letters: Z(26), W(23), T(20), Q(17), N(14), each 3 back, next K(11). Answer 37K.

**Q11 — (a)** · *Direction sense - successive turns* · Moderate  
Take the start as (0,0), east and north positive. West 9: (-9,0). Right (north) 12: (-9,12). Right (east) 15: (6,12). Right (south) 4: (6,8). Distance = sqrt(6^2 + 8^2) = 10 m, and he is east and north of the start: North-East.

**Q12 — (d)** · *Conditional logic - valid deduction* · Moderate  
From (2) and (3): the ministry did not waive the survey (contrapositive). From (4) and (1): complete or waived. Waiver is ruled out, so the survey is complete. (b) contradicts (3); (a) contradicts the first deduction; (c) contradicts the conclusion.

**Q13 — (c)** · *Table - percentage departure vs absolute difference (trap)* · Moderate  
Departures: A +80/800 = +10%; B -10%; C +75/500 = +15%; D +60/1500 = +4%; E -25%. C is highest in percentage terms. Trap: A has the largest absolute excess (80 mm).

**Q14 — (b)** · *Table - aggregate vs average of percentages (trap)* · Moderate  
Total normal = 800+1200+500+1500+400 = 4400; total actual = 880+1080+575+1560+300 = 4395. Difference -5 mm, i.e. 5/4400 = 0.11%. Traps: (a) is the simple average of the five percentage departures ((10-10+15+4-25)/5 = -1.2%), invalid because the bases differ; (c) misreads the 5 mm as 5%.

**Q15 — (d)** · *Table - profit margin vs total profit (trap)* · Moderate  
Profit per unit / cost: P 50/200 = 25%; Q 100/300 = 33.3%; R 15/135 = 11.1%; S 140/340 = 41.2%. S is highest. Trap: Q has the highest total profit (40 x 100 = Rs 4,000 thousand versus S at 20 x 140 = Rs 2,800 thousand), but a larger volume, not a better margin.

**Q16 — (b)** · *Table - overall profit percentage on cost (weighted, trap)* · Hard  
Revenue (Rs thousand): 10,000 + 16,000 + 9,000 + 9,600 = 44,600. Cost: 8,000 + 12,000 + 8,100 + 6,800 = 34,900. Profit 9,700. 9,700/34,900 = 27.8%. Traps: (a) 21.7% is profit on revenue (9,700/44,600); (d) is Q's margin alone. A simple average of the four margins is close but not valid as a method.

**Q17 — (c)** · *Integrity - improper instruction from a superior* · Moderate  
Truthful records and rule-adherence cannot be traded for a superior's assurance; the balanced response is courteous refusal, a constructive fix, and escalation only if pressed. (a) falsifies records; (b) evades; (d) skips a reasonable first step and is premature.

**Q18 — (b)** · *Workplace harassment complaint* · Moderate  
Follow the statutory process (ICC), protect the complainant, and respect due process for the accused. (a) and (d) pressure the complainant; (c) pre-judges the outcome.

**Q19 — (d)** · *Competing duties - deadline versus personal emergency* · Moderate  
The balanced, empathetic and accountable course is to release her, secure continuity, share the workload, and keep the superior informed. (a) is insensitive; (b) is irresponsible; (c) puts the work above a genuine emergency.

**Q20 — (c)** · *Number theory - sum of integers coprime to n* · Hard  
If gcd(n,100)=1 then gcd(100-n,100)=1, so the coprime numbers pair up with sum 100. Their count is phi(100) = 100 x (1/2) x (4/5) = 40, giving 20 pairs. Sum = 20 x 100 = 2000 (n = 100 itself is not coprime to 100).

**Q21 — (a)** · *Time and work - combined rates* · Moderate  
Add the three pair-rates: 2(A+B+C) = 1/12 + 1/15 + 1/20 = 12/60 = 1/5, so A+B+C = 1/10 per day. In 2 days they finish 1/5. B+C = 1/15 per day, so the remaining 4/5 takes (4/5) x 15 = 12 days. Total 2 + 12 = 14 days.

**Q22 — (d)** · *Probability - complementary counting* · Hard  
With three colours and three balls, the opposite of 'at least two alike' is 'all three different'. Ways: 4 x 5 x 3 = 60. Total ways: C(12,3) = 220. P(all different) = 60/220 = 3/11, so the required probability = 1 - 3/11 = 8/11. Trap: 3/11 is the complement.
