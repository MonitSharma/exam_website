Where the real paper differs from a representative item, the official key wins.

The point of each item below is the **trap** — the option designed to catch the candidate who reasons one step too fast.

### A. Reading Comprehension

**RC-1 (Representative — "central argument" trap).**
A passage argues that public funding of basic research is justified *because markets under-invest in goods whose benefits spill over to society*. Question: "Which statement best reflects the central argument?"
- Trap option: *"Basic research has no commercial value."* — This is **a detail the author rejects**, not the thesis. It's tempting because the word "commercial" appears in the passage, so it *feels* on-topic.
- Correct: the option about **market under-investment in spillover (public) goods**.
- **Method:** for "central argument," pick the option that the **whole passage** would have to support — not the sharpest or most quotable line. If an option is true of only one sentence, it's a detail, not the main idea. Eliminate any option containing absolutes ("no value", "never", "always") unless the author literally said it.

**RC-2 (Representative — inference vs. stated fact).**
Question: "Which can be **inferred**?" Trap option restates a sentence **verbatim**.
- A verbatim restatement is **stated, not inferred** — in UPSC RC the "inference" answer is one logical step beyond the text but still fully supported. The verbatim option is the decoy.
- **Method:** for "infer" questions, reject anything that is word-for-word in the passage, and reject anything needing **outside knowledge**. The answer sits in the narrow band: *follows necessarily, but isn't printed.*

**RC-3 (Representative — "assumption" question).**
Question: "The argument rests on which assumption?"
- **Method (negation test):** the correct assumption is the one that, **if false, collapses the argument**. Test each option by negating it. The one whose negation destroys the conclusion is the answer; options that can be false while the argument still stands are wrong. This single test resolves most CSAT assumption items and is the highest-leverage RC skill for 2023-style passages.

### B. Quantitative / Basic Numeracy (the heaviest 2023 block)

**Q-4 (Representative — percentage change, the classic reversal trap).**
"A price rises 20%, then falls 20%. Net change?"
- Trap: **0%** (the "they cancel" instinct).
- Correct: **−4%**. 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96.
- **Shortcut:** for a +x% then −x% sequence, net change is always **−(x²/100)%**. Here −(400/100) = −4%. Memorise this; UPSC reuses it (also as profit-then-loss, salary hike-then-cut).

**Q-5 (Representative — average trap on inclusive counting).**
"Average of first n natural numbers" / "average of consecutive integers."
- Trap: dividing by the wrong count, or forgetting that for an **evenly spaced set** the average = (first + last)/2 regardless of how many terms.
- **Shortcut:** for any arithmetic progression, mean = (first + last)/2. Don't sum-and-divide under time pressure. The trap option usually equals the **median of a mis-stated range** (off-by-one on the endpoints).

**Q-6 (Representative — ratio / mixture).**
"Vessel has milk:water = 5:1. Some mixture is removed and replaced with water..."
- Trap: applying the ratio to the **removed** quantity as if concentration stayed constant.
- **Method:** track the **absolute amount of milk** (the thing that only leaves, never enters), not the ratio. Replacement problems: milk_final = milk_initial × (1 − removed/total)^(number of replacements). Reason on the conserved quantity; the ratio is a *result*, not a working variable.

**Q-7 (Representative — time & work / pipes).**
"A does a job in 12 days, B in 18 days; together?"
- Trap: averaging the days → 15. Wrong.
- Correct: add **rates**, not times. 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 → **36/5 = 7.2 days**.
- **Shortcut:** use **LCM as total work**. Work = 36 units; A = 3/day, B = 2/day, together 5/day → 36/5 = 7.2 days. LCM-units kills almost every 2023-style work/pipe/efficiency question with no fractions.

**Q-8 (Representative — divisibility / number properties).**
"For how many values… is the product divisible by 100?" (2023 had number-property items of this flavour.)
- Trap: counting factors of 10 once.
- **Method:** 100 = 2² × 5². You need **at least two 2s and two 5s** in the prime factorisation. Count factors of 5 (usually the binding constraint, since 2s are plentiful) and confirm enough 2s. Most "divisible by 100 / trailing-zeros" questions reduce to **counting 5s**.

### C. Data Interpretation

**Q-9 (Representative — DI percentage-of-total trap).**
A table/pie gives shares; question asks "by what % is A greater than B?"
- Trap: subtracting the two **percentage points** (e.g., 30% − 20% = "10%").
- Correct: percentage *difference* = (A − B)/B × 100 = (30 − 20)/20 = **50%**.
- **Method:** "% greater than B" always divides by **B**, not by the total. Percentage-points and percentage-difference are different animals; UPSC DI almost always baits the points-subtraction option.

### D. Logical & Analytical Reasoning

**Q-10 (Representative — seating / linear arrangement).**
"Six people in a row, a chain of 'left of / immediately right of / not adjacent to' clues."
- Trap: assuming "left of" means **immediately** left. It usually means *somewhere* to the left unless "immediately/next to" is stated.
- **Method:** draw the row, fix the **most constrained clue first** (the one with "immediately" or an end position), then place the rest. Don't read "left of" as adjacency — that single misread generates a fully self-consistent but **wrong** arrangement, which is exactly the trap answer.

**Q-11 (Representative — coding-decoding / pattern).**
- Trap: solving by **letter shift** when the rule is **positional/reverse-alphabet**, or vice-versa.
- **Method:** test **two** independent letters against your hypothesised rule before committing. If both fit, apply; if not, switch hypothesis. Half of 2023-style coding traps come from confirming the rule on a single letter that happens to satisfy *both* candidate rules.

**Q-12 (Representative — syllogism / "which conclusion follows").**
- Trap: a conclusion that is **factually true in the real world** but **not entailed** by the given statements.
- **Method:** answer **only from the statements**, treat them as true even if absurd, and reject any conclusion requiring real-world knowledge. Use Venn possibilities: a conclusion "follows" only if it holds in **every** valid diagram, not just one.

## 3. Recurring patterns UPSC keeps repeating

1. **The "main idea vs. seductive detail" RC trap.** Every year, RC central-argument questions offer one option that quotes a vivid line from the passage. The whole-passage test (an option must be supported by the *entire* passage, not one sentence) beats it almost every time. This is the single most reliable RC pattern across 2018–2025.

2. **Percentage / ratio reversibility traps in quant.** +x% then −x% ≠ no change; "percentage points" ≠ "percentage difference"; averaging rates instead of adding them. These three reappear in some form nearly every year. Internalise the formulas (−x²/100; divide-by-base; LCM-units) so they're reflex, not derivation, under time pressure.

3. **"Follows / can be inferred / assumption" all test the same muscle:** strictly-from-the-text reasoning with no outside knowledge, decided by the **negation test** for assumptions and the **every-valid-case test** for conclusions. RC and logical reasoning share this engine — train it once, score in both sections.

## 4. One strategy tweak for next time

**Run RC on a hard time-box: 75 seconds per question, two passes.** 2023 RC was long enough that strong candidates lost the paper by sinking 4–5 minutes into one stubborn passage. This week, give each RC question **75 seconds**; if you're not converging, mark it, circle the question number, and move on — then return in a dedicated second pass with the minutes you bank from the easy quant. Score your attempt **two ways**: actual net marks, *and* a "no-blind-guess" net (counting only answers you had a real reason for). If the gap between them is large, your leak is **reckless guessing**, not knowledge — and that's the cheapest 10 marks you'll ever recover before next week.
