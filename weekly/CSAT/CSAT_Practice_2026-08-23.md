## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (science / policy / analytical)

> For most of the twentieth century the policy of forest agencies in temperate countries was stated as an objective and pursued as a duty: put out every fire, and put it out early. The policy worked, in the narrow sense that fires were extinguished. Its failure is legible only in aggregate and only over decades. Many of the forests in question had burned lightly and often for millennia — grasses and litter consumed at intervals short enough that fuel never accumulated to a dangerous depth. Suppression did not remove fire from these systems; it converted a regime of frequent small fires into one of rare enormous ones, and it did so throughout a period in which the annual record showed steady improvement.
>
> That inversion is the general form of a problem which recurs far outside forestry. A system exposed to small, regular disturbances develops a structure that metabolises them. Remove the disturbances and the structure does not become safer; it becomes untested. Fuel accumulates — in a forest as biomass, in a financial system as leverage, in a bureaucracy as unexamined procedure — and the accumulated quantity is invisible precisely because nothing has recently forced it into view. The interval between events lengthens while the severity of the eventual event grows, so that the running average of harm may fall for a long time before it rises catastrophically.
>
> The practical difficulty is not that any of this is unknown. It is that the costs of prevention are concentrated, immediate and attributable, while its benefits are diffuse, delayed and counterfactual. An official who authorises a controlled burn owns every acre of smoke it produces; an official who suppresses fire for thirty years owns nothing, and will in any case have been transferred before the reckoning. Rationality at the level of a career is not rationality at the level of a forest — which is why the remedy, where it exists at all, has less to do with better ecology than with who is held to account for what, and over what horizon.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(b) Officials in forest agencies are generally negligent and inadequately trained in ecology.
(c) Controlled burning is the most cost-effective technique currently available for managing accumulated forest fuel.
(d) Suppressing small and regular disturbances can convert them into rare catastrophic ones, and the obstacle to correcting this lies in how costs and credit are distributed among decision-makers.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> Forgiveness is easily confused with three things it is not, and the confusions are instructive. It is not excuse: to excuse is to decide that the person did not, in the relevant sense, do it — that they were coerced, or ignorant, or not themselves — and where an excuse succeeds there is nothing left to forgive. It is not condonation: to condone is to hold that what was done was not, after all, wrong, which again dissolves the object. And it is not forgetting, which is not an act at all but the eventual failure of a faculty, and which can as easily leave resentment intact beneath it.
>
> What remains, once these are set aside, is peculiar. The one who forgives continues to judge the act wrong and the agent responsible for it, and nonetheless gives up something to which that judgement entitles her — the standing to resent, to reproach, to hold the deed permanently against its author. This is why forgiveness cannot be owed. A wrong generates a claim in the hands of the wronged, and a claim one is obliged to relinquish was never a claim at all. The moral pressure that others so often apply — that it is time, that bitterness is corrosive, that the offender has suffered enough — may be sound advice, but it is not the enforcement of a duty, and to treat it as one is to wrong the injured party a second time.
>
> Nor is forgiveness the same as reconciliation, though the two travel together often enough to be mistaken for one. A person may forgive and still decline to trust, still keep the distance that prudence recommends. To insist otherwise is to require of the injured not only that she surrender her resentment but that she stake her safety upon the surrender — a demand that looks generous only from the outside.

(d) Resentment is invariably corrosive and ought to be relinquished for the sake of the victim's own wellbeing.

## Answers & Worked Methods

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (d).** The passage moves from the fire example to the general principle (suppressed small disturbances → rare large ones) and ends on the incentive asymmetry; (d) contains both halves. (a) is more extreme than the text, (b) is a character judgement the author explicitly avoids by locating the problem in horizons of accountability, and (c) is a technique the passage never endorses as "most cost-effective".

**Q2 — (c).** "The running average of harm may fall for a long time before it rises catastrophically" — a falling average is precisely what the author says is compatible with accumulating risk. (b) overstates ("never carry information").

**Q3 — (a).** For the remedy to be about accountability rather than ecology, the ecological knowledge must already be adequate — otherwise better ecology would still be the binding constraint. Negate (a) and the claim collapses; that is the assumption test.

**Q4 — (c).** The passage is structured as four contrasts (excuse, condonation, forgetting, reconciliation) and one positive definition — the surrender of a retained claim.

**Q5 — (b).** Coercion is the author's own example of an excuse, and "where an excuse succeeds there is nothing left to forgive."

**Q6 — (c).** The author calls such counsel "sound advice" but denies it is a duty, and says treating it as one wrongs the victim again — critical, but qualified, not contemptuous.

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (b).** Row 1 faces north, so left = west; Row 2 faces south, so left = east. Let positions 1–4 run west to east.
D is immediately west of A, and A is not at an end, so (D,A) = (1,2) or (2,3). Exactly one person sits between B and D, and B is at an end. If (D,A) = (1,2), then B and C occupy 3 and 4 with |B − 1| = 2 ⇒ B = 3, which is not an end — rejected. So D = 2, A = 3; B and C take 1 and 4, and |B − 2| = 2 ⇒ B = 4, C = 1.
**Row 1 (W→E): C, D, A, B.**
C faces Q, so Q = column 1 (an end, as required). R immediately left of P means R is one seat *east* of P, so (P,R) = (2,3) or (3,4). S faces the person immediately east of A, i.e. column 4 (B), so S = 4, forcing P = 2, R = 3.
**Row 2 (W→E): Q, P, R, S.**
A faces R. R faces south, so R's right is west: one right = P, two right = Q. **Answer: Q.** (Trap (d) comes from taking "right" as east.)

**Q8 — (c) 2037.** 2026 is an ordinary year, so the calendar repeats when the accumulated odd days from 2026 onwards total a multiple of 7 *and* the target year has the same leap status. Odd days: 2026→1, 2027→1, 2028→2, 2029→1, 2030→1, 2031→1, 2032→2, 2033→1, 2034→1, 2035→1, 2036→2. Running total after 2036 = 1+1+2+1+1+1+2+1+1+1+2 = 14 ≡ 0 (mod 7). So 1 January 2037 falls on the same weekday as 1 January 2026, and 2037 is likewise an ordinary year. (Verified: 1 Jan 2026 and 1 Jan 2037 are both Thursdays.) The 11-year gap is the standard one when a leap year sits close behind the base year.

**Q9 — (c) Both follow.** All novels ⊆ books, and books ∩ manuscripts = ∅, so novels ∩ manuscripts = ∅ ⇒ I follows. Some manuscripts are scrolls; those scrolls are manuscripts, and no manuscript is a book, so those scrolls are not books ⇒ II follows. II is the chain that is usually missed — a universal negative plus a particular affirmative yields a valid particular negative.

**Q10 — (a) 11.** Compare sentences pairwise on the *common word*:
- S1 ∩ S2 = {cargo}; common code = 4 ⇒ cargo = 4.
- S1 ∩ S3 = {heavy}; common code = 27 ⇒ heavy = 27.
- S1 ∩ S4 = {ships, heavy}; common codes = {19, 27}; heavy = 27 ⇒ ships = 19.
S1 = {19, 4, 27, 11} minus ships, cargo, heavy ⇒ **carry = 11.**

**Q11 — (d) Cannot be determined.** M is female (grandmother), Q is female (daughter-in-law). P is male (husband of M), O is male (only son of M), L is male (brother of N). N's gender is nowhere fixed — "L is the brother of N" constrains L, not N. So the count is either two or three; it cannot be determined. Trap (a): assuming N is male by default.

**Q12 — (b) Z.** Rank 1 = highest. "X scored more than exactly two candidates" ⇒ X is rank 3. The chain is Y > W > Z. V is not rank 1, so rank 1 must come from {Y, W, Z}, and only Y can top that chain ⇒ Y = 1. The remaining ranks 2, 4, 5 go to V, W, Z with W > Z. Possible orders: Y-V-X-W-Z, Y-W-X-V-Z, Y-W-X-Z-V. The condition V > W leaves only **Y-V-X-W-Z**, so Z is lowest. Without that condition the answer would be indeterminate — which is why the "if" clause is doing real work.

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (b) 60–80.** Top 15% of 400 = 60 students. The 80–100 band holds only 50, so the 60th student from the top must be drawn from the 60–80 band; 10 of that band's 120 students qualify. The exact cut-off is unknown (the distribution within the band is not given), but it necessarily *lies* in 60–80. Trap (d): the interval is determinable even though the exact mark is not.

**Q14 — (c) 55.** Cumulative frequencies: 35, 110, 230, 350, 400. The 200th observation lies in the 40–60 class. Median = 40 + [(200 − 110)/120] × 20 = 40 + (90/120)(20) = 40 + 15 = **55**. Trap (a) 50: taking the mid-point of the median class instead of interpolating.

**Q15 — (a) ₹22,000.** Real wage in base-year rupees = nominal × (base index / current index) = 33,000 × 100/150 = **₹22,000**. His real wage has fallen from ₹24,000. Trap (c) ₹26,400 = 33,000 × 0.8, i.e. deflating by the 20% "gap" rather than by the ratio.

**Q16 — (b) 17.8%.** To restore the 2020 real wage of ₹24,000 when the index is 162, the nominal wage must be 24,000 × 162/100 = ₹38,880. Required rise over ₹33,000 = 5,880/33,000 = 17.82% ≈ **17.8%**. Trap (a) 8.0%: that is merely the index rise 150 → 162, which only *preserves* the already-eroded 2024 real wage, not the 2020 one.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (d).** Prompt, formal correction through the proper channel, written intimation to the superior, and a process fix — accurate public data plus institutional integrity, without either concealment (a, b) or an irregular leak (c).

**Q18 — (a).** Need-based prioritisation, documented, with the superior informed — equitable, rule-abiding, and it protects the officer without inviting the delay of (d) or the false even-handedness of (b), which would under-serve the worst-hit village.

**Q19 — (b).** Proportionate first step: counsel privately, explain the rule, correct the post, keep a record, and route media contact through the authorised channel — firm on conduct rules without escalating straight to punishment (a) or humiliating the officer publicly (d).

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (b) 266.** Among 1 to 999, by inclusion–exclusion: ⌊999/2⌋ + ⌊999/3⌋ + ⌊999/5⌋ − ⌊999/6⌋ − ⌊999/10⌋ − ⌊999/15⌋ + ⌊999/30⌋ = 499 + 333 + 199 − 166 − 99 − 66 + 33 = 733 divisible by at least one. So 999 − 733 = **266**. Cross-check by density: 999 × (1/2)(2/3)(4/5) = 999 × 4/15 = 266.4, and the fractional part resolves downwards.

**Q21 — (c) 16⅔%.** Take CP = ₹100 per kg (₹0.10 per g). Marked price = 140; after 25% discount, SP = 140 × 0.75 = ₹105 for a billed kilogram. He actually parts with 900 g, which cost him 900 × 0.10 = ₹90. Profit = 105 − 90 = ₹15 on a cost of ₹90 ⇒ 15/90 = **16⅔%**. Trap (a) 5%: comparing SP 105 against ₹100 and ignoring the short weight.

**Q22 — (b) 720.** ALLOCATE = A, L, L, O, C, A, T, E — consonants L, L, C, T (L repeated) and vowels A, A, O, E (A repeated). Arrange the 4 consonants first: 4!/2! = 12 ways. This creates 5 gaps (including the two ends); choose 4 gaps and fill them with the vowels: C(5,4) × 4!/2! = 5 × 12 = 60. Total = 12 × 60 = **720**. (Brute-force enumeration confirms 720.)

## Timing & strategy tip

Your instinct will be to attack the two RC passages first because they carry six marks — resist it for exactly two minutes. Do a 90-second **triage sweep** before you solve anything: mark each of the 22 questions as *do now*, *do later*, or *leave*. Decision-Making (3 questions, no negative marking, ~60 seconds each) and any single-step reasoning item go into *do now*; the multi-clue seating puzzle and the hardest quant item go into *leave* until the end. This costs you 90 seconds and typically saves five minutes, because the real damage in CSAT is never the question you couldn't solve — it's the eight minutes you spent discovering that you couldn't solve it, with four easy marks still unread at the back of the paper.
