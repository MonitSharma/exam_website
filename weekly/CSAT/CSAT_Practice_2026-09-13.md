## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (analytical / administrative)

Every administration that wishes to be judged by results must first decide what to count, and the decision is never innocent. A number chosen as a proxy for a purpose begins life as a description and ends as an instruction. So long as the measure is merely observed, it carries information: the proportion of children who can read a simple paragraph tells us something about schooling because, at the moment it was chosen, reading was one of many things schools happened to produce. Once the measure is attached to promotion, funding or public ranking, the relation inverts. The measure no longer reports on the activity; the activity reorganises itself around the measure. Teaching narrows to what the test samples, and the figure rises while the thing it stood for does not.

It is tempting to read this as a story about dishonesty, and dishonesty is certainly available - registers are massaged, the weakest candidates are kept from appearing. But the more serious effect requires no deceit at all. A hospital judged on waiting times will, in perfect good faith, treat the easy cases first; a police force judged on cases disposed will, in perfect good faith, prefer the complaint that closes quickly. Each of these is a reasonable response by a conscientious official to the signal actually being sent. The distortion is produced by the incentive, not by the character of the person subject to it.

The usual remedies compound the problem. More indicators are added, which multiplies the surfaces available for gaming and buries the few numbers that mattered; audits are intensified, which improves the accuracy of the figures without touching what the figures have done to behaviour. What is seldom tried is the modest alternative: to keep some measures deliberately unattached to consequence, to be sampled rather than reported, so that at least one instrument in the system is still telling the administration what is happening rather than what it wishes to hear.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(d) The number of performance indicators used in administration should be increased so that no single indicator can dominate an official's incentives.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

Tolerance is praised so widely that it has become difficult to see how strange a virtue it is. To tolerate something is not to approve of it; if I approve, there is nothing to tolerate, and describing my attitude as tolerance is a small insult to both of us. Nor is it simply to ignore a thing, since a man who notices nothing tolerates nothing. Tolerance has a precise shape: it requires that I object to what you do, that I have some power to interfere with it, and that I nevertheless refrain. Remove any of the three and the virtue disappears - an objection without power is mere disapproval, power without objection is indifference, and objection with power exercised is simply suppression.

This structure explains why tolerance is uncomfortable, and why the discomfort is not a defect to be engineered away. The tolerant person is not the one for whom disagreement has ceased to matter; he is the one for whom it continues to matter and who declines to settle it by force. That is a standing tension, and it is what gives the restraint its value. The popular counterfeit, in which we are urged to regard all views as equally sound, dissolves the tension by dissolving the objection, and so produces not tolerance but a kind of cheerful vacancy that cannot be relied upon when a real conviction is at stake.

It also explains the limit. Since tolerance is exercised by whoever holds the power to interfere, it is always in part a relation of the strong to the weak, and a society in which minorities are merely tolerated has not yet given them anything they can claim as of right. Toleration is therefore best understood as a stage rather than a destination - an honourable arrangement between people who disagree, which a settled polity eventually converts into equal standing, at which point the word itself falls quietly out of use.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(c) Tolerance requires us to accept that all opinions are equally likely to be sound.
(d) A society grows more tolerant in proportion as its members cease to care about the differences between them.

# ANSWERS AND EXPLANATIONS

## Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (c).** The passage moves from 'description becomes instruction' to the failure of the two usual remedies and closes on the 'modest alternative' of unattached, sampled measures - both halves are in (c). (b) is the reading the second paragraph explicitly sets aside ('the more serious effect requires no deceit at all'); (d) is one of the remedies the passage says compounds the problem; (a) is never argued.

**Q2 — (d).** The passage's own example: once the measure carries consequences, 'teaching narrows to what the test samples, and the figure rises while the thing it stood for does not.' (c) is the trap - the author insists the serious effect 'requires no deceit at all'; (b) is the remedy he says multiplies the surfaces available for gaming.

**Q3 — (b).** Negation test: if measuring an activity distorted it regardless of what was attached to the measure, then detaching consequences would rescue nothing and the recommendation collapses. (a) contradicts the proposal (it assumes unattached figures can be trusted); (c) is about cost, which the passage never argues; (d) would undermine the whole project of proxies, not support this remedy.

**Q4 — (a).** The passage gives a three-condition definition, defends the tension it creates, and then names its limit - toleration as 'a stage rather than a destination'. (c) and (d) are the 'popular counterfeit' the second paragraph rejects; (b) inverts the argument, since indifference is precisely what the author distinguishes tolerance from.

**Q5 — (d).** Tolerance requires an objection; the passage states that 'power without objection is indifference'. The man described has power and no objection, so the virtue does not arise. (b) adds a claim about contempt that the passage never makes.

**Q6 — (b).** He calls the restraint valuable and the arrangement 'honourable', yet insists it is 'a stage rather than a destination'. (c) is the trap - he notes the strong/weak relation but does not treat tolerance as a mere disguise; (a) overstates and (d) ignores the clear evaluation in the final paragraph.

## Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (c).** Facing the centre, a person's left is the clockwise direction. Fix A and place seats clockwise 1 to 8 with A at 1. From (i), C is at 4. From (ii), E is two seats anticlockwise of C, i.e. at 2. From (iii), B is at 5. From (iv), D is a neighbour of B, i.e. at 6 (seat 4 is C). From (v), G is immediately clockwise of F; the free seats are 3, 7, 8, so F and G occupy 7 and 8, and H takes 3 - consistent with (vi), since H at 3 is not adjacent to A at 1. Final clockwise order: A, E, H, C, B, D, F, G. Second to the left of D (seat 6) is seat 8 = G. Trap (a): F is only the first to D's left.

**Q8 — (a).** First letters: A(1), C(3), F(6), J(10) - differences +2, +3, +4, so the next is 10 + 5 = 15 = O. Second letters: Z(26), X(24), U(21), Q(17) - differences -2, -3, -4, so the next is 17 - 5 = 12 = L. Hence OL. Trap (b) NM keeps the differences constant at +4 and -4.

**Q9 — (d).** Each letter moves forward by its own position in the word: 1st letter +1, 2nd +2, 3rd +3, and so on. Check: D+1=E, E+2=G, L+3=O, H+4=L, I+5=N, giving EGOLN. Applying it to MUMBAI: M+1=N, U+2=W, M+3=P, B+4=F, A+5=F, I+6=O, i.e. NWPFFO. Trap (b) shifts every letter by +1 only; (a) shifts backwards.

**Q10 — (b).** Take east as +x and north as +y. Facing south, a left turn gives east, a further left turn gives north, then a right turn gives east, then a right turn gives south. Displacements: (0, -6), (+8, 0), (0, +11), (+4, 0), (0, -10). Net eastward = 8 + 4 = 12 km; net northward = -6 + 11 - 10 = -5, i.e. 5 km south. Distance = sqrt(12^2 + 5^2) = 13 km, and the finishing point lies east and south of the start, i.e. to the south-east. Trap (c) 17 km simply adds 12 and 5 instead of using the right triangle; trap (a) gets the arithmetic right but the direction wrong.

**Q11 — (c).** Scientists lie inside readers, and readers are wholly disjoint from farmers, so no scientist can be a farmer - I follows. Some farmers are poets; those poets are farmers, and no farmer is a reader, so those poets are not readers - II follows. III fails: those same farmer-poets cannot be scientists, since every scientist is a reader and no reader is a farmer, so not all poets can be scientists. III is the trap, because 'possibility' conclusions are usually safe when no explicit contradiction is visible.

**Q12 — (a).** The conclusion is causal, so it is weakened best by an alternative cause operating at the same time. (a) supplies one: far fewer heavy vehicles on the road would reduce fatalities whether or not cameras existed. (c) is a control comparison that strengthens the conclusion; (d) bears on cost-effectiveness, not causation; (b) is weak - incomplete coverage producing a 30 per cent fall would, if anything, suggest the cameras are potent.

## Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (a).** Rejected units = produced x rate. P: 40,000 x 4% = 1,600. Q: 15,000 x 9% = 1,350. R: 28,000 x 5.5% = 1,540. S: 22,000 x 6% = 1,320. The largest count is P's 1,600, even though P has the lowest rejection rate. Trap (b): Q has much the highest rate but the smallest output.

**Q14 — (d).** Total produced = 40,000 + 15,000 + 28,000 + 22,000 = 1,05,000. Total rejected = 1,600 + 1,350 + 1,540 + 1,320 = 5,810. Rate = 5,810 / 1,05,000 = 5.53%, i.e. about 5.5%. Trap (b) 6.1% is the unweighted mean of the four rates, (4 + 9 + 5.5 + 6)/4 = 6.125 - invalid because the plants differ greatly in size.

**Q15 — (b).** Quantity sold to the trader = 35% of 500 = 175 quintals. Gross price per quintal = Rs 2,400; commission at 6% = Rs 144, leaving Rs 2,256; transport of Rs 40 per quintal leaves Rs 2,216 net per quintal. Net = 175 x 2,216 = Rs 3,87,800. Trap (a) deducts the commission but forgets the transport (175 x 2,256 = 3,94,800); trap (c) is the gross value 175 x 2,400.

**Q16 — (c).** Government centre: 200 quintals x 2,200 = 4,40,000, less 2% = Rs 4,31,200. Trader: Rs 3,87,800 (above). Retail: 125 quintals taken, 4% spoils, so 120 are sold at 2,700 = 3,24,000; costs are Rs 150 on all 125 quintals = 18,750, leaving Rs 3,05,250. ## Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (d).** Profit is shared in the ratio of capital x time. A: 60,000 x 12 = 7,20,000. B: 80,000 x 8 = 6,40,000. C joins six months after the start, so 1,20,000 x 6 = 7,20,000. Ratio = 720 : 640 : 720 = 9 : 8 : 9, total 26 parts. One part = 2,08,000 / 26 = Rs 8,000, so B's share = 8 x 8,000 = Rs 64,000. Trap (c) Rs 69,333 divides the profit into three equal-ish shares by capital alone; trap (a) is A's (and C's) share.

**Q21 — (b).** A number leaving remainder 3 on division by 5, 6 and 8 has the form N = 120k + 3, since LCM(5, 6, 8) = 120. For N to be divisible by 9: 120k + 3 is congruent to 3k + 3 modulo 9, so 3(k + 1) must be a multiple of 9, i.e. k + 1 must be a multiple of 3, giving k = 2 as the least positive choice. Hence N = 243. Check: 243 = 5(48) + 3 = 6(40) + 3 = 8(30) + 3, and 243 = 9 x 27. Trap (a) 123 satisfies the remainder condition (k = 1) but 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 is not a multiple of 9.

**Q22 — (c).** Let the still-water speed be b. Then 45/(b - 3) - 45/(b + 3) = 2. The left side is 45[(b + 3) - (b - 3)]/(b^2 - 9) = 270/(b^2 - 9). So 270 = 2(b^2 - 9), giving b^2 = 144 and b = 12 km/h. Check: upstream 45/9 = 5 hours, downstream 45/15 = 3 hours, a difference of 2 hours.

## Timing and strategy tip

**Do the three Decision-Making questions first, before anything else — they carry no negative marking, they need no computation, and three correct answers there is 7.5 marks banked in under four minutes.** Then take the two RC passages (about 12 minutes), then Reasoning, and leave Quant for last. The single biggest cause of a failed CSAT paper is spending eleven minutes on a hard arrangement puzzle and never reaching a passage that would have taken four. Set a hard stop of 90 seconds per non-RC question: if you are not on a clear path by then, mark it, move, and come back only if the clock allows.
