> A constitution is often imagined as a fence — a list of things the state may not do to those it governs. On this familiar picture, liberty lives in the gaps the fence leaves open, and the citizen is free to the precise extent that the law falls silent. Yet this account, for all its appeal, mistakes the part for the whole. The same document that restrains power also constitutes it: it creates the offices, defines the procedures, and confers the very authority whose excesses it then limits. Without the constitution there would be no lawful power to fence in, only the unstructured force of whoever happened to be strongest.

> Seen this way, the rule of law is not merely a brake but an engine. Its deepest function is not to leave individuals alone but to make collective action possible on terms that do not collapse into the rule of the powerful. A society governed by stable, public, prospective rules can coordinate the plans of millions of strangers who will never meet, precisely because each can rely on the rules holding tomorrow as they held today. A citizen subject to such a power is unfree even if, as it happens, the power is never used against her; for she must live as a supplicant, anticipating a will she cannot foresee. Liberty, on this understanding, is less the silence of the law than the constancy of it.

### Passage II


> We tend to treat forgetting as memory's failure — a leak in a vessel meant to hold everything. The ideal mind, on this view, would forget nothing, recording each face, fact and afternoon in perfect and permanent detail. But a mind that forgot nothing would not be a better mind; it would be a paralysed one. To think at all is to generalise, and to generalise is to let go of particulars. The reader who remembered every individual letter would never arrive at the word; the traveller who retained every blade of grass would never recognise the meadow.

> Forgetting, then, is not the opposite of memory but one of its instruments. By discarding the incidental, the mind clears space for the pattern; by loosening its grip on the exact, it gains the power to recognise the similar. What looks like loss is often a kind of editing — a continual sifting that keeps the useful near the surface and lets the irrelevant sink. Memory that did not forget would not be richer; it would be a featureless archive in which nothing could be found because nothing had been judged worth finding.

> This is why the rare individuals who cannot forget are so often tormented rather than gifted. Buried under the undifferentiated weight of everything that ever happened to them, they struggle to abstract, to compare, to move on. Their predicament suggests that the economy of the mind depends not on retaining the most but on retaining the right things — and that the discipline of forgetting is, paradoxically, a condition of understanding.

### Passage IV


> When a technology for copying words cheaply appeared in the towns of fifteenth-century Europe, its first effect was not the one we now remember. Printing did not immediately enlighten; it multiplied. Whatever was being copied — wisdom or error, devotion or slander — could now travel further and faster than before, and the early presses poured out almanacs and pardons as readily as the works we have since chosen to celebrate. > What the press changed was less the content of thought than its conditions. Once a text could be reproduced identically in a thousand copies, knowledge ceased to be the fragile possession of those who guarded the manuscripts. A claim made in one city could be checked, contested and built upon in another; errors that had quietly propagated through generations of hand-copying could at last be caught by comparison. But it favoured them only as a riverbed favours a river. It set the conditions within which a slow transformation could occur, while leaving to human beings the long labour of actually bringing it about.

> We like to believe that a person can be judged only for what lies within his control. ### Passage VI


> Imagine a pasture open to all, on which each herdsman may graze as many animals as he wishes. For any one herder, the logic is plain: each additional animal he adds yields him its full value, while the cost of the slightly worsened pasture is shared among everyone who uses it. So he adds another, and another — and so, reasoning identically, does every other herder. Each behaves rationally by his own lights; together they destroy the very pasture on which all depend. The ruin is not the work of villains but of ordinary people following incentives that point, individually, in exactly the wrong collective direction.

> What makes such situations hard is that exhortation does not touch their cause. Telling each herder to restrain himself asks him to bear a certain private cost for a benefit that, if others do not also restrain themselves, will not materialise — and that, if others do restrain themselves, he can enjoy without any sacrifice of his own. The conscientious are thus penalised and the free riders rewarded, which is precisely the wrong arrangement of incentives for the outcome we want.

> The way out, where one exists, rarely lies in expecting people to act against their interests. It lies in changing the interests themselves — by assigning rights, setting shared limits, or binding everyone to rules that make restraint individually sensible because others are bound to it too. The problem is not that people are bad but that the structure is, and structures, unlike characters, can be redesigned.

### Passage VII


> The official map of a great city — its zoning plans, its registered businesses, its tax rolls — captures only a fraction of the life actually lived within it. Alongside the formal economy of contracts and licences runs a vast informal one: the street vendor without a permit, the home workshop off the books, the network of unrecorded loans and unwritten tenancies through which much of the city's poor majority actually survives. Planners have often regarded this shadow economy as a problem to be cleared away — a sign of disorder, backwardness, a failure of the city to become fully modern.

> But to treat informality merely as the absence of order is to misread it. The unlicensed market is not the lack of a system; it is a different system, with its own rules, reputations and enforcement, often more responsive to the needs of the poor than the formal channels that exclude them. When the licensed alternative is too costly, too slow or too out of reach for those at the bottom, the informal arrangement is not a deviation from how the city works but a large part of how it works at all. To bulldoze it in the name of order can destroy the only economy the poor can actually participate in, replacing a messy functioning system with a tidy non-functioning one.

> This does not mean informality should be romanticised; it can also trap people in insecurity, without the protections that formal status confers. The point is rather that the choice is not between order and disorder but between two kinds of order — and that policy which fails to see the hidden one will keep mistaking the city's survival mechanisms for its diseases.

### Passage VIII


> A translation is often judged by a single demand: that it be faithful to the original. But faithful to what? A sentence in any living language carries more than its dictionary meaning. It carries rhythm, connotation, the weight of what is customary to say and the surprise of what is not — a whole atmosphere that clings to words in their native setting and evaporates the moment they are lifted out. The translator who renders only the literal sense, matching word to word with mechanical care, produces something accurate and dead: a body without breath. Fidelity to the words can be a betrayal of the meaning.

> So the translator is forced into a kind of double loyalty that can never be perfectly honoured. To carry across the music and the feeling, she must sometimes depart from the letter; to keep faith with the letter, she must sometimes sacrifice the music. There is no formula that resolves this, no setting at which the dial can rest. Every choice gains something and loses something, and the translator's art lies precisely in judging, again and again, which loss is the more bearable.

> This is why translation, far from being a mechanical substitution, is one of the most demanding forms of reading. The translator must understand the original more deeply than an ordinary reader does — must feel not only what it says but how it means — and then perform the near-impossible feat of saying something that is at once another text and the same one. That the task cannot be completed perfectly is not a mark of its failure but of its seriousness: it is hard precisely because there is so much, beyond mere words, to be kept.
