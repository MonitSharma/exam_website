## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3

> Ecologists once pictured nature as a system that, when disturbed, would return gracefully to its former state, like a pendulum settling back to rest. A forest cleared by fire would regrow; a lake muddied by run-off would clear. This image of a single forgiving equilibrium shaped decades of conservation policy. But a growing body of evidence suggests that many ecosystems possess not one stable state but several, and that the passage between them is neither gradual nor reversible. A shallow lake enriched by fertiliser may stay clear for years, absorbing nutrient after nutrient with little visible change — until, at some unremarkable threshold, it flips abruptly into a turbid, algae-choked condition that persists even after the nutrients are withdrawn. The system does not retrace its steps; the path back demands far more than the removal of the original cause.
>
> What makes such thresholds treacherous is precisely their quietness. Resilience — the capacity to absorb disturbance without changing state — erodes invisibly, so that an ecosystem can appear robust while its margin of safety silently narrows. The final push need not be dramatic: a system already close to its tipping point can be toppled by a perturbation it would once have shrugged off. By the time the change announces itself, the cheap moment for intervention has long passed.
>
> The lesson is not that collapse is inevitable but that stability can be deceptive. A landscape that looks healthy may be one poor season from transformation, and the absence of visible decline is no proof of safety. Prudence, then, counsels attention to the loss of resilience itself — the unglamorous work of monitoring what has not yet gone wrong.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Ecosystems always return to their original state after any disturbance.
- (b) Fertiliser run-off is the principal threat facing the world's lakes.
- (c) Many ecosystems can shift abruptly and irreversibly between alternative stable states as their resilience erodes unseen.
- (d) Conservation policy has largely eliminated the risk of ecological collapse.

**Q2.** On the author's view, it can be inferred that an ecosystem which "looks healthy":

- (a) is guaranteed to remain stable for the foreseeable future.
- (b) has necessarily already crossed its tipping point.
- (c) may nonetheless be close to an abrupt transition, since lost resilience is not outwardly visible.
- (d) can always be fully restored merely by removing the original disturbance.

**Q3.** The author's recommendation to monitor "the loss of resilience itself" rests most directly on which assumption?

- (a) Ecosystems possess only one stable state.
- (b) Resilience can, at least in principle, be detected or measured before a tipping point is reached.
- (c) Human intervention is always futile once decline has begun.
- (d) Every disturbance inevitably causes a regime shift.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6

> It is an article of modern faith that freedom expands as our options multiply, and that the freest person is the one before whom the most doors stand open. On this reckoning every additional choice is a small enlargement of liberty, and any constraint a loss to be regretted. Yet the experience of choosing tells a more complicated story. Confronted with a hundred possibilities, the chooser is often not liberated but paralysed, oppressed by the very abundance meant to empower her. Each option carries the shadow of the others foregone, so that satisfaction curdles into the suspicion that some unchosen path was better. Freedom, pursued without limit, can consume itself.
>
> The artist knows this in her bones. It is not the blank, unbounded canvas that calls forth her best work but the chosen frame — the sonnet's fourteen lines, the sculptor's resistant marble. Constraint, far from stifling creation, supplies the friction against which the imagination gains purchase. Remove every limit and you do not free the maker; you abandon her in a void where nothing has weight because nothing is at stake.
>
> This suggests that liberty is not simply the absence of restriction but the presence of meaningful limits we can make our own. The person who commits — to a vocation, a discipline, a single faithful relationship — forecloses a thousand possibilities and, in doing so, acquires a self that the perpetual keeper of options never attains. To choose everything is, in the end, to choose nothing; and the deepest freedom may lie not in keeping every door ajar but in the courage to close most of them.

**Q4.** The passage is primarily concerned with arguing that:

- (a) meaningful constraints can be the condition of genuine agency and achievement, not merely a loss of freedom.
- (b) freedom increases without limit as the number of available options grows.
- (c) artists produce their best work only when given unlimited resources.
- (d) all commitments are ultimately regrettable losses of liberty.

**Q5.** The claim that "to choose everything is, in the end, to choose nothing" relies most directly on the assumption that:

- (a) people are incapable of making any decisions at all.
- (b) having more options never causes any anxiety.
- (c) a meaningful self or achievement is formed partly through the foreclosing of alternatives.
- (d) artistic creation requires no skill, only constraint.

**Q6.** The author's attitude toward the belief that "the freest person is the one before whom the most doors stand open" is best described as:

- (a) wholehearted endorsement.
- (b) skeptical — treating it as a half-truth that ultimately misleads.
- (c) indifferent.
- (d) bitterly contemptuous.

### Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (c).** The whole passage builds to the idea of multiple stable states with abrupt, irreversible flips driven by quietly eroding resilience. (a) and (d) are the views being rejected; (b) is one illustration, not the thesis.

**Q2 — (c).** The text says resilience "erodes invisibly" and "the absence of visible decline is no proof of safety" — so apparent health is compatible with nearness to a tipping point. (a)/(d) overstate safety; (b) overstates the danger.

**Q3 — (b).** Negation test: if resilience could *not* be detected before the threshold, "monitoring what has not yet gone wrong" would be impossible and the advice collapses — so the argument assumes it can be detected. (a) and (d) contradict the passage; (c) is too strong.

**Q4 — (a).** The passage defends constraint as the condition of agency and creation ("liberty is… the presence of meaningful limits we can make our own"). (b) is the opening belief it undercuts; (c)/(d) are distortions.

**Q5 — (c).** The claim only works if foreclosing alternatives is part of acquiring a self/achievement; negate that and "choosing everything = nothing" no longer follows. The others are unrelated or caricatures.

**Q6 — (b).** The author calls the belief an "article of modern faith" and shows it consuming itself — a skeptical, corrective stance, not endorsement (a), indifference (c), or contempt (d).

### Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (c) E.** A=4; C=7; B immediately below C = 6; "two floors between A and D" with D below ⇒ D=1 (floors 2,3 lie between). Free floors {2,3,5} go to E, F, G with E immediately above F: only (E=3, F=2) fits, so G=5 (and G≠2 ✓). Floor 3, immediately below A(4), is **E**. Full stack (1→7): D, F, E, A, G, B, C.

**Q8 — (c) A.** "A taller than B, shorter than C" ⇒ C > A > B; "D taller than C" ⇒ D on top; "E shorter than B" ⇒ E at the bottom. Order: **D > C > A > B > E**. Third tallest = **A**.

**Q9 — (d) nve.** Compare overlaps: sentences 1 and 2 share only the word "red," and share only the code **sa** ⇒ red = sa. Sentences 2 and 3 share only "mangoes" and only **be** ⇒ mangoes = be. In "red ripe mangoes = sa nve be," removing red (sa) and mangoes (be) leaves **ripe = nve**.

**Q10 — (b) Mother.** "The only daughter of my father" = the speaker herself. So "his mother is the speaker" ⇒ the woman is the man's **mother**.

**Q11 — (b) V.** Alphabet positions 2, 4, 7, 11, 16 rise by +2, +3, +4, +5; next gap +6 ⇒ 16 + 6 = 22 = **V**.

**Q12 — (c) Either I or II.** From "all singers are dancers" + "some dancers are poets" you cannot tell whether any singer is a poet. Conclusion I ("some singers are poets") and II ("no singer is a poet") are exact contradictories, so **exactly one must be true** — neither follows alone, but together they form a complementary (either-or) pair.

### Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (b) 875.** Mon 120; Tue 120 × 1.25 = 150; Wed 150 − 20 = 130; Thu 120 + 130 = 250; Fri 250 × 0.9 = 225. Total = 120 + 150 + 130 + 250 + 225 = **875**.

**Q14 — (c) 90%.** Friday ÷ Thursday = 225 ÷ 250 = **90%**. Trap (a) 10% is the *decrease*, not Friday's share of Thursday.

**Q15 — (a) 2024.** Percent profit on expenditure: 2023 = 30/50 = 60%; 2024 = 40/60 = 66.7%; 2025 = 30/90 = 33.3%. Highest is **2024**. Trap: 2025 has the largest income but the *lowest* percent profit.

**Q16 — (b) 25% less.** Profit 2024 = 40, 2025 = 30. Change = (30 − 40)/40 × 100 = **−25%**, i.e. 25% less. Trap (a) 10% mistakes the absolute fall of 10 crore for a percentage.

### Decision-Making

**Q17 — (a).** Verify quietly, raise it privately first, escalate through proper channels only if unexplained — protects integrity and due process without public humiliation or collusion.

**Q18 — (b).** A joint, neutral conversation that refocuses on the shared goal is fair and constructive; taking sides, ignoring it, or permanently separating them all fail the team.

**Q19 — (c).** Uphold the mandated check while actively helping meet the deadline — the rule-abiding *and* solution-oriented response. Outright reporting (a) is disproportionate; (b) and (d) abandon the rule.

### Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (c) 9 days.** Rates: A = 1/12, B = 1/18. B works all T days, A works (T − 3). (T − 3)/12 + T/18 = 1 ⇒ ×36: 3(T − 3) + 2T = 36 ⇒ 5T = 45 ⇒ **T = 9**. Check: A 6/12 + B 9/18 = ½ + ½ = 1 ✓. Trap (a) 7.2 = both working together throughout (12·18/30).

**Q21 — (c) 12 litres.** In 60 L at 7 : 3, milk = 42, water = 18. Milk is unchanged; for 7 : 5, water must be 42 × 5/7 = 30. Add 30 − 18 = **12 litres**. Check: 42 : 30 = 7 : 5 ✓.

**Q22 — (b) 130°.** Hour hand at 3:40 = 3 × 30 + 40 × 0.5 = 110°; minute hand = 40 × 6 = 240°. Difference = 240 − 110 = **130°**. (Formula |30H − 5.5M| = |90 − 220| = 130°.)

## Timing & strategy tip

**Open with the either-or syllogism mindset, but bank the arrangement and DI first.** This week's two "hardest-looking" items — the floors puzzle (Q7) and the either-or syllogism (Q12) — are actually deterministic once you commit the fixed clues to a quick sketch, so they reward your structured thinking with certain marks; do them before the RC. For the RC, run a strict **75 seconds per question, two passes**: on the first pass lock in the main-idea and tone questions (fast), and *defer* the two assumption questions (Q3, Q5) to a second pass, since the negation test takes a beat longer. If any single item hasn't given you a foothold in ~90 seconds, mark it and move — one stuck question quietly costs you three easy marks elsewhere.
