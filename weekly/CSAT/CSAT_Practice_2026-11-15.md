## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3

> When a technology makes the use of a resource more efficient, it seems obvious that total consumption of that resource must fall: a car that travels farther on a litre of fuel should burn less fuel. The nineteenth-century economist William Stanley Jevons noticed that the reverse had happened with coal. Steam engines that used less coal per unit of work did not shrink the nation's coal bill; they made steam power cheaper, and cheaper power found new uses, so that total demand rose. Modern economists call the general phenomenon the rebound effect, and gauge its strength by how much of the engineering saving is clawed back through changed behaviour. A household that fits efficient bulbs may leave lights on longer; a firm whose logistics software saves fuel may widen its delivery area. When the clawback is partial, efficiency still lowers consumption, only by less than the engineering estimate. When it exceeds the whole saving, as in the case Jevons described, consumption rises, and economists call this backfire.
>
> It would be wrong to infer that efficiency is futile. Empirical studies suggest that rebounds in mature, saturated uses, such as household refrigeration, are small, since nobody runs a second fridge merely because the first became cheaper to operate. Backfire is likelier where demand is unsatisfied and the resource is a major input to a growing activity. The policy lesson is therefore not to abandon efficiency standards but to stop treating engineering estimates as forecasts. Where rebounds are large, efficiency gains should be paired with instruments, such as a tax or a cap on the resource itself, that keep its price from falling in step with the technology. Efficiency lowers the cost of using a resource; only a price or quantity constraint on the resource itself can ensure that lower costs translate into lower use.

**Q1.** Which one of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Efficiency standards are futile because every efficiency gain is fully cancelled by increased consumption.
- (b) Jevons's finding about coal holds for every resource, so engineering estimates of savings are always wrong.
- (c) Efficiency gains do not by themselves guarantee lower consumption because cheaper use induces more use, so where rebounds are large they should be paired with a price or quantity constraint on the resource.
- (d) Rebound effects are negligible in modern economies, so policy should focus on engineering improvements alone.

**Q2.** A government projects that a new generation of efficient air-conditioners will cut a hot, low-income country's electricity use by exactly the amount engineers calculate. In the light of the passage, this projection is

- (a) likely to overstate the saving, because many households that could not afford cooling may now use it, so demand is unsatisfied and the rebound could be large.
- (b) likely to understate the saving, because efficiency always reduces consumption by more than engineers estimate.
- (c) reliable, because engineering estimates are the best available forecast of actual consumption.
- (d) irrelevant, because air-conditioning is a luxury and luxuries are never subject to rebound effects.

**Q3.** The author's recommendation of 'a tax or a cap on the resource itself' rests on which one of the following assumptions?

- (a) Users are completely insensitive to the price of the resource they consume.
- (b) Governments can measure engineering savings more accurately than economists can estimate rebounds.
- (c) Every efficiency improvement, in every sector, leads to backfire.
- (d) If the price of the resource does not fall, users will not expand their use of it simply because the technology has become more efficient.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6

> There is a well-known story of an empire whose cartographers, dissatisfied with maps that left things out, drew one on the scale of one to one. The map was perfectly accurate and entirely useless, and later generations left it to rot in the desert. The moral usually drawn is that a representation must simplify to be of any use. But the moral can be pressed further. A map is useful not in spite of its omissions but because of them: it omits precisely what a particular traveller does not need, and the skill of the mapmaker lies in judging what that is. A road map that marked every pebble would hide the roads; a geological map that marked only roads would hide the rock.
>
> It follows that no map can be assessed for accuracy in the abstract. To ask whether a map is true is to ask a question that has no answer until one adds: true for what purpose? The same is plausibly so of the models by which the sciences and social disciplines represent their subjects. A model that assumes frictionless motion is not a lie about a ball rolling on a floor; it is a deliberate idealisation that serves the question being asked, and it fails only when pressed to answer a question that the omitted friction decides. The danger is not simplification but forgetfulness: users come to treat the map as the territory, and are then surprised when the ground refuses to behave as the drawing promised. A discipline that remembers what its models leave out is entitled to its idealisations; one that forgets is merely mistaken in a more sophisticated vocabulary.

**Q4.** Which one of the following best expresses the central idea of the passage?

- (a) Representations are valuable because they simplify purposefully, so their accuracy can be judged only relative to a purpose; the real danger is forgetting what has been left out.
- (b) A map or model is trustworthy only if it includes every detail of what it represents.
- (c) The story of the one-to-one map shows that scientific models are essentially false and should be abandoned.
- (d) Cartographers, unlike scientists, are entitled to idealise because their audience is non-specialist.

**Q5.** Which one of the following practices would the author be most likely to regard as unobjectionable?

- (a) An engineer rejects a model outright because it leaves out some real features of the system.
- (b) An economist uses a growth model that ignores financial frictions to study long-run trends, states the omission openly, and declines to use it to forecast a banking crisis.
- (c) A planner uses a model built on average rainfall to predict a flood year, having forgotten what averaging leaves out.
- (d) A cartographer insists that the only truly accurate map is one that includes everything on the ground.

**Q6.** The author's attitude to the criticism that scientific models are 'false' because they omit real features is best described as

- (a) wholly sympathetic, since a model that omits anything cannot be called true.
- (b) hostile to all modelling, regarding it as a sophisticated form of error.
- (c) unpersuaded, since the criticism mistakes purposeful omission for falsehood, though the author concedes that users' forgetfulness is a real danger.
- (d) indifferent, treating the matter as a dispute over vocabulary.

## Answer Key & Explanations

**Q1. (c)** — The passage says rebound is real but varies (small for saturated uses, large for unsatisfied ones) and recommends pairing efficiency with price/quantity limits where rebound is large. The other options are extreme (futile / always / negligible).

**Q2. (a)** — Per the passage, backfire/large rebound is likelier where demand is unsatisfied and use is growing; engineering estimates should not be treated as forecasts.

**Q3. (d)** — The remedy works only if keeping the resource's price from falling holds back the extra use that cheaper operation would invite - i.e., use responds to price. Option (a) would make the tax useless; (c) contradicts the passage's own refrigeration example.

**Q4. (a)** — The passage argues omission is the source of a map's usefulness, accuracy is purpose-relative, and 'forgetfulness' (map mistaken for territory) is the danger.

**Q5. (b)** — The author licenses idealisations by those who remember what the model omits and do not press it on questions the omission decides. Options b, c and d each commit the error criticised (rejecting simplification, forgetfulness, or demanding the 1:1 map).

**Q6. (c)** — The ball-on-a-floor example says frictionless models are 'not a lie'; but the author still warns against treating the map as the territory - a measured rejection, not hostility or indifference.

**Q7. (b)** — R is in Pune so R is not the lawyer (Delhi). P is not the doctor, so P is lawyer or engineer. If P were the engineer, P cannot be in Patna, Kochi (clue ii) or Pune (R) so P would be in Delhi, but Delhi holds the lawyer - contradiction. So P = lawyer, Delhi. R and S are doctor/engineer; the doctor lives in Patna and R is in Pune, so S = doctor (Patna), R = engineer (Pune). Q (teacher) gets the remaining city, Kochi. Doctor in Patna, teacher in Kochi = (b). (Trap: option reversing the order.)

**Q8. (c)** — In the morning the sun is in the east, so shadows fall towards the west. Ravi's shadow is on his right, so his right hand points west, which means Ravi faces North. Sunil stands back to back with Ravi, so he faces the opposite direction: South.

**Q9. (a)** — 'Only thinkers are reformers' = All reformers are thinkers, so I follows. Some reformers are teachers and all reformers are thinkers, so those teachers are thinkers: II follows. Poets and reformers are both subsets of thinkers with no stated overlap, so III is only a possibility, not a certainty.

**Q10. (d)** — Each letter is replaced by its mirror in the alphabet (position p -> 27-p): B(2)->Y(25), L(12)->O(15), U(21)->F(6), E(5)->V(22) confirms it. S(19)->8=H, K(11)->16=P, Y(25)->2=B. SKY = HPB.

**Q11. (b)** — B is C's mother; C is E's father. So B is E's father's mother = paternal grandmother. (Maternal grandmother would need B to be D's mother.)

**Q12. (d)** — Strengthening means ruling out other explanations and adding specificity. A comparison group with no fall, plus the fall concentrated where lanes exist, supports causation. (b) is neutral/ambiguous; (c) is irrelevant; (d) offers rival causes, so it weakens.

**Q13. (c)** — Profit = revenue x margin: North 230x0.10 = 23.0; South 195x0.12 = 23.4; East 90x0.20 = 18.0; West 275x0.08 = 22.0 (₹ crore). South is highest. (Traps: East has the highest margin but low revenue; West has the largest revenue but the lowest margin.)

**Q14. (a)** — Total profit = 23.0+23.4+18.0+22.0 = ₹86.4 crore; overall margin = 86.4/790 = 10.94% ~ 10.9%. Trap: the simple average of the four margins, (10+12+20+8)/4 = 12.5%, ignores that regions have different revenues.

**Q15. (b)** — Yields 2020 -> 2025: A 2.0->2.4; B 2.2->2.0; C 2.5->3.0; D 1.8->1.6. B and D fell. Trap: production rose in B and D, but area rose faster.

**Q16. (c)** — Combined production: 2020 = 40+33+25+54 = 152; 2025 = 60+36+30+64 = 190. Increase = 38; 38/152 = 25%. Trap: 38 is the absolute increase in lakh tonnes, not a percentage.

**Q17. (b)** — Best answer: need-based, transparent and documented allocation, with respectful communication and a plan for the shortfall. Yielding is partisan; ignoring the representative is discourteous and opaque; an equal split ignores need.

**Q18. (c)** — Balanced and rule-abiding: stop the harm quickly, give the colleague a chance to correct, and use the formal channel for accountability. Ignoring tolerates harm; social media and public shaming are disproportionate and unprofessional.

**Q19. (a)** — Conflicts of interest are handled by disclosure and recusal, not by private judgement of one's own fairness. Silent participation, secret score manipulation, or back-channel pressure all compromise integrity and transparency.

**Q20. (b)** — Milk left = 60 x (1 - 12/60)^2 = 60 x 0.64 = 38.4 L. Water = 60 - 38.4 = 21.6 L. Ratio = 38.4 : 21.6 = 16 : 9. (4:1 is the ratio after only one replacement.)

**Q21. (d)** — Take total work = 36 units. Rates: A 3, B 2, C 1 units/day. First 4 days at A+B = 5/day: 20 units. Remaining 16 units at B+C = 3/day: 16/3 = 5 1/3 days. Total = 4 + 5 1/3 = 9 1/3 days.

**Q22. (c)** — Total ways C(9,3) = 84. Exactly two red: C(5,2) x C(4,1) = 40. Three red: C(5,3) = 10. Favourable = 50; 50/84 = 25/42. (10/21 = 40/84 counts exactly two red only.)
