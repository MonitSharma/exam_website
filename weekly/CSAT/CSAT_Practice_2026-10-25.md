## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3

> Economists evaluating climate policy must decide how much a benefit arriving a century from now is worth today. The standard technique is discounting: future gains are scaled down by a rate that compounds with distance in time. At a discount rate of 5 per cent, damage of a hundred rupees avoided in a century is worth under one rupee today; at 1.4 per cent, it is worth about twenty-five. The difference is not a technical detail: it determines whether stringent abatement looks like prudent investment or reckless sacrifice. Defenders of high rates argue from observation. Markets reveal that people prefer present consumption, and policy, they say, should not second-guess revealed preferences. Besides, if future generations are likely to be richer, asking the poor of today to pay for the comfort of the affluent of tomorrow seems perverse. Critics reply that the market rate reflects the impatience of individuals within a single lifetime, which says little about how a society should weigh persons who have not yet been born and cannot bid in any market. Pure time preference, they argue, is discrimination by birth date. Yet the critics' own position is not without difficulty: if the rate is set near zero, almost any present sacrifice is justified by sufficiently distant benefits, and the argument proves too much. Perhaps the wiser course is to concede that the rate is an ethical judgment dressed as a market fact, and to make that judgment openly, with the range of alternatives displayed, rather than let it hide inside a spreadsheet cell.

**Q1.** Which one of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

- (b) Both high and near-zero discount rates face serious objections, so the rate should be recognised as an ethical choice and made openly.
- (c) Future generations will be richer, so present sacrifices for climate abatement are unjustified.
- (d) Economists should abandon cost-benefit analysis of climate policy because the future cannot be valued.

**Q2.** Which one of the following practices would the author be most likely to endorse?

- (a) A cost-benefit report on a coastal-protection project that uses the prevailing market rate and treats it as a technical input.
- (b) A report that uses a zero discount rate so that future generations are not discriminated against.
- (c) A decision to drop cost-benefit analysis entirely and rely on political preference.
- (d) A report that shows results at several discount rates, say 1.5, 3 and 5 per cent, and explains the ethical reasoning behind each.

**Q3.** The author says that a near-zero rate 'proves too much'. This criticism assumes that:

- (a) a policy rule that would justify almost any present sacrifice is, for that reason, unacceptable.
- (b) distant benefits are always smaller than present costs.
- (c) markets can accurately price the welfare of unborn generations.
- (d) economists are unable to estimate benefits a century away.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6

> It is often said that poetry is what is lost in translation, and the saying is usually taken as a lament. But the lament presupposes that a work possesses a fixed essence which one language holds and another fails to carry across, as though meaning were a liquid poured between vessels of different shape, some of it spilled in the transfer. This picture flatters the original and slights the translator. A language is not a container but a way of dividing up experience, and the divisions are not identical from one tongue to another: what one language marks with a single word another may distribute across several, and what one treats as a grammatical necessity another leaves to the listener's tact. To translate is therefore never simply to relocate; it is to decide, repeatedly, which of the original's commitments shall be honoured and which sacrificed. The faithful translator is not the one who loses least but the one whose losses are most intelligently chosen. Nor is the original left untouched by the exchange. A work read in a second language is read by new eyes, and its neglected features, such as an ambiguity, a rhythm or a silence, may become visible for the first time, even to speakers of its own tongue. Untranslatability, on this view, is not a wall but a measure of how much a language has to teach another. We should be wary of those who celebrate it as proof of the sacredness of their own idiom; they have mistaken a difficulty for a virtue.

- (c) Translation involves choosing what to preserve and what to give up, and can even reveal new features of the original, so untranslatability is a source of learning rather than of pride or lament.
- (d) Languages divide experience in identical ways, so translation is largely mechanical.

# Answers & Worked Methods

**Q1 — (b)** · *Main idea - discounting and ethical judgment in climate economics* · Moderate  
(b) The author weighs both camps, shows each has a flaw, and concludes the rate is an ethical judgment to be made openly. (a) is the defenders' view, which the author does not endorse; (c) is one argument reported, not the thesis; (d) is never proposed.

**Q2 — (d)** · *Inference - applying the argument to a new case* · Moderate  
(d) matches the closing recommendation: display the range of alternatives and state the judgment openly. (a) hides the judgment in a market figure, (b) falls to the 'proves too much' objection, (c) is not suggested.

**Q3 — (a)** · *Assumption - 'proves too much' argument* · Hard  
Negation test: if a rule justifying nearly any sacrifice were acceptable, the 'proves too much' objection would carry no force. So (a) is the necessary assumption. (b), (c), (d) are not required by the objection.

**Q4 — (c)** · *Main idea - translation as interpretive choice* · Moderate  
(c) combines the three moves of the passage: translation as choice, enrichment of the original, and untranslatability as a lesson. (a) and (d) contradict it; (b) contradicts 'not the one who loses least'.

**Q5 — (b)** · *Inference - author's likely judgement on a case* · Moderate  
The author defines faithfulness as 'losses most intelligently chosen', not as zero loss. So (b). (d) is wrong because something (rhyme) was lost.

**Q6 — (d)** · *Tone and attitude - towards celebrants of untranslatability* · Moderate  
'We should be wary...they have mistaken a difficulty for a virtue' is a measured warning with mild criticism. (c) is too extreme; (a) and (b) contradict the text.

**Q7 — (c)** · *Circular seating - opposite and adjacent constraints* · Moderate  
Place A at position 0 and D at 3 (opposite). C is between B and D, so C is adjacent to D (position 2 or 4) and B is on the far side of C (1 or 5 respectively). F, adjacent to A, takes the other of 1/5, leaving E at the last seat. Case C=2: B=1, F=5, E=4, and the seat opposite C is 5 = F. Case C=4: B=5, F=1, E=2, and the seat opposite C (position 1) is F. Either way F. (The two cases are mirror images.) Answer: F.

**Q8 — (a)** · *Coding-decoding - position-dependent letter shift* · Moderate  
PLAN to QNDR: P+1=Q, L+2=N, A+3=D, N+4=R, so the shifts are +1, +2, +3, +4... DRAIN: D+1=E, R+2=T, A+3=D, I+4=M, N+5=S, giving ETDMS.

**Q9 — (b)** · *Syllogism - valid conclusions from statements* · Moderate  
I: some engineers are artists and all artists are dreamers, so some engineers are dreamers - follows. II: artists are inside dreamers and no dreamer is a cynic, so no artist is a cynic - follows. III: the converse of 'All artists are dreamers' does not hold - does not follow. IV: those engineers who are dreamers are not cynics - follows. Answer: I, II and IV only.

**Q10 — (d)** · *Knights and knaves - truth-teller / liar puzzle* · Hard  
Case 1: P truthful, so Q is a liar. Q's statement is then false, so P and R differ, so R is a liar. Consistent (P T, Q L, R L). Case 2: P is a liar, so Q is truthful. Q's statement is true, so P and R are the same type, so R is a liar. Consistent (P L, Q T, R L). Both cases are valid, but R is a liar in both, while P and Q differ between cases. Answer: R.

**Q11 — (c)** · *Series - second-order differences* · Moderate  
Differences: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, which are the squares 1², 2², 3², 4², 5². The next difference is 6² = 36, so the next term is 58 + 36 = 94.

**Q12 — (a)** · *Blood relations - pointing to a photograph* · Moderate  
Meera's father's father is her paternal grandfather. His only daughter is Meera's father's sister (Meera's paternal aunt). The man is the aunt's son, so he is Meera's (male) cousin.

**Q13 — (d)** · *Table - percentage vs absolute increase (trap)* · Moderate  
Percentage increase: Education 600/2400 = 25%; Health 900/1500 = 60%; Agriculture 360/1800 = 20%; Infrastructure 300/3000 = 10%; Others 840/1300 = 64.6%. Highest = Others. Trap: Health has the largest absolute increase (900) but not the largest percentage.

**Q14 — (b)** · *Table - compound annual growth of the total* · Hard  
Total went from 10,000 to 13,000, a factor of 1.3 over two years. Annual factor = sqrt(1.3) = 1.140, i.e. about 14%. Traps: 30% is the total two-year growth and 15% is the naive 30/2; (1.15)² = 1.3225 overshoots 1.3.

**Q15 — (c)** · *Table - weighted overall percentage (trap)* · Moderate  
Passed: Physics 300x0.70 = 210; Chemistry 200x0.80 = 160; Mathematics 200x0.95 = 190; Statistics 300x0.60 = 180. Total passed = 740 out of 1000 = 74%. Trap: the simple average of the four pass rates is (70+80+95+60)/4 = 76.25%, which ignores the different class sizes.

**Q16 — (a)** · *Table - share of failures* · Hard  
Failures: Physics 90, Chemistry 40, Mathematics 10, Statistics 120. Total failures = 260. Statistics share = 120/260 = 46.2%, about 46%. Trap: 40% is the failure rate within Statistics (120/300), not the share of all failures.

**Q17 — (c)** · *Integrity - quality shortfall versus pressure for payment* · Moderate  
(c) is balanced and rule-abiding: it protects public money and quality, is fair to the contractor for compliant work, and addresses the workers' concern. (a) and (d) compromise on quality; (b) is disproportionate without due process.

**Q18 — (b)** · *Handling a protest - blockade versus legitimate grievance* · Moderate  
(b) is empathetic yet lawful: it handles the emergency, opens dialogue, and keeps force as a last resort. (a) is excessive, (c) is insensitive, (d) bypasses procedure and sets a bad precedent.

**Q19 — (d)** · *Transparency - RTI request on an embarrassing matter* · Moderate  
(d) follows the law and accountability while keeping the superior informed. (a), (b) and (c) obstruct the right to information and are improper.

**Q20 — (a)** · *Number theory - remainder using cyclicity* · Moderate  
17 leaves remainder 4 mod 13. Powers of 4 mod 13: 4, 3, 12, 9, 10, 1, so the cycle length is 6. 2026 = 6x337 + 4, so 4^2026 = 4^4 = 256, and 256 mod 13 = 256 - 247 = 9.

**Q21 — (b)** · *Mixtures - successive replacement* · Hard  
After two replacements milk = 80x(4/5)² = 51.2 L (water 28.8 L, ratio 16:9 - the trap). Removing 20 L of mixture removes 20x(51.2/80) = 12.8 L of milk, leaving 38.4 L; adding 20 L of milk gives 58.4 L milk and 21.6 L water. Ratio = 58.4 : 21.6 = 73 : 27.

**Q22 — (c)** · *Boats and streams - changed current* · Hard  
Original: 60/(10+s) + 60/(10-s) = 1200/(100-s²) = 12.5, so 100 - s² = 96 and s = 2 km/h. Doubled: s = 4 km/h, so time = 60/14 + 60/6 = 4.286 + 10 = 14.286 h = 100/7 h, about 14 h 17 min.

## Timing / Strategy Tip

Triage by time, not by section: give any puzzle or quant item a hard ceiling of about 90 seconds, mark it and move on, and spend the saved minutes on RC and DI, where careful reading converts reliably. Since CSAT only qualifies you (about 66 of 200), banking 27 to 28 solid answers matters more than finishing all 22 - and with a 1/3 penalty, skip any item where you cannot eliminate at least two options (but always attempt Decision-Making).
