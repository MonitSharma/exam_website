## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (economic / analytical)

> Housing debates in growing cities are conducted almost entirely in the language of shortage, and this choice of word does much of the damage. A shortage is what occurs when a price is prevented from rising: queues form, waiting lists lengthen, and the remedy is obvious enough — let the price move, or supply more of the thing. Urban housing is not, for the most part, in shortage in this sense. Prices are free to rise and they do rise, spectacularly, which is precisely the complaint. What the city confronts is a different condition: the supply of dwellings responds feebly, or not at all, to the signal that those prices send.
>
> The reason is that a dwelling is a bundle of two goods sold as one. The structure is a manufactured object, reproducible at roughly constant cost anywhere; the location is not reproducible at all. When a city's rents climb, the climb is almost entirely in the second component. Builders would happily add structures — that is their trade — but the quantity of structure permitted on a given plot is fixed in advance by rules that a previous generation of residents wrote, and those rules are not indexed to demand. The market signal arrives at a door that has been bolted from the inside.
>
> This has an uncomfortable implication for the standard remedies. Subsidies to buyers, cheap mortgages, tax relief on interest — each raises what households can pay without altering what may be built, and so is absorbed into the price of land. The subsidy is real, but its beneficiary is not the household; it is whoever owned the plot on the morning the policy was announced. Nor is it enough to observe that new construction is expensive. Where the binding constraint is a permission rather than a resource, cost is a symptom. The city is not running out of concrete. It is running out of consent.

**Q1.** Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?

(a) Urban housing prices rise because construction costs have risen faster than incomes in growing cities.
(b) Because dwellings combine a reproducible structure with a non-reproducible location, and building rights are fixed independently of demand, high urban prices reflect a supply that cannot respond rather than a classical shortage.
(c) Housing subsidies are ineffective because households misuse them, and should therefore be replaced by direct public construction.
(d) Cities should abandon the vocabulary of shortage because economic terminology confuses the public and delays reform.

**Q2.** It can be inferred that the author would regard a new scheme granting first-time buyers an interest subsidy, introduced in a city whose building rules are left unchanged, as:

(c) misguided, because first-time buyers are not the group most burdened by high rents.
(d) beneficial mainly to landowners, since the additional purchasing power would be absorbed into land prices rather than into more dwellings.

**Q3.** The author's closing claim that the city "is running out of consent" rests most directly on the assumption that:

(a) residents who wrote the existing rules were indifferent to the welfare of future arrivals.
(b) the materials and labour required for additional construction remain available at costs that do not themselves choke off supply.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

> There is a temptation, when a question is difficult and an expert stands nearby, to treat deference as a form of humility and independent judgement as a form of vanity. The temptation is respectable and mostly correct: no one can verify everything, and a person who insists on personally checking the chemistry of every medicine will die of something ordinary while checking. Deference is not the abdication of reason; it is one of reason's economies.
>
> But an economy has a price, and the price here is that the deferring person acquires a belief without acquiring any of the machinery that would let him revise it. He holds a conclusion that is, from the inside, indistinguishable from a conclusion he had reached himself, and he will defend it with the confidence appropriate to the latter. This is why deference so easily curdles. The believer cannot say what would change his mind, because the reasons that would change it were never his to begin with. He has borrowed the verdict without the evidence, and a verdict without evidence is not knowledge held lightly — it is dogma held cheaply.
>
> What follows is not that we should defer less, but that we should defer differently. The judgement that a layperson can competently make is not the object-level one — whether this drug works — but the second-order one: whether this person, on this question, in this institution, has been placed under conditions that reward being right and penalise being wrong. That judgement requires no technical training. It requires attention to incentives, to track records, to whether dissent within the field is tolerated or punished. It is, admittedly, a harder discipline than either credulity or contrarianism, both of which relieve their practitioners of the duty to look closely. The contrarian and the devotee make the same mistake in opposite directions: each has decided in advance what expertise is worth, and so neither has to examine any particular expert at all.

**Q4.** Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?

(b) Since laypeople cannot evaluate technical claims, they should suspend judgement on specialist questions altogether.

(d) Contrarianism is a more serious intellectual failing than credulity, because it rejects knowledge that is freely available.

# ANSWERS AND EXPLANATIONS

## Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (b).** The passage's spine is the two-good structure of a dwelling (reproducible structure, non-reproducible location) plus rules that fix permitted quantity independently of demand; (a) is contradicted ("cost is a symptom"), (c) and (d) are not argued.

**Q2 — (d).** The author states explicitly that measures raising what households can pay, without altering what may be built, are absorbed into land price and benefit "whoever owned the plot".

**Q3 — (b).** "Running out of consent, not concrete" only holds if physical inputs are genuinely not the binding constraint — i.e. materials and labour remain available at costs that do not themselves choke supply. (c) overstates; (a) and (d) attribute motives the passage never claims.

**Q4 — (c).** The final paragraph makes the prescription explicit: defer differently, at the second order (incentives, track record, tolerance of dissent), not less.

**Q5 — (a).** The author's complaint is that the deferring person "cannot say what would change his mind"; someone who can has the machinery for revision, which is precisely what the author wants deference to carry.

**Q6 — (d).** He calls deference "one of reason's economies" (approval) but insists on "a harder discipline than either credulity or contrarianism" — approving with a demand, not sceptical or neutral.

## Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (a).** Test the eight combinations, or reason directly. Suppose R is a knave: then "exactly one of us is a knight" is false. If R is a knave and P is a knight, P's statement makes Q also a knave (same type as R); then Q's claim "P and I are of different types" would be true, but Q is a knave — contradiction. If R and P are both knaves, P's statement is false, so Q and R differ in type, making Q a knight; then Q's true statement requires P and Q to differ (they do), but now exactly one knight (Q) exists, so R's statement was true — impossible for a knave. Hence **R is a knight**, so exactly one of the three is a knight, i.e. P and Q are knaves. Check: P (knave) says Q and R are the same type — false ✓ (knave and knight). Q (knave) says P and Q differ — false ✓ (both knaves). R (knight) says exactly one is a knight — true ✓. Unique and consistent.

**Q8 — (b).** A unit cube gets two green faces only if it lies on one of the **four vertical edges** of the big cube (each vertical edge is the meeting of two green side faces). Each vertical edge holds 6 unit cubes, but the topmost and bottommost of them also touch a red face. So each edge contributes 6 − 2 = 4, and 4 × 4 = **16**.

**Q9 — (d).** Poets ⊆ dreamers; some dreamers are cynics; cynics ∩ optimists = ∅.
- I: the cynical dreamers need not be poets — does not follow.
- II: those dreamers who are cynics are, by statement 3, not optimists — **follows**.
- III: nothing prevents every poet from being an optimist (poets may lie entirely outside the cynic region) — does not follow.
Only II.

**Q10 — (d).** Elements: 42, cart, 17, nest, 63, aim, 28, pool. Words in alphabetical order: aim, cart, nest, pool. Numbers in descending order: 63, 42, 28, 17.
- Step I: aim → left end, 63 → right end: **aim 42 cart 17 nest 28 pool 63**
- Step II: cart → position 2, 42 → position 7: **aim cart 17 nest 28 pool 42 63**
Fifth from the left in Step II = **28**. (Step III: aim cart nest 17 pool 28 42 63; Step IV completes the arrangement.)

**Q11 — (a).** An implicit assumption is what must be true for the stated means to serve the stated end. Free travel can raise employment only if travel cost is an actual barrier. (b) is absurd — a fare waiver presupposes bus use; (c) is a condition of implementation, not of the reasoning; (d) is irrelevant.

**Q12 — (d).** G not selected ⇒ by condition 1 the lawyer must be **H**. Condition 4 then excludes **E**; condition 3 (contrapositive) excludes **B**. The remaining three seats come from {A, C, D, F}, i.e. one of the four triples:
- {A, C, D}: violates 5 (C without F). ✗
- {A, D, F}: violates 2 (A with D). ✗
- {A, C, F}: valid → committee A, C, F, H.
- {C, D, F}: valid → committee C, D, F, H.
**F appears in both valid committees**, so "F is selected" must be true. A is absent from the second committee, D from the first, and E is excluded outright.

| Category | 2020 (₹ cr) | 2025 (₹ cr) | Increase |

## Decision-Making

(a) and (d) are irregular; (b) abandons a destitute applicant when a lawful remedy exists.

## Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (b).** Take the work as LCM(12, 18) = 36 units. A does 3 units/day, B does 2 units/day. Working alternately starting with **B**, each 2-day block (B then A) yields 5 units. After 7 blocks = 14 days, 35 units are done, 1 unit remains. Day 15 is B's turn (odd-numbered days are B's); B needs 1/2 of that day for the last unit. Total = **14½ days**. (Trap: starting with A gives 14⅓ days — the starting order matters.)

**Q21 — (b).** Volume of cone = (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)π(36)(24) = 288π cm³. Usable metal after a 10% loss = 0.9 × 288π = 259.2π cm³. Volume of one sphere = (4/3)π(2)³ = 32π/3 cm³. Number = 259.2π ÷ (32π/3) = 259.2 × 3/32 = 24.3, so **24 complete spheres**. Trap (d) 27 is the answer with no wastage.

**Q22 — (a).** For digits strictly increasing, 0 can never appear (it could only be the smallest digit, i.e. the leading digit, which is not allowed). So choose any 3 distinct digits from 1–9; each such choice can be arranged in increasing order in exactly one way. Count = C(9, 3) = **84**. Trap (b) 120 = C(10, 3), which wrongly admits 0.

## Timing tip of the week

**Bank the DI caselet before you touch the second RC passage.** Slab-tariff and share-to-value sets like Q13–Q16 are pure arithmetic with a fixed number of steps — you can predict, within ten seconds of reading, that they will take about 90 seconds each and that you will get them right. An abstract passage cannot be predicted that way: it may take 3 minutes or 6, and the extra 3 minutes buy you one uncertain mark. In a qualifying paper, secure the predictable marks first and let the unpredictable ones compete for whatever time is left.
