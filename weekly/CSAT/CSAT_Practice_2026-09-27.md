## Section A — Reading Comprehension (Q1–Q6)

### Passage I — Q1–Q3 (analytical / science & decisions)

A weather service that announces "rain tomorrow" and one that announces "a seventy per cent chance of rain tomorrow" may be relying on the same model, yet they are offering different things. The first has made a decision on the listener's behalf; the second has handed the decision back. For a long time public agencies preferred the first form, reasoning that ordinary users find probabilities confusing and want to be told what will happen. The preference is understandable, but it rests on a misunderstanding of what a forecast is for. A forecast has no value in itself; its value lies entirely in the decisions it improves. And the same probability should lead different users to different decisions. A farmer deciding whether to spray a pesticide that washes off in rain should hold back at a probability that a construction contractor, for whom an idle day is costly, would happily ignore. Each user has a threshold, set by the ratio of what it costs to protect against the event to what is lost if the event occurs unprotected, and a forecast is useful only if it can be compared against that threshold. A categorical forecast silently imposes a single threshold — the forecaster's — on everyone.

There is a second cost. A forecaster who must say yes or no will be judged by how often the yes comes true, and will learn to lean in whichever direction is less embarrassing. Warnings of cyclones and floods drift towards over-caution, until users learn to discount them. A probabilistic forecast can instead be checked for calibration: of all the days on which rain was given a seventy per cent chance, did it rain on about seventy per cent of them? A service that is well calibrated deserves trust even when it is wrong on a particular day, and its users can learn how far to rely on it.

None of this means that probabilities explain themselves. They must be accompanied by guidance on what they imply for common decisions. But the remedy for the public's unfamiliarity with uncertainty is to help people reason about it, not to conceal it.

### Passage II — Q4–Q6 (abstract / philosophical)

We praise originality as though it were the opposite of imitation, and tend to regard the imitator as someone who has not yet found himself. Yet it is hard to name any capacity we did not acquire by copying. The child learns to speak by reproducing sounds whose meaning it does not yet grasp; the apprentice learns a craft by repeating movements whose purpose becomes clear only later; the young writer learns to write by composing, often unconsciously, in the manner of writers she admires. At each stage the learner submits to a form she did not choose and could not yet have chosen, because the standards by which one might choose are themselves part of what is being learnt.

It does not follow that originality is an illusion. It follows that originality is a late achievement, and a relational one. A new idea is new only against a background of the old; a departure from convention is intelligible only to those who know the convention, including the one who departs. The composer who breaks a rule of harmony must first hear why the rule was there, or his breach is not a discovery but an accident. Those who are impatient to be original and refuse the discipline of imitation do not escape influence; they merely submit to the influences that are most widespread and least examined, and end up sounding like everyone else.

What distinguishes the mature practitioner is not that she has ceased to imitate but that she imitates knowingly and selectively. She has taken in enough of a tradition to see its tensions — the places where its own standards pull against one another — and her innovations typically come from pressing on those points. The self we call original is, in this sense, less a possession than a way of standing within an inheritance: close enough to be formed by it, far enough to argue with it.

# Answers & Worked Methods

## Reading Comprehension

**Q1 — (b)** · *Main idea - forecasting as decision support (probabilistic vs categorical forecasts)* · Moderate  
The passage argues (i) the same probability should lead different users to different actions, so a yes/no verdict imposes the forecaster's threshold on all, and (ii) probabilities can be checked for calibration. (b) combines both strands. (a) imputes deliberate deception; (c) reverses the author's view (he rejects concealment); (d) contradicts the opening line — both may use the same model.

**Q2 — (d)** · *Inference - applying the cost/loss threshold to new cases* · Hard  
The passage sets each user's threshold at cost of protection ÷ loss if unprotected; protection is worthwhile when the probability exceeds it. Ratios: (a) 0.83, (b) 0.67, (c) 0.625, (d) 0.10. Only in (d) is 0.30 above the threshold. (A data-science reader will recognise the classic cost–loss decision model: act if p > C/L.)

**Q3 — (a)** · *Assumption - negation test on the 'single threshold' criticism* · Moderate  
Negate (a): if every user had the same cost/loss ratio, a single threshold would suit everyone and the criticism would collapse — so (a) is necessary. (b) and (c) are not claimed; (d) is too strong and unnecessary.

**Q4 — (c)** · *Main idea - originality as a relational achievement within a tradition* · Moderate  
The author explicitly denies (a) ('It does not follow that originality is an illusion'); (b) overstates — the mature practitioner imitates 'selectively'; (d) is a supporting detail and 'inevitable' is too strong. (c) states the thesis: originality is a late, relational achievement.

**Q5 — (b)** · *Inference - author's likely judgement on cases* · Moderate  
The passage says those who refuse the discipline of imitation 'submit to the influences that are most widespread and least examined, and end up sounding like everyone else' — exactly (b). (a) and (d) are knowing, selective imitation; (c) is 'pressing on' a tradition's internal tensions.

**Q6 — (a)** · *Tone and attitude - towards the impatiently original* · Easy  
The author says they 'do not escape influence' and 'end up sounding like everyone else' — their aim defeats itself. Not admiring or neutral; not contempt for innovation generally, which he values as a mature achievement.

## Logical Reasoning & Analytical Ability

**Q7 — (c)** · *Linear seating arrangement (single row, facing north)* · Moderate  
Number seats 1–6 from left to right (facing north, 'right' = higher number). From (i)–(ii): Q = 1 with P = 4, or Q = 6 with P = 3. From (iv) S must be immediately left of Q, which is impossible if Q = 1; so Q = 6, S = 5, P = 3, and by (iii) U = 4. T and R fill seats 1 and 2; by (v) R = 2, T = 1. Row: T R P U S Q. Ends: T and Q.

**Q8 — (b)** · *Calendar - odd days across a leap year* · Moderate  
Count odd days year by year from 15 Aug to 15 Aug: 2026→27: 365 days = 1; 2027→28: includes 29 Feb 2028 = 2; 2028→29: 1; 2029→30: 1. Total 5 odd days. Saturday + 5 = Thursday. Trap: forgetting the leap day gives 4 → Wednesday; counting 2026's own February (already past) is the other common slip.

**Q9 — (d)** · *Coded inequalities - chained comparisons* · Moderate  
I: P ≥ Q > R > S → P > S ✔. II: T ≥ R and Q > R, but T and Q are not linked — cannot be determined ✘. III: U = Q > R > S → U > S ✔. Hence only I and III.

**Q10 — (a)** · *Dice - deducing opposite faces from two positions with orientation* · Hard  
Face 1 is on top in both positions, so the four side faces form a fixed cycle (front → right → back → left) when viewed from above. Position 1 gives the cycle (2, 3, x, y). Position 2 is the same die rotated about the vertical axis: front 4 is followed by right 2, so 4 comes immediately before 2 in the cycle — i.e. y = 4 and x = 5. Cycle: 2, 3, 5, 4. Opposite pairs: 2–5, 3–4, and 1–6. So 4 is opposite 3. Trap: (c) 5 is opposite 2, not 3.

**Q11 — (c)** · *Mathematical operations - symbol and number interchange* · Moderate  
Swap 3 and 6: 18 − 6 + 4 ÷ 3 × 5. Replace symbols: 18 ÷ 6 × 4 + 3 − 5. BODMAS: 18 ÷ 6 = 3; 3 × 4 = 12; 12 + 3 − 5 = 10. Trap (a) 25 comes from ignoring the number swap (18 ÷ 3 × 4 + 6 − 5).

**Q12 — (b)** · *Strong and weak arguments* · Easy  
I is strong: it is directly relevant and names a real benefit (informed choice, lower information asymmetry). II is weak: it is a sweeping claim — health care is a legitimately regulated sector. III is weak: 'others do it' is imitation, not a reason tied to the merits.

## Data Interpretation

**Q13 — (b)** · *Index numbers - year-on-year percentage change vs absolute change (trap)* · Moderate  
Growth = index ÷ previous index − 1: 2021: 12/100 = 12.0%; 2022: 14/112 = 12.5%; 2023: fall; 2024: 14/120 = 11.7%; 2025: 16/134 = 11.9%. Highest is 2022. Trap (d): 2025 has the largest rise in index points (16) but on a larger base. Shortcut: compare 14/112 = 1/8 with 16/134 < 1/8.

**Q14 — (c)** · *Index numbers - revenue change from price and quantity together* · Moderate  
Revenue ∝ price index × quantity. 2020: 100 × 50 = 5,000; 2025: 150 × 60 = 9,000. Ratio 1.8 → 80% higher. (In rupees: ₹40/kg × 5 crore kg = ₹200 crore vs ₹60/kg × 6 crore kg = ₹360 crore.) Trap (a): adding 50% price growth and 20% quantity growth ignores the cross term 0.5 × 0.2 = 10%.

**Q15 — (d)** · *Averages with a changed denominator (runs per dismissal vs per innings)* · Moderate  
Averages (runs ÷ dismissals): A 640/16 = 40; B 684/18 = 38; C 672/16 = 42; D 540/12 = 45; E 814/22 = 37. D is highest. Trap (a): B has the highest runs per innings (38) because he has no not-outs — dividing by innings instead of dismissals gives B.

**Q16 — (a)** · *Required value to reach a target average* · Hard  
E's current average = 814/22 = 37. After one more dismissal he has 23 dismissals, so he needs 38 × 23 = 874 runs in all: 874 − 814 = 60. Shortcut: he needs 38 for the new innings plus 1 extra for each of the 22 earlier dismissals = 60. Trap (c) 136 uses innings (25) instead of dismissals.

## Quantitative Aptitude

**Q20 — (c)** · *Number theory - perfect-square divisors* · Moderate  
720 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5. A square divisor needs even exponents: 2 ∈ {0, 2, 4} (3 ways), 3 ∈ {0, 2} (2 ways), 5 ∈ {0} (1 way). Total 3 × 2 × 1 = 6: 1, 4, 9, 16, 36, 144.

**Q21 — (a)** · *Profit and loss - mark-up, successive discounts and false weight* · Hard  
Let cost be ₹100 per kg. Price charged for a 'kg' = 100 × 1.40 × 0.90 × 0.95 = ₹119.70. Goods actually given = 0.9 kg, costing ₹90. Profit % = 119.70/90 − 1 = 1.33 − 1 = 33%. Trap (b) 19.7% ignores the false weight; (c) 29.7% adds the 10% from the weight instead of compounding it.

**Q22 — (d)** · *Circular track - distinct meeting points (opposite directions)* · Hard  
Opposite directions: relative speed 12 m/s, so they meet every 600/12 = 50 s, A advancing 250 m each time. A's meeting positions are 250k mod 600: 0, 250, 500, 150, 400, 50, 300, 550, 200, 450, 100, 350, then back to 0 — 12 distinct points. Rule: with speeds in lowest ratio a:b (here 5:7), opposite directions give a + b distinct points; same direction gives |a − b| (= 2, trap (a)).

## Timing / strategy tip

**In DI, before you divide, ask "divided by what?"** Three of this week's four DI items (Q13, Q15, Q16) and the Q21 profit question are built on a denominator trap: percentage change needs the *previous* year's base, a batting average divides by *dismissals* not innings, and a false weight changes the *cost* side, not the price side. Spend five seconds writing the ratio as a fraction with its denominator named (e.g. "runs ÷ dismissals") before computing — the arithmetic is rarely what costs marks in CSAT; the wrong base is. On a 35-minute clock, bank RC and Decision-Making first (≈14 min), then take DI and reasoning, and leave the one hardest quant item for last.
