## Questions

**Q1. [RC]**

Read the following passage and answer the question that follows.

Policy evaluation suffers from a problem that is easily named and hard to escape: those who receive a programme are rarely a random sample of those who might have. Villages that adopt a new credit scheme early tend to be better connected, better led, or simply hungrier for change; a comparison of their later incomes with those of laggards therefore mixes the effect of the scheme with the effect of whatever made them adopt it. Statistical adjustment can remove only those differences that the analyst has thought to measure, and the most consequential ones — motivation, local trust, administrative competence — are precisely those that resist measurement.

Natural experiments offer a partial escape. When a scheme is allocated by lottery because applications exceed places, or by an arbitrary eligibility cut-off such that households just above and just below the line differ in nothing but their eligibility, the comparison becomes credible in a way no regression can make it. Such designs have produced some of the most persuasive findings in recent applied economics.

Their persuasiveness, however, is purchased at a price. A cut-off design tells us about households near the cut-off, and a lottery tells us about those who applied; whether the result transfers to the poorest households, or to another state with a weaker bureaucracy, is a separate question that the design cannot answer. The temptation is to treat internal validity as though it conferred external validity. A finding can be unimpeachable where it was obtained and still be a poor guide to action elsewhere. The sensible response is neither to discard such evidence nor to generalise from it freely, but to ask what mechanism produced the effect and whether that mechanism is likely to operate in the new setting.

Q. Which one of the following best expresses the central argument of the passage?

- (a) Statistical adjustment is useless in policy evaluation and should be replaced entirely by lotteries.
- (b) Natural experiments overcome the selection problem credibly, but their findings should be carried to new settings only after examining the mechanism behind them.
- (c) Programmes that are adopted early are always more effective than those adopted late.
- (d) Evidence from natural experiments is unreliable because eligibility cut-offs are arbitrary.

**Q4. [RC]**

Read the following passage and answer the question that follows.

We are accustomed to treating remembrance as the unqualified duty of a decent society: the injuries of the past, once forgotten, are said to be liable to recur. The slogan has force, and yet it conceals a difficulty. A community that remembers everything with equal vividness cannot distinguish the grievance that deserves redress from the one that merely deserves to be outlived. Every long-settled society is built on layers of wrongs — conquests, expulsions, broken promises — and if each layer retained full claim on the present, no present arrangement could be legitimate and no neighbour could be trusted without first being audited.

Ancient practices of amnesty understood this. Declaring that certain things shall not be spoken of in the assembly was not an act of cowardice but a technique of coexistence: it allowed enemies of yesterday to become co-citizens of today. But amnesty has its own hazard. Forgetting imposed from above, by those who committed the wrongs, is merely impunity wearing the garb of reconciliation. The distinction lies not in how much is remembered but in who decides, and whether the wronged are permitted to participate in the decision.

It follows that the opposition between memory and oblivion is misleading. What a society needs is not a maximum of one or the other but a discipline of selection: an agreed, contestable and revisable account of which wrongs shall continue to bind, which shall be acknowledged and laid down, and which have been so thoroughly absorbed that to revive them would manufacture a quarrel rather than heal one. Such a discipline is never finished. Each generation inherits it, and each must decide anew what it can afford to carry.

- (a) Forgetting past wrongs is always preferable to remembering them because it secures peace.
- (b) Ancient amnesties were superior to modern reconciliation practices.
- (c) A society needs neither total remembrance nor total oblivion but a participatory, revisable process for deciding which past wrongs should still bind.
- (d) Remembrance is a duty only for societies that have never committed wrongs.

## Answers and Worked Solutions

**Q1. (b)** — *Main idea - analytical (causal inference and natural experiments in policy evaluation)* (Moderate)

The passage praises natural experiments (internal validity) and then warns about external validity, advising a mechanism-based check. The other options overstate (replacing all adjustment), misread (early adopters always better) or reject the evidence outright.

**Q2. (c)** — *Inference - applying the argument to a new case (scholarship lottery)* (Moderate)

The author's prescription is to ask what mechanism produced the effect and whether it operates elsewhere. Extending nationwide generalises freely; disregarding the result discards it; waiting for a regression prefers the weaker design.

**Q3. (a)** — *Assumption identification - credibility of cut-off designs (negation test)* (Hard)

Negation test: if households could place themselves just below the line (for example by under-reporting income), the two groups would differ in more than eligibility and the claim collapses. The options on all eligible households or identical results elsewhere concern external validity, which the author says the design cannot settle.

**Q4. (c)** — *Main idea - philosophical (selective memory and amnesty)* (Moderate)

The third paragraph states it: a 'discipline of selection', contestable and revisable. Pure forgetting is the opposite extreme the author rejects; the other options are not argued.

**Q5. (d)** — *Inference - author's likely judgement on cases (illegitimate amnesty)* (Moderate)

The author calls forgetting imposed by wrongdoers 'impunity wearing the garb of reconciliation'; the test is who decides and whether the wronged participate. The other three options all involve victim participation, consultation or revisability.

**Q6. (b)** — *Tone and attitude - towards the 'never forget' slogan* (Moderate)

'The slogan has force, and yet it conceals a difficulty' signals qualified criticism: not scorn, not endorsement, not neutrality.

**Q7. (d)** — *Blood relations - relationship chain with undetermined gender* (Moderate)

Method: P brother of Q, T father of P, so T is also Q's father. Q is mother of R; R's sister S is also Q's child. So S is T's grandchild. 'S' is not marked male or female ('R is the sister of S' gives R's gender only) - gender cannot be determined.

**Q8. (d)** — *Direction sense - shadows and successive turns* (Moderate)

Method: Morning sun is in the east, so shadows fall west. Aman's right is west, so Aman faces north; Bhavna, facing him, faces south. Turn 90 degrees clockwise: south -> west. Turn 135 degrees anticlockwise from west: west -> south (90) -> south-east (45 more). Final: South-East.

**Q9. (c)** — *Syllogism with complementary (either-or) pair* (Hard)

Method: Those doctors who are artists are not politicians, so III follows. 'Some doctors are artists' converts to IV. I and II are a complementary pair (I-type vs E-type on the same terms); neither follows alone because only the artist-doctors are known to be non-politicians, but one of them must be true, so either-or follows.

**Q10. (b)** — *Clocks - angle between hands* (Moderate)

Method: At 4:00 the hour hand is at 120 degrees and the minute hand at 0 degrees, so the angle is 120. The minute hand gains 5.5 degrees per minute. Need the gap to fall to 90: 120 - 5.5t = 90 -> t = 30/5.5 = 60/11 = 5 5/11 min. (The second right angle, 5.5t - 120 = 90, gives 38 2/11 min, the trap.)

**Q11. (c)** — *Puzzle - matching two attributes to four persons* (Hard)

Method: Dev is a teacher, not in Patna; the engineer is in Pune so the teacher is not in Pune; Asha is in Kochi; hence Dev is in Jaipur. Asha cannot be engineer (Pune), doctor (clue 2) or teacher (Dev), so Asha is the lawyer (Kochi, consistent with clue 1). Bala and Chitra take engineer/doctor and Pune/Patna; Chitra is not in Pune, so Chitra is in Patna and Bala in Pune as engineer; therefore Chitra is the doctor in Patna.

**Q12. (c)** — *Critical reasoning - strengthening a causal claim* (Moderate)

Strengthening a causal claim means showing the specific mechanism (bag waste fell) and ruling out alternatives. Lower rainfall offers an alternative explanation; more drain-cleaning changes what is collected; similar falls in cities without a ban show the fall occurs without the ban.

**Q13. (c)** — *Table - absolute increase in a sub-group vs share and growth traps* (Moderate)

Girls 2021 (thousands): A 36, B 60, C 24, D 22. Girls 2025: A 48, B 67.6, C 39.6, D 24. Increase: A 12, B 7.6, C 15.6, D 2. Largest = C. Trap: D shows the largest rise in girls' share (5 points) but a tiny absolute rise; A is the usual guess from a large base.

**Q14. (b)** — *Table - weighted vs simple average of percentages (trap)* (Moderate)

Girls 2025 = 48 + 67.6 + 39.6 + 24 = 179.2; total = 360; share = 179.2/360 = 49.8%. Trap: the simple average of the four percentages (48+52+44+60)/4 = 51.0% ignores district sizes.

**Q15. (c)** — *Caselet - overlapping sets (exactly two items)* (Hard)

Convert to numbers: phone-TV 120, TV-fridge 80, phone-fridge 100, all three 40. Exactly two = (120-40) + (80-40) + (100-40) = 80 + 40 + 60 = 180. Traps: 300 = sum of pairwise overlaps (counts the all-three group three times); 220 = at least two (180 + 40).

**Q16. (d)** — *Caselet - overlapping sets (only one item)* (Hard)

Phone owners = 280. Subtract those who also own something else: phone-TV only = 120-40 = 80; phone-fridge only = 100-40 = 60; all three = 40. Phone only = 280 - 80 - 60 - 40 = 100. Trap: 280 - 120 - 100 = 60 forgets to add back the all-three group (which was subtracted twice).

**Q17. (b)** — *Gifts and hospitality - integrity and conflict of interest* (Moderate)

Gifts from a person with a pending official matter compromise (or appear to compromise) impartiality and breach conduct rules; return it, record it and stay merit-based.

**Q18. (c)** — *Empathy with rules - subordinate in personal crisis* (Moderate)

Balanced option: empathy plus rule-abiding, protecting the work. A show-cause notice is rigid and punitive, overlooking everything is neglect and unfair to the team, and a unilateral transfer is arbitrary.

**Q19. (d)** — *Environment versus livelihood - proportionate enforcement* (Moderate)

The balanced, lawful, proportionate response protects public health, gives due process and cushions livelihoods, with a credible consequence for non-compliance.

**Q20. (c)** — *Compound vs simple interest - 3-year difference* (Moderate)

CI - SI over 3 years = P[(1.1)^3 - 1 - 0.3] = P(1.331 - 1.3) = 0.031P. So P = 3100/0.031 = Rs 1,00,000.

**Q21. (d)** — *Relative speed - trains crossing in opposite and same directions* (Hard)

Combined length 250 m. Opposite: u + v = 250/6 m/s = 150 km/h. Same direction: u - v = 250/30 m/s = 30 km/h. So u = (150+30)/2 = 90 km/h (v = 60 km/h).

**Q22. (d)** — *Permutations - arrangements with no two identical letters adjacent* (Hard)

Place the 7 non-S letters (M, I x4, P x2) first: 7!/(4! 2!) = 105 ways. They create 8 gaps; choose 4 gaps for the S's: C(8,4) = 70. Total = 105 x 70 = 7350. (Trap: 34650 = 11!/(4!4!2!) is the unrestricted count.)
