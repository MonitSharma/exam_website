## SCHEMES & PROGRAMMES

### 1. BHAVYA–Rasayan Scheme (Chemical Parks)
**GS3 — Indian Economy: Industrial Policy, Infrastructure, Manufacturing**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | Department of Chemicals & Petrochemicals, **Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilizers** |
| **Approved** | Union Cabinet, **24 July 2026** |
| **Type** | Central Sector Scheme |
| **Outlay** | **₹3,030 crore** (₹3,000 cr for common infrastructure + ₹30 cr administrative) |
| **Period** | FY 2026-27 to FY 2030-31 (5 years) |

**Objective:** Create dedicated, plug-and-play chemical manufacturing parks to cut India's import dependence in specialty and bulk chemicals and improve global cost competitiveness.

**Key features**

- **3 dedicated Chemical Parks** to be set up; **up to ₹1,000 crore central grant per park**.
- Conditional on a **minimum ₹500 crore contribution by the concerned State Government** — a genuine cost-sharing design.
- Common infrastructure: Common Effluent Treatment Plants (CETPs), hazardous-waste disposal, water distribution, **steam generation networks, solvent recovery systems** and connected pipelines.
- Announced in **Union Budget FY 2026-27**; it is a **vertical under the umbrella Bharat Audyogik Vikas Yojana (BHAVYA)** framework approved in March 2026.

**Beneficiaries:** Chemical MSMEs and large manufacturers; downstream pharma, agrochemical, textile and paint industries.

**Significance:** Chemicals are among India's largest import categories after crude and electronics. Shared effluent/solvent-recovery infrastructure is the single biggest fixed-cost barrier for small chemical units, so pooling it is the right lever.
**Criticism / watch:** ₹1,000 cr per park is thin for the scale of utilities involved; the ₹500 cr state match may exclude fiscally weaker states, concentrating parks in already-industrialised regions. Environmental clearance and community consent for chemical clusters remain contentious.

### 2. Mission for Cotton Productivity
**GS3 — Agriculture: cropping patterns, agricultural marketing, food processing / Textile industry**
*(Sectoral spotlight in the news 20 July 2026 — Government's "White Gold" push)*

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | **Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare**, jointly implemented with **Ministry of Textiles** |
| **Approved** | Union Cabinet, **2026** (mission period **2026-27 to 2030-31**) |
| **Type** | Centrally Sponsored / Mission-mode scheme |
| **Outlay** | **₹5,659.22 crore** |

**Objective:** Achieve self-sufficiency in cotton and restore India's competitiveness in global textile markets by 2030-31.

**Key features**

- Targets **lint productivity 440 kg/ha → 755 kg/ha**, and **production 297 lakh bales → 498 lakh bales** by 2030-31.
- Focus on **140 districts across 14 major cotton-growing states** in the first phase.
- R&D on **climate-smart, pest-resistant, high-yielding varieties including Extra Long Staple (ELS) cotton**, using advanced breeding and biotech tools.
- Upscaling of **High Density Planting System (HDPS)**, **Closer Spacing (CS)** and Integrated Cotton Management; supported by **10 ICAR institutes and CSIR**.
- News-hook additions this week: emphasis on **fibre quality, traceability, value addition and farmers' market access**.

**Prelims fact:** India is **1st in the world in cotton cultivated area** and **2nd in production and consumption**.

**Significance:** India's cotton yield is well below the world average despite the largest acreage; ELS dependence on imports (mainly Egypt, USA) hurts high-value textile exports. Directly feeds the 5F (Farm-to-Fibre-to-Factory-to-Fashion-to-Foreign) vision.
**Criticism:** No clear policy resolution on **Bt cotton / HTBt and new-generation GM traits**, pink bollworm resistance, or the pending regulatory question on transgenic approvals — arguably the binding constraint on yield. MSP-procurement dependence via CCI also unaddressed.

### 3. VISHWAS, 2026 (Voluntary Initiative for Settlement of Historic Waivers and Amicable Solutions)
**GS2/GS3 — Government policies & interventions; Social security; Ease of doing business**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | **Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO)**, Ministry of Labour & Employment |
| **Effective** | **29 June 2026**; open for **six months**; widely reported/rolled out 15–18 July 2026 |
| **Type** | One-time dispute-resolution / amnesty scheme (Central Sector) |

**Objective:** Amicable, concessional settlement of long-pending disputes over **damages** levied under **Section 14B of the EPF & MP Act, 1952** and **Section 128 of the Code on Social Security, 2020**.

**Key features**

- **Reduced damage rates:** default up to 2 months → **0.25% p.m.**; >2 but <4 months → **0.50% p.m.**; >4 months → **1% p.m.**
- Covers orders under challenge before judicial forums, final orders where recovery is pending or partly complete, and cases where notices have been issued.
- **Fraud cases are excluded.**
- Aim: reduce litigation, unlock stuck recovery, encourage voluntary compliance.

**Beneficiaries:** Employers/establishments covered under EPF with pending damages disputes; indirectly EPF subscribers whose dues get regularised.

**Significance:** Notable as the first EPFO-side amnesty aligned to the **Code on Social Security, 2020** — a marker of the labour codes actually operating. Fits the "decriminalise/de-litigate" reform track (cf. Jan Vishwas Act).
**Criticism:** Amnesty schemes create **moral hazard** — compliant employers effectively subsidise defaulters; repeated one-time windows dilute deterrence.

### 4. Sarbat.AI — Punjab: AI as a Mainstream School Subject (State)
**GS2 — Education; GS3 — Science & Technology / AI in governance**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | **Punjab School Education Board (PSEB)**, Department of School Education, Government of Punjab |
| **Launched** | **22 July 2026** (Education Minister Harjot Singh Bains) |
| **Type** | State scheme |

**Objective:** Make Artificial Intelligence a **mainstream, compulsory subject from Class I to Class XII** — Punjab is the **first Indian state to do so**.

**Key features**

- Phased rollout: **Phase 1 — Classes VIII–XII**, **Phase 2 — Classes I–VII**.
- Curriculum developed by PSEB drawing on **Singapore, Finland, UAE and US** models.
- Coverage: **~31 lakh (3.1 million) students across 25,172 schools**.
- Technology partnerships including **Google and Intel**.
- Stated goals: future-ready skills, innovation, **ethical AI**, entrepreneurship, responsible digital citizenship.

**Significance:** A concrete state-level operationalisation of NEP 2020's push on computational thinking and coding; useful example for GS2 answers on cooperative federalism in education.
**Criticism / feasibility:** Teacher capacity is the obvious bottleneck — AI pedagogy for Class I–VII requires trained teachers Punjab does not yet have at scale. Device and connectivity gaps in rural government schools risk widening the digital divide rather than closing it.

### 5. Samras Vidya Setu Mega Campaign — Gujarat (State)
**GS2 — Education: RTE, out-of-school children, social sector interventions**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | Education Department, **Government of Gujarat** |
| **Launched** | **23 July 2026**, by CM Bhupendra Patel from Ahmedabad |
| **Type** | State campaign |

**Objective:** Identify and **re-enrol over 80,000 school dropouts** into formal education in **age-appropriate classes (Std. I–XII)**.

**Key features**

- **District-wise lists** of dropout children prepared statewide.
- Convergent outreach: **teachers + local elected representatives + community leaders** visiting parents.
- **Bridge/remedial academic support** to close learning gaps after re-entry.
- Context: Gujarat's school dropout rate reported down to **~2%**.

**Significance:** Directly relevant to **Article 21A / RTE Act, 2009** obligations and NEP 2020's target of **100% GER in school education by 2030**. Compare with MP's *Saraswati Abhiyaan* (dropout girls) and UP's *Sharda* campaign — a good multi-state comparison for GS2.
**Criticism:** Re-enrolment is the easy half; **retention** after re-entry is the hard part. Absent addressing child labour, migration and learning-level gaps (ASER-type deficits), re-enrolled children frequently drop out again.

### 6. Climate Resilient Village (CRV) Programme — Tamil Nadu Climate Change Mission (State)
**GS3 — Environment: climate change adaptation, disaster management; GS1 — coastal geography**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | **Tamil Nadu Green Climate Company (TNGCC)** under the **Tamil Nadu Climate Change Mission** |
| **Milestone** | **22 July 2026** — **Killai (Cuddalore district)** became TN's **first fully operational Climate Resilient Village** |
| **Type** | State mission (funded via District Climate Change Mission + **Corporate Environmental Responsibility (CER)**) |
| **Project cost** | **₹230.15 lakh** for Killai |

**Objective:** Build village-level, community-owned adaptive capacity against floods, cyclones, drought, coastal erosion, sea-level rise and saline intrusion.

**Key features**

- Package combines **ecosystem restoration + waste management + livelihood support + community preparedness**.
- Flagship works at Killai: restoration of a **3-km stretch of the Buckingham Canal** (completed 14 March 2026), adjacent to the **Pichavaram mangroves**.
- Renewable-energy adoption and community participation built into the model.
- **11 Climate Resilient Villages identified** across Tamil Nadu.

**Significance:** One of India's few sub-national institutional vehicles for adaptation (TN also has a Climate Change Mission, Green Climate Company, Green Tamil Nadu Mission and Wetlands Mission). Strong example for questions on **State Action Plans on Climate Change (SAPCC)** and locally-led adaptation. Ties to India's **NAPCC** and adaptation commitments under the Paris Agreement.
**Criticism:** Heavy reliance on **CER/CSR-type funding** makes scale-up uncertain; ₹2.3 crore per village against thousands of vulnerable coastal habitations is a slow route without dedicated budget lines.

### 7. NIRD&PR recognised as NCVET **Awarding Body (Dual)**
**GS2 — Skill development, Panchayati Raj institutions; GS3 — Employment**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Bodies** | **National Council for Vocational Education and Training (NCVET)**, Ministry of Skill Development & Entrepreneurship → recognised **National Institute of Rural Development & Panchayati Raj (NIRD&PR)**, Ministry of Rural Development |
| **Date** | **21 July 2026** |

**What "Awarding Body (Dual)" means:** NIRD&PR can now **train, assess and certify** learners for its approved qualifications — where training is imparted directly on campuses/centres it owns or fully manages — issuing **NSQF-aligned certificates**.

**Key features**

- Initial approvals: **"Industrial General Pest Management Technician"** qualification, and a **micro-credential on Generation and Mobilisation of Own Source Revenue (OSR) by Panchayats**.
- Planned tracks: industry-relevant courses for **rural youth**, entrepreneurship courses for **Self-Help Groups (SHGs)**, and professional courses for **Rural Development & Panchayati Raj functionaries**.

**Significance:** The OSR micro-credential is quietly important — **Own Source Revenue** is the weakest link in Panchayat fiscal autonomy (most PRIs depend almost entirely on devolved grants). Certifying this as an NSQF skill is a structural nudge on **73rd Amendment** implementation. Also a rare instance of a training institute holding **both training and certification authority** — efficient, but see below.
**Criticism:** Dual (train + certify) recognition creates a **conflict-of-interest risk** in assessment independence — the standing critique of NCVET's dual-AB category.

## POLICY / REGULATORY CHANGE OF THE WEEK

### 8. EIA Notification 2006 amended — **SAEIA** and **SCEIA** created
**GS3 — Environment: EIA, conservation, environmental governance | GS2 — Statutory bodies, centre-state relations**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nodal** | **Ministry of Environment, Forest & Climate Change (MoEFCC)** |
| **Notified** | **13 July 2026** — **S.O. 3862(E)** (amends EIA Notification, 2006) and **S.O. 3863(E)** (composition) |
| **Draft stage** | Draft published **5 March 2026** for public comment |

**What changed**

- Creates, **for every State and UT**, a **Standing Authority on Environment Impact Assessment (SAEIA)** and a **Standing Committee on Environment Impact Appraisal (SCEIA)**.
- SAEIA will discharge the functions of a **non-functional SEIAA**; SCEIA those of a **non-functional SEAC**.
- Duration: **up to 6 months in the first instance, extendable once by up to 6 months** (i.e. **maximum 1 year**).
- **Stated rationale:** states reconstitute SEIAA/SEAC late; appraisal halts; pending Category-B proposals pile up and get bulk-transferred to the Centre, stretching timelines.

**Significance:** Category-B projects (the bulk of India's industrial/infra clearances) sit with state authorities. A standing fallback removes a genuine bottleneck and is a real ease-of-doing-business fix.
**Criticism (high-yield for GS3):** The draft left composition undefined ("ex-officio Members as deemed appropriate"); the final notification's composition raises questions about **domain expertise and independent (non-official) expert representation**, since ex-officio government members dominate. Critics argue it **centralises** appraisal, weakens the federal design of the 2006 notification, and converts a temporary fix into a standing parallel channel that reduces incentives for states to constitute proper SEIAAs. Sits alongside earlier criticised moves — post-facto clearances, exemption expansions and the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023.

## REPORTS & INDICES

### A. State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (**SOFI**) **2026**
**GS2 — Health, Nutrition, Poverty & Hunger; GS3 — Food security | Released 21 July 2026**

**Publishing bodies:** Jointly by **five UN agencies** — **FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO**.
**What it measures:** Global and country progress on **SDG 2 (Zero Hunger)** and SDG 3 nutrition targets — Prevalence of Undernourishment (PoU), food insecurity (FIES), cost and affordability of a healthy diet, stunting, wasting, overweight, anaemia.

**Global headline:** Global hunger declined to **~7.8% of world population in 2025**, but **~645 million people** still faced hunger — the world is **off track for Zero Hunger by 2030**.

**India's standing / key findings**

1. **Undernourishment fell sharply:** **142.5 million people (9.8% of population) in 2023–25**, down from **243.9 million (21.1%) in 2004–06**. India's improvement drove **Southern Asia's** decline (13.9% in 2021 → 9.3% in 2025).
2. **Affordability is the binding constraint:** **520.1 million Indians (35.5% of population)** could not afford a healthy diet in 2025; cost of a healthy diet reached **PPP $4.11 per person per day**, a **~48% rise since 2017**.
3. **Double burden of malnutrition:** child **stunting down to 32.9%**, **wasting 18.7%** (still among the world's highest); while **childhood overweight, adult obesity and anaemia in women continued to rise**.

**Exam angle:** Contrast SOFI's PoU (FAO methodology, based on dietary energy supply) with the **Global Hunger Index** (Concern Worldwide/Welthungerhilfe), which India officially disputes. Link to **PMGKAY, POSHAN 2.0, PM Poshan, Anaemia Mukt Bharat, eat-right initiatives**.

### B. **AAROH** — Annual Report on Mine Closure
**GS3 — Environment (land reclamation, ecological restoration); Mineral resources; Energy transition | Released 22 July 2026**

**Publishing body:** **Ministry of Coal**, compiled by the **Coal Controller Organisation (CCO)**.
**Released by:** Union Minister of Coal & Mines **G. Kishan Reddy**, with MoS (I/C) Science & Technology **Dr Jitendra Singh** and MoS Coal & Mines **Satish Chandra Dubey**.

**What it is:** **India's first nationally consolidated report** documenting **scientific mine closure and repurposing** of coal mines — a post-independence first.

**Key findings / features**

1. Documents **42 coal mines scientifically closed** in accordance with government-approved **Mine Closure Plans**.
2. Each mine profile carries **reclamation and post-mining land-use outcomes**, before-and-after visuals, and **QR-code-linked documentary videos**.
3. Frames closure as a **development opportunity** — ecological restoration, land reclamation, productive reuse (eco-parks, water bodies, solar, plantations) and community benefit, not merely site abandonment.
4. **International tie-up:** an implementation agreement was signed with Germany's **GIZ** (reported at ~**EUR 10 million** scale) for cooperation on scientific mine closure and sustainable repurposing.

**Exam angle:** Connects **just transition** in coal districts, **District Mineral Foundation (DMF)/PMKKKY**, the **Mine Closure Plan + escrow account** mechanism under coal-block agreements, and India's net-zero-by-2070 commitment. Strong GS3 material on "just transition away from coal".

### C. **Roadmap for Building India as a Leading Bioeconomy Powerhouse by 2035**
**GS3 — Science & Technology (biotechnology), Indian Economy, Growth & Employment | Released 16–18 July 2026**

**Publishing body:** **NITI Aayog** (NITI **Frontier Tech Hub**), in consultation with the **Department of Biotechnology (DBT)**, **Association of Biotechnology Led Enterprises (ABLE)**, industry and academia.
**Released by:** **Dr Jitendra Singh**, Minister of State (I/C), Ministry of Science & Technology, **18 July 2026**.

**What it sets out:** A **ten-year roadmap** to make India a leading global bioeconomy player.

**Key findings / recommendations**

1. **Targets:** scale India's bioeconomy to **USD 691 billion by 2035** and **USD 2.6 trillion by 2047**, creating **30+ million high-value jobs**.
2. **Six National BioMissions** proposed: **gene & cell therapies; climate-resilient agriculture; synthetic biology; disease surveillance; marine biotechnology; next-generation biopharmaceuticals**.
3. **Financing:** a **₹50,000-crore BioEconomy Growth Fund (2026–2035)** to bridge the lab-to-commercial-manufacturing "valley of death", plus **PLI-style incentives**, faster regulatory approvals and stronger IP protection.

**Exam angle:** Pair with **BioE3 Policy** (Biotechnology for Economy, Environment and Employment), the **National Biopharma Mission**, **BIRAC**, and India's bio-manufacturing/bio-foundry push. Note the recurring constraint: **regulatory clearance timelines (GEAC, CDSCO)** and biosafety governance.

### D. *Priority Policy Actions to Promote Healthier Diets and Food Environments for Children and Adolescents in India*
**GS2 — Health (NCDs, nutrition policy); GS3 — Food processing regulation | Released 22 July 2026**

**Publishing body:** the **ICMR–NIN**-led (ICMR–National Institute of Nutrition, Hyderabad) **"Let's Fix Our Food" Consortium**.
**What it does:** Sets out evidence-based policy priorities to prevent **diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs)** in children and adolescents.

**Key recommendations**

1. **Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labelling (FOPNL)** and **regulation of marketing of unhealthy foods to children**.
2. **Taxation of HFSS foods** (High in Fat, Salt and Sugar); promotion of **healthier dietary fats**, **salt reduction** and **low-sodium salt substitutes**.
3. **Healthy school food environments** and stronger **nutrition literacy**; supported by new state-level evidence on school food environments, food marketing and fiscal policy options.

**Exam angle:** Directly relevant to the pending **FSSAI FOPNL regulations** debate (Indian Nutrition Rating vs warning labels), **Eat Right India**, **GST rates on aerated/HFSS products**, and India's NCD burden. Note the double-burden framing shared with SOFI 2026.
