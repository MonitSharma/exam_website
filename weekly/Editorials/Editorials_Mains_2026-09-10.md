## 1. BRICS at Delhi — reform, or rivalry?

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — International Relations** (primary: bilateral/regional/global groupings, institutions of global governance, India's interests); GS3 — Economy (de-dollarisation, alternative payment systems)

**Editorial:** T.S. Tirumurti, "India's opportunity to put BRICS back together", *The Hindu* (Lead), 8 September 2026. The author is a former Indian Permanent Representative to the UN and a former BRICS Sherpa — attribute him as an insider-practitioner, which is why the piece carries weight.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

BRICS was founded as a **reform** project — to win emerging economies a larger voice inside existing global-governance institutions — but has drifted towards being a vehicle of **rivalry** with the West, driven largely by China's ambitions and by a rapid expansion India and Brazil resisted. Tirumurti argues the Delhi Summit is India's chance at course correction: protect the consensus principle, keep the grouping **"non-West" rather than "anti-West"**, and revive "reformed multilateralism".

### Key data & facts

- **18th BRICS Summit, New Delhi, 12–13 September 2026**; India is chair. India's chairship theme rests on four pillars — **Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation, Sustainability**.
- Membership is now **11** after Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Indonesia joined the original five, plus about **10 partner countries**.
- India hosted its **first BRICS Summit in 2012** (the 4th overall), theme "Global Stability, Security and Prosperity"; the Indian presidency fed into the creation of the **New Development Bank**.
- BRICS accounted for roughly **20% of world GDP in 2010** — the statistic that made the grouping strategically useful to China.
- Erosion of consensus: the **2020 Russian presidency issued the first non-consensus chair's statement** (on COVID-19); the same happened at the **BRICS Foreign Ministers' meeting in New Delhi in May 2026** because the new members could not agree.
- **De-dollarisation:** China's push for a common BRICS currency has had a lukewarm reception — members are uneasy about a renminbi-dominated unit and prefer **linking payment systems, CBDCs and national-currency settlement**. China is separately launching **mBridge**.
- Parallel-structure building: China's **World AI Cooperation Organisation** (Shanghai), alongside AIIB, BRI and the Digital Silk Road.
- **"Reformed multilateralism"** was first articulated by PM Modi at the **2018 Johannesburg Summit** and written into the **2019 Brasilia** document by India, Brazil and South Africa.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Expansion is a strength, not a dilution.** A grouping that now spans the Gulf, Africa, Southeast Asia and Latin America has far more claim to speak for the Global South than a five-member club; the price of representativeness is always some loss of cohesion.
- **The "reform vs rivalry" binary may be dated.** If the Bretton Woods institutions have proved unreformable — the IMF quota review has repeatedly stalled — building parallel capacity (NDB, CRA, payment rails) is not rivalry but a rational hedge.
- **India's own behaviour cuts both ways.** India benefits from BRICS-facilitated rupee-rouble and national-currency settlement even while resisting de-dollarisation rhetoric; critics would say the "non-West/anti-West" line is more comfortable than consistent.
- Tirumurti concedes the weaknesses himself: China "is hardly a middle power", and several deserving middle powers are absent — which limits how far the "Global South middle powers" framing can be pushed.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Consensus is BRICS' constitutional principle, and it is eroding.** Use the 2020 chair's statement and the May 2026 FMM as evidence; the sharp comparison is with the **SCO**, where majority views get pushed through, and the warning is against becoming **another SAARC**, paralysed by members' bilateral disputes.
2. **India runs a two-front brief inside BRICS** — pursuing institutional reform while simultaneously acting as a counterweight to China. The clinching illustration: China has **not supported the UNSC permanent-seat bids of India, Brazil and South Africa**, which exposed the limits of Chinese "reformism".
3. **BRICS as an instrument of multi-alignment, not alignment.** India's stake is a grouping that changes the *rules* of the order without demanding a *side* in it — a usable one-line thesis for any question on India's strategic autonomy.

## 2. Revising the Model BIT — and who gets a say in it

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — International Relations / Governance** (treaty-making, effect of policies of developed and developing countries, parliamentary oversight); **GS3 — Economy** (investment models, FDI, ease of doing business)

**Editorial:** "A bit of a reset, with a wider debate: revising India's Model BIT", op-ed, 9 September 2026. Reported alongside government confirmation that a **revamped Model BIT is headed for the Cabinet**.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India's 2015/16 Model BIT was drafted defensively, after a run of adverse investor claims, and tilted so far towards protecting the State's **right to regulate** that India has concluded only a handful of treaties on its basis. The op-ed accepts the case for a reset, but argues the debate has been almost entirely about **substance and arbitration procedure** while ignoring a second dimension — the **democratic deficit** in how investment treaties are negotiated and ratified.

### Key data & facts

- The **2015 Model BIT** (Cabinet-approved December 2015, commonly cited as the 2016 model) followed a wave of investor-State claims; India then **terminated roughly 75 BITs in 2016–17**.
- Very few new treaties have been signed on the 2015 template — the practical evidence that the model is unattractive to counterparties.
- Reported changes under consideration: cutting the mandatory exhaustion of **domestic remedies from five years to two** before an investor may go to international arbitration, plus stronger substantive protections and investment-facilitation provisions.
- Comparative practice on treaty scrutiny: the **UK and Australia** table negotiated treaty texts before Parliament prior to ratification; **Norway** and **Colombia** ran public consultations on their model BIT drafts. India itself **placed the 2015 draft in the public domain** for comment — a precedent the author says should be repeated.
- Carve-outs the revised model is expected to preserve: **public health, environment, taxation, labour and national security**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The 2015 model did its job.** Its restrictive design ended India's exposure to costly awards, and FDI inflows did not collapse in its absence — which suggests treaty protection is a weak determinant of investment compared with market size, infrastructure and regulatory quality.
- **Loosening the local-remedies rule invites regulatory chill.** A two-year window makes it far easier for investors to arbitrate against tax, environmental or land decisions — precisely the "public-interest regulation" the op-ed wants preserved.
- **On the democratic-deficit point:** treaty-making is executive power under **Article 253** read with the Union List; governments argue that opening negotiating texts to Parliament and the public weakens India's bargaining position and slows an already slow process. The counter to *that* is that India's own 2015 consultation did not visibly harm it.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **The central trade-off in one line:** a BIT must supply investors with **predictability** without buying it at the cost of the State's **regulatory sovereignty** — and the 2015 model over-corrected in one direction after 2016 over-corrected in the other.
2. **Treaty-making is a live accountability question in Indian polity.** Investment treaties bind land, environment, public services and tax policy for decades, yet face **no mandatory parliamentary scrutiny**. Pair with the standing demand to strengthen Departmentally Related Standing Committees — usable in GS2 answers on executive accountability, not just IR.
3. **Link BIT reform to the FTA push.** The revised model is meant to feed **investment chapters in CEPAs/CECAs/FTAs**, so the design choice ripples into every trade negotiation India is currently running — a good bridge sentence between GS2 and GS3.

## 3. The political cost of unconditional cash transfers

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Governance / Social Justice** (welfare schemes for vulnerable sections, issues in design and implementation); **GS3 — Economy** (government budgeting, fiscal sustainability, inclusive growth)

**Editorial:** "The political cost of unconditional cash transfers", *The Hindu* (Op-Ed), 9 September 2026.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Unconditional cash transfers to women have become both a welfare instrument and an electoral strategy across States, but their political returns are not automatic. The op-ed's key insight is that **targeting errors — and, just as importantly, the *perception* of unfairness — impose political costs**, because voters judge a government by benefits denied as much as by benefits received.

### Key data & facts

- States are expected to spend roughly **$18 billion on UCTs in 2025-26**, much of it directed at women.
- The scheme family: **Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thittam** (TN), **Lakshmir Bhandar** (WB), **Gruha Lakshmi** (Karnataka), **Mukhya Majhi Ladki Bahin Yojana** (Maharashtra).
- **Tamil Nadu case study:** ₹1,000/month promised to women heads of household before the 2021 election; fiscal constraints forced eligibility restrictions when the scheme actually launched in **September 2023**. Coverage is now about **1.31 crore women**, after **16,94,339 beneficiaries** were added in a second phase in December 2025. Annual outlay is in the **₹13,000 crore range** in 2025-26 (up from about ₹7,000 crore in 2023-24). *[Note: one widely circulated coaching summary reports "13 crore women" for Tamil Nadu — that is a transcription error; the State's total population is about 7.2 crore. Use 1.31 crore.]*
- These schemes partly advance **SDG 5.4** (recognition of unpaid domestic and care work).
- Because informal-sector incomes are unobservable, targeting relies on **proxy means tests** — land ownership, electricity consumption, household assets — which generate both **inclusion and exclusion errors**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Unconditionality is the point.** Conditional transfers impose compliance costs on exactly the households least able to bear them, and evidence from cash-transfer programmes globally shows recipients do not systematically spend on "temptation goods". Adding conditions re-imports the paternalism that DBT was meant to remove.
- **"Freebie" framing is politically loaded.** Corporate tax concessions and loan write-offs are rarely subjected to the same fiscal-sustainability scrutiny as ₹1,000 to a woman-headed household.
- **The op-ed's own remedy has a hole.** Its exemplar of a self-selecting conditional programme, the **Midday Meal Scheme**, works because school enrolment is observable and near-universal — a condition that does not transfer to income-support schemes.
- On fiscal risk, the counter-view is that transfers of this size are consumption-supporting in a demand-constrained economy, and that the real fiscal problem is States' capital-expenditure compression, not the transfers themselves.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Targeting is a trade-off, not a technical problem.** Economics favours narrow targeting; politics rewards breadth. The sharper the targeting, the greater the exposure to **exclusion grievances** — so administrative efficiency and political sustainability pull in opposite directions. This is an excellent framing line for any welfare-design question.
2. **Perceived error is as costly as actual error.** Citizens who consider themselves deserving but are formally ineligible experience the scheme as **discrimination by the State** — which converts a welfare programme into a legitimacy problem. Links directly to the **exclusion-error critique of Aadhaar-based targeting** and to the universalism-vs-targeting debate.
3. **The ratchet effect.** Once households build recurring transfers into their budgets, withdrawal is politically impossible, so **competitive welfarism converts a discretionary outlay into a permanent committed liability** — squeezing capex, health and education. Use with State fiscal-deficit and committed-expenditure data.

## 4. Reclaiming universities — governance, not indiscipline

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Governance** (institutional autonomy, education policy, statutory bodies); **GS1 — Indian Society** (youth, social change); GS4 overlap on institutional trust

**Editorial:** "Reclaiming universities through trust, student voice", *The Hindu* (Lead), 9 September 2026.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Campus protests are being read as a discipline problem when they are a **governance failure**. Indian universities run on a **"control model"** — Vice-Chancellor-centric, inaccessible, over-regulated and underfunded — and the author proposes a **"liberty model"** built on trust and a genuine student role in academic, administrative and financial decisions.

### Key data & facts

- **News peg:** the NALSAR convocation controversy. The **Bar Council of India directed State Bar Councils to keep the enrolment of NALSAR's 2026 batch in abeyance**; the Supreme Court under **CJI Surya Kant** stayed the order, holding that students have a right to protest and that the BCI had no disciplinary control over them, and separately **reduced the practice requirement for the judicial services examination from three years to one**. The BCI chairman subsequently withdrew the order.
- **Public spending on education is about 4–4.1% of GDP**, against the **NEP 2020 target of 6%** — with higher education receiving the smallest share.
- Academic freedom has **no explicit mention in the Indian Constitution**, unlike **Article 5(3) of the German Basic Law** (*Wissenschaftsfreiheit*), and comparable provisions in Japan, South Africa, Portugal and Spain, and **Article 137 of Hong Kong's Basic Law**.
- Institutional example cited: **Aligarh Muslim University** gives students a statutory role in Vice-Chancellor selection.
- Listed symptoms: hostel shortages, vacant faculty posts and poorly paid guest faculty, ideologically driven recruitment, recurrent **paper leaks**, and contested VC appointments including a documented conflict-of-interest case.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Universities are not democracies, and student bodies are not electorates.** Academic standards, curriculum design and faculty appointments involve expertise judgements; giving transient student cohorts a decisive voice risks capture by organised campus politics rather than the median student.
- **Autonomy without accountability has its own record.** Publicly funded institutions are answerable for outcomes; regulators exist because self-governance has sometimes failed on quality, finances and safety.
- **The comparison to the Gurukul system is rhetorical rather than analytical** — it was autonomous but also small, exclusionary and unfunded by the State, so it is a weak template for a mass higher-education system.
- On the BCI episode specifically, the regulator's defenders argued professional bodies must be able to assess fitness to practise — the Court's answer was that this power does not extend to disciplining students *as students*.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Frame protest as a feedback failure.** Where internal grievance channels are closed — VCs who hold no open houses, no student representation in statutory bodies — dissent externalises and escalates. This is a **governance-design** argument, not a law-and-order one.
2. **Autonomy is meaningless without funding.** An institution dependent on year-to-year grants, with vacant sanctioned posts, cannot exercise the freedom the statute nominally gives it — connect the **4.1% vs 6% of GDP** gap to the autonomy debate rather than treating them as separate issues.
3. **India has a constitutional gap on academic freedom.** It is protected only derivatively, via **Article 19(1)(a)** and the Court's reading of institutional autonomy; the comparative material (Germany, Japan, South Africa) is high-value value-addition for any question on knowledge institutions.
4. **Merit in appointments is the upstream variable.** The op-ed's link between compromised VC selection and downstream failures — including paper leaks — is a useful causal chain: *appointment integrity → institutional credibility → student trust*.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS2 — International Relations**)

> **"BRICS was created to reform the international order, not to replace it."** In the light of the grouping's recent expansion and internal divisions, **examine** the challenges before India in keeping BRICS a "non-West" rather than an "anti-West" platform. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Directive note:** *Examine* asks you to investigate the issue in detail and probe its components — you must break the challenge into identifiable parts and weigh each, not merely list problems. Because the quoted premise is embedded, you should also indicate briefly whether you accept it. Do not write this as a "BRICS achievements and challenges" answer; the axis is **India's specific difficulty in holding a particular line**.

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Introduction (2–3 lines).** Locate BRICS' founding purpose — greater voice for emerging economies inside existing institutions of global economic governance — and state the current tension: an 11-member grouping meeting in New Delhi on 12–13 September 2026 under India's chairship, with the reform agenda competing against a contestation agenda.

2. **Challenge 1 — the arithmetic of expansion.** Six new members (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Indonesia) plus ~10 partners have widened the grouping's geographic claim but imported unresolved **bilateral rivalries** into it. Evidence of institutional strain: the **first non-consensus chair's statement in 2020** and the failure to agree at the **May 2026 Foreign Ministers' meeting in New Delhi**. Risk to name explicitly: **the SAARC precedent** (bilateral disputes paralysing a regional body) and **the SCO precedent** (majoritarian decision-making displacing consensus).

3. **Challenge 2 — asymmetry with China, and India's two-front brief.** China's economic weight makes it the agenda-setter; it treats BRICS as a counterpoise to the West and a Global South platform via **BRICS-Plus**. India must simultaneously pursue reform *and* balance China inside the same room. The decisive illustration: **China's non-support for the UNSC permanent-seat bids of India, Brazil and South Africa**, which showed that Chinese support for "reform" is selective. Add the parallel-institution problem — AIIB, BRI, the Digital Silk Road, the new **World AI Cooperation Organisation** — which build *around* the existing order rather than reforming it.

4. **Challenge 3 — external pressure narrowing India's room.** Punitive **US tariffs on India and Brazil**, proposed US legislation penalising importers of Russian oil, strain in the Quad and in India–US ties all raise the political cost to India of *not* tilting anti-West — the paradox being that Western coercion is itself the strongest recruiter for the anti-West camp. Handle this factually and without partisan framing.

5. **Challenge 4 — the financial-architecture question, and the way forward.** De-dollarisation is the sharpest test: India resists a **renminbi-dominated common currency** but supports **linked payment systems, CBDCs and national-currency settlement** — a distinction worth stating precisely, because it separates *reducing dependence* from *building a rival bloc*. Conclude with India's levers: defend the **consensus principle**, push **"reformed multilateralism"** (Johannesburg 2018 → Brasilia 2019 → Delhi 2026), consolidate the **NDB** as a credible development lender, and position BRICS as the only organised platform of **Global South middle powers** — which serves India's core interest in **multi-alignment**. A balanced closing line should concede the counter-view: if Bretton Woods reform stays frozen, the pressure to build parallel institutions will keep growing regardless of India's preference.

**Value-addition to sprinkle in:** first Indian summit 2012; NDB and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement; India's 2026 chairship pillars (Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation, Sustainability); mBridge; BRICS' ~20% share of world GDP in 2010 as the baseline for its original bargaining claim.
