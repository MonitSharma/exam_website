## 1. NFHS-6 reveals progress amid nutrition challenges
**Source:** The Hindu, Lead (19 Jun 2026) · *GS1 (society — population, role of women) + GS2 (social sector — health & nutrition)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** NFHS-6 (data collected 2023–24) is a tale of two Indias: strong gains in healthcare *access* — institutional births, skilled attendance, immunisation — but stubbornly poor *feeding practices* and diet quality. The editorial's central claim is that **better healthcare alone cannot fix undernutrition**; the binding constraint has shifted from medical access to food systems, behaviour and women's time.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- **Stunting (under-5):** fell from **35.5% (NFHS-5) to 29.3% (NFHS-6)** — welcome, but still roughly one in three children. (Cross-checked against *Business Standard*/*Down To Earth* reporting.)
- **Access gains:** institutional births ~**90%** (public facilities ~**58%**); skilled birth attendance ~**91%**; antenatal care ~**95%**; full vaccination (12–23 months) ~**87%**, driven by ASHA/AWW/ANM frontline workers (private facilities only ~3%).
- **The feeding gap (the paradox):** only ~**50%** of newborns breastfed within the **first hour**; only ~**60%** of 6–8-month-olds get timely solid/semi-solid food; only ~**15%** (15.3%) of children **6–23 months** receive an **adequate diet** (cross-checked).
- **Nutrition-transition trap:** households spending less on cereals, more on dairy/processed foods — an "illusion of dietary diversity"; an ICMR-NIN-compliant diet (pulses, millets, fruit, vegetables, animal foods) is **unaffordable** for many.

**The other side of the debate.** A single survey round is a snapshot, not a trend; some of the "feeding" failures reflect cultural timing (e.g., delayed *annaprasana*) rather than pure deprivation, so the fix is behaviour-change communication, not only income. Defenders of the current model note real, measurable wins (stunting down, near-universal institutional delivery) and argue continuity, not a strategy overhaul, is what's needed.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- **"Access ≠ outcomes"** thesis: medical access has improved faster than nutrition because the latter depends on diet quality, sanitation, and **maternal time poverty** (women in unpaid informal labour, no crèches) — frame crèches as *social infrastructure*, not just childcare.
- **First 1,000 days** as the critical window; flag the **data gap** — NFHS-6 lacks disaggregated 0–2 data even though stunting peaks in year two.
- Link to **POSHAN Abhiyaan** (shift emphasis from rehabilitation to *prevention*), Anganwadi strengthening, and **multisectoral convergence** (water, sanitation, Gram Sabha agenda).

## 2. Reconnect public health with people's needs
**Source:** The Hindu, Lead (24 Jun 2026) · *GS2 (governance — management of the social sector / Universal Health Coverage)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** The editorial warns that India's pursuit of UHC has drifted toward **"wellness" branding and digital databases** — Health & Wellness Centres (HWCs), the Ayushman Bharat Digital Health Mission (ABDHM) — while the hard work of strengthening the three-tier delivery system lags. It argues the "wellness" turn **individualises** health (personal lifestyle choices) and risks diverting attention from measurable **population-health** needs.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- India's healthcare problem is rooted in the **unaffordability of private care** and the **inadequate quality of public facilities**; strengthening **Sub-Centres (SCs), PHCs and CHCs** — the backbone of the three-tier system — is the core unfinished task.
- The "wellness" concept expanded "health" beyond absence of disease to mental/social/spiritual/environmental well-being — holistic, but **hard to measure** (no agreed population-level wellness metric), which weakens evidence-based governance.
- ABDHM (ABHA cards, electronic health records, facility/professional registries) builds **digital scaffolding**, but databases are not the same as delivery.

**The other side of the debate.** Wellness and prevention genuinely reduce the long-run burden of **non-communicable/lifestyle diseases**, and digital infrastructure (ABDHM) improves portability, targeting and continuity of care — so the "wellness + digital" push is a complement to, not a substitute for, primary-care strengthening. The real fault would be treating them as either/or.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- **"Measurability is governance"**: policy needs measurable outcomes (disease burden, utilisation, nutrition, mortality) — a caution against shifting from population metrics to fuzzy "well-being."
- Pair with NFHS-6 (#1): both point to the same gap — **inputs/access have grown faster than outcomes**; the missing layer is functional primary care + nutrition.
- Solution framing: **comprehensive primary health care** (preventive + promotive + curative + rehabilitative) over selective/"wellness-only" approaches; tie to low public health spend and high out-of-pocket expenditure.

## 3. The real barriers to trade are no longer tariffs
**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (19 Jun 2026) · *GS3 (economy — external sector, trade) + GS2 (IR — effect of developed-country policies on India)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** As average tariffs fall worldwide, **Non-Tariff Barriers (NTBs)** — standards, quality-control orders, testing/certification, customs procedures — have become the real determinants of market access. The piece argues India's future trade gains depend less on cutting tariffs and more on **regulatory cooperation and mutual recognition of standards**.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- Despite FTAs with **ASEAN, Japan and South Korea**, Indian exporters face NTBs — lengthy pharma approvals (Japan), complex registration (ASEAN), restrictive customs — so India's **FTA utilisation rate** stays well below that of developed economies.
- India is itself shifting from tariffs toward regulatory tools — **Quality Control Orders (QCOs)**, product standards, import regulations, domestic-manufacturing support.
- **New-generation deals address NTBs:** the **India–UAE CEPA** uses mutual recognition of standards; the **India–EFTA TEPA** goes further with mutual-recognition agreements, streamlined conformity assessment, and legally binding regulatory-cooperation commitments.

**The other side of the debate.** Many NTBs are **legitimate** — protecting consumers, public health and the environment — so "reducing NTBs" must not become a euphemism for diluting safety/standards. And whoever *sets* global standards gains leverage; for India, building its own credible standards/testing ecosystem may matter more than simply accepting others' norms ("hidden protectionism" cuts both ways).

**Mains value-addition points.**

- **Standards as strategic power**: regulatory frameworks are now instruments of economic statecraft — connect to "weaponised interdependence" and the EU's regulatory clout.
- For India: invest in **conformity-assessment infrastructure, mutual-recognition agreements, and standards diplomacy** to convert paper FTAs into real exports.
- Frame QCOs as a **double-edged tool** — quality upgrade and *Atmanirbhar* shield, but a compliance burden on MSMEs if poorly sequenced.

## 4. India's patchy industrial climate strategy
**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (24 Jun 2026) · *GS3 (environment — climate change & sustainable development; economy — industry)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Reconciling Make-in-India / Viksit Bharat 2047 with **net-zero by 2070** requires industrial decarbonisation, but India's policy net has a hole: a large slice of industrial emissions sits in an unmeasured **"non-specific industries"** category outside any efficiency or carbon-trading scheme. Without better classification and data, the green transition stays incomplete.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- Per India's **Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1)**, **>20%** of national emissions come from industry — **~13%** from manufacturing & construction (fuel combustion) and **~9%** from industrial processes & product use.
- **Identified** industrial sub-sectors account for slightly **>55%** of sectoral emissions; **>40%** fall under the broad, undefined **"non-specific industries"** bucket — largely outside policy.
- Existing tools: the **Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT)** scheme, transitioning into the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)**, covering aluminium, cement, fertilizers, iron & steel, petrochemicals, refining, pulp & paper, textiles, chlor-alkali — but *not* the non-specific category.

**The other side of the debate.** Targeting only large, "identified" energy-intensive sectors is a defensible **80/20 start** — they are the biggest, most tractable emitters, and over-broadening monitoring could burden small/informal units that lack capacity. Decarbonisation must also be sequenced against **development and jobs**, given India's low historical and per-capita emissions and the "right to develop."

**Mains value-addition points.**

- **"You can't manage what you don't measure"**: better sectoral classification, granular emissions data and transparency are prerequisites for targeted mitigation — a data-governance argument, not just an environmental one.
- Connect PAT → **CCTS / carbon markets** as India's shift from command-and-control to **market-based** climate instruments; mention green hydrogen, energy efficiency, and a just transition.
- Use the **CBDR-RC** lens: India balances net-zero-2070 ambition with development needs — decarbonise *without* deindustrialising.

## GS Mains practice question (this week's dominant theme)

> **Q.** "India's recent gains in healthcare *access* have outpaced its gains in nutrition and population-health *outcomes*." **Examine** the structural and behavioural factors behind this access–outcome gap, and suggest measures to bridge it. **(15 marks, 250 words)**
>
> *Directive — "examine": investigate the issue in detail, identify the underlying factors, and present a reasoned, evidence-backed view (lighter on judgement than "critically examine").*
> *Maps to: GS2 (social sector — management of services relating to Health & Nutrition) + GS1 (society — population, role of women).*

**Model-answer skeleton (5 points).**

1. **Intro / frame (2–3 lines).** Open with the NFHS-6 paradox — institutional births ~90%, full immunisation ~87%, stunting down to ~29%, *yet* only ~15% of 6–23-month-olds get an adequate diet. Thesis: access is necessary but not sufficient for outcomes.
2. **Structural factors.** Unaffordable/uneven quality of public care (weak SCs/PHCs/CHCs); diet-affordability gap (ICMR-NIN diet costly, processed food cheap → nutrition-transition trap); water, sanitation and poverty deprivations; thin public health spending and high out-of-pocket expenditure.
3. **Behavioural & social factors.** Late breastfeeding/complementary feeding; cultural timing (*annaprasana*); **maternal time poverty** (women in unpaid informal work, absent crèches); "wellness"/digital-health drift that individualises responsibility and underweights delivery.
4. **Measures to bridge the gap.** Strengthen comprehensive primary health care over selective wellness branding; POSHAN Abhiyaan shift from rehabilitation to **prevention** in the first 1,000 days; culturally grounded behaviour-change communication; **crèches as social infrastructure** + engaging men in childcare; multisectoral convergence (nutrition on the Gram Sabha agenda, WASH) and better disaggregated 0–2 data.
5. **Conclusion.** "Better healthcare brought us this far; better food systems, empowered mothers and convergent community action take us further." Tie to demographic-dividend and Viksit Bharat @2047 — human capital, not just hospitals.
