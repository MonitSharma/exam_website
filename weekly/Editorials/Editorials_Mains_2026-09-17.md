## 1. Mineral taxation after MADA — has Parliament overturned a judgment or merely limited it?

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Polity & Federalism** (functions and responsibilities of the Union and the States, issues and challenges pertaining to the federal structure, separation of powers, parliament and state legislatures); **GS3 — Economy** (mobilisation of resources, government budgeting, mineral and energy resources, industrial policy)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2026 inserts a new **Section 9D** barring any State from levying "any tax, cess or other levy, by whatever name called" on mineral rights or mineral-bearing land except on conditions prescribed by the Centre — and retrospectively deems uncollected State levies invalid "at all material times". Commentators writing in *The Hindu* and in legal journals argue this unwinds the fiscal entitlement the Supreme Court's nine-judge Bench recognised for mineral-rich States in **Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (2024)**, and that while the Centre may lawfully *limit* State taxing power under Entry 50 of List II, it cannot by ordinary statute reach Entry 49 or nullify an adjudicated liability. The defence is equally coherent: a single national mineral-tax profile is what makes marginal critical-mineral projects bankable and cuts an import bill already above ₹10 lakh crore a year.

### Key data & facts

- **MADA v. SAIL, (2024) 10 SCC 1** — nine-judge Bench, **8:1**, 25 July 2024. Held **royalty is not a tax** but a contractual payment to the lessor, and **overruled** *India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1990), which for three decades had been read as denying States any levy referable to mineral rights.
- Two heads of State competence located by the majority: **Entry 50, List II** (taxes on mineral rights, *expressly* "subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development") and **Entry 49, List II** (taxes on lands — which the Court held could reach mineral-bearing land using mineral value or yield as the *measure*). **Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissented**, warning a patchwork of State levies would fragment the national mineral market.
- **Consequential order, August 2024:** relief was *not* made purely prospective. States may recover dues from **1 April 2005**; interest and penalties on amounts predating 25 July 2024 were waived; payment staggered over **twelve years from 1 April 2026**. Aggregate exposure estimated well over **₹1 lakh crore** — a windfall for Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and West Bengal, a retrospective liability of the same size for miners.
- **Legislative timeline of the 2026 Act:** introduced in the Lok Sabha **10 August 2026**, passed by the Lok Sabha **12 August** reportedly without debate, by the Rajya Sabha **13 August**, presidential assent **18 August 2026**.
- **Three levers of the Act:** (i) new **Section 9D** conditioning all State mineral levies on central prescription; (ii) the Entry 54 List I declaration in **Section 2** expanded from "mines" to "**regulation of mines and mineral-bearing lands**", with "mineral-bearing land" newly defined and rule-making power vested in the Centre via Section 13; (iii) a **non-obstante clause** invalidating uncollected levies "at all material times" while protecting amounts already collected from refund.
- **Government's case:** States still receive roughly **88–90%** of mining-sector revenue through royalty, District Mineral Foundation and National Mineral Exploration Trust contributions; annual mineral import bill exceeds **₹10 lakh crore**.
- **Reform lineage:** 2015 amendment (auctions) → 2021 (captive production liberalised) → 2023 and 2025 (critical minerals opened to private participation) → 2026 (fiscal uniformity).

### The other side / counter-argument

Three defences of the Act are serious. **First**, Entry 50 *itself* contains the limitation clause — the MADA majority expressly acknowledged Parliament's power to circumscribe State taxing power on mineral rights, so the Centre is arguably doing precisely what the Court said it could. **Second**, Nagarathna J.'s dissent anticipated the policy problem: cascading, unpredictable State cesses layered on royalty raise input costs for steel, aluminium, cement, power and the battery sector, and make lithium, cobalt, graphite and rare-earth projects — whose economics are marginal and long-dated — unbankable. Tax certainty is a precondition for exploration capital. **Third**, States retain the overwhelming share of mining revenue through royalty and DMF, so the Act constrains an increment, not the base.

The critics' rebuttal is sharper on constitutional method than on policy. **Entry 49 contains no subordination clause**, and MADA itself held that Union regulatory power under Entry 54 does not extend to Entry 49 — so a statutory *definition* of "mineral-bearing land" cannot by itself redraw the constitutional distribution of competence; that is the classic signature of **colourable legislation**. Leaving the contours of "mineral-bearing land" to subordinate rules raises **excessive delegation**. And the settled principle is that a legislature must **remove the basis** of a judgment, not merely override its result: MADA rested on the allocation of competence under Entries 49 and 50, which the 2026 Act does not purport to alter. The no-refund proviso also invites an **Article 14** challenge, since it classifies States and assessees purely by the *timing* of collection. Jharkhand and Kerala have reportedly raised federalism objections; **Article 131** suits and **Article 32/226** writs may follow.

### Mains value-addition points

- **The sharpest framing:** this is not "Centre versus States" but a question about **the limits of Parliament's power to alter the legal consequences of an adjudicated determination**. Use it wherever a question asks about legislative overruling of judgments — it pairs with the *Madras Bar Association*, *Indira Sawhney* and NJAC line of cases.
- **Entry 50 versus Entry 49 is the whole answer.** On mineral *rights*, the Centre stands on defensible ground. On mineral-bearing *land* and on retrospectivity, it does not. An answer that treats the Act as uniformly valid or uniformly invalid misses the point; the marks are in the split.
- **Resource-curse inversion:** the most affected States — Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh — are among India's poorer States, and they carry the environmental and displacement costs of extraction. Curtailing their fiscal claim while the benefits accrue downstream is the **resource-justice** argument, usable in GS2 (social justice) and GS3 (inclusive growth) alike.
- **Passing a Bill affecting State finances without debate**, and within three sitting days across both Houses, is itself a usable illustration for questions on **parliamentary scrutiny and the decline of deliberative process**.
- **Critical-minerals framing:** note the tension between two legitimate national goals — reducing import dependence in lithium/cobalt/rare earths, and honouring fiscal federalism. The way forward suggested in commentary is a **negotiated ceiling rate** with the GST Council as the institutional template, rather than a unilateral central lock.

## 2. Aadhaar for voter rolls — efficiency case, constitutional constraint

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Polity & Governance** (Election Commission and its powers, Representation of the People Act, citizenship, statutory bodies, e-governance and its limitations); GS2 — Rights Issues (privacy, exclusion)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Writing in *The Indian Express*, **R.S. Sharma** — former Secretary to the Government of India and founding Director-General of UIDAI — argues that the **Special Intensive Revision (SIR)** of electoral rolls should abandon door-to-door paper verification in favour of **voluntary Aadhaar-linked face authentication**, with the Ministry of Home Affairs' citizenship-determination role reserved for the small number of cases that analytics flag as genuine outliers. His case is one of cost, scale and demonstrated reliability; the constraint he concedes is that Aadhaar cannot, by statute, prove citizenship.

### Key data & facts

- **Constitutional and statutory base:** electoral-roll preparation is an Election Commission function under **Article 324**, operationalised through the **Representation of the People Act, 1950**. **Section 9 of the Aadhaar Act, 2016** expressly bars Aadhaar from being treated as proof of **citizenship or domicile** — it establishes identity and residence only.
- **SIR scale:** rolling out in phases since **June 2025**; over **13 crore names** deleted nationally. The exercise mobilises roughly **10 lakh public servants** (largely schoolteachers) at a cost of thousands of crores per cycle.
- **Supreme Court, 27 May:** upheld the SIR exercise but held that final **citizenship determination rests with the MHA under the Citizenship Act, 1955**, not with the Election Commission.
- **Face-authentication track record cited by the author:** over **10 crore** DigiYatra face-authentication transactions across 100+ airports; **1.47 crore** pensioners authenticating annually via **Jeevan Pramaan**; a cumulative **500 crore** government-wide Aadhaar face authentications. Aadhaar-based de-duplication is credited with roughly **₹2.7 lakh crore** in avoided leakages.
- **Age-inference argument:** biometrics are not captured below age five, so anyone enrolled before roughly 2013 was at least five then and is at least 18 now — sufficient, the author says, to establish voting age without a birth certificate.
- **Documentary alternatives are thin:** passports are held by under **8% of Indians**, and the MEA clarified in June that a passport is a travel document, not proof of citizenship.
- **Cautionary precedents the author himself cites:** the multipurpose **National Identity Card** pilot was abandoned; the **Assam NRC** excluded **19 lakh** people amid enormous distress; the **National Population Register** settled for enumerating "usual residents" rather than attempting a field citizenship test.

### The other side / counter-argument

Two structural objections cut against the efficiency case.

**The legal one:** *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017 on privacy; 2018 on Aadhaar) confined **mandatory** Aadhaar use to **Section 7** welfare subsidies. Extending Aadhaar into the electoral domain — even on a voluntary basis — plausibly requires **fresh legislative clarity** rather than executive practice or an EC circular. There is also a purpose-limitation problem: a credential designed as a bare identity token acquires, once linked to the roll, a political function its enabling statute never contemplated.

**The practical one:** "voluntary" linkage at scale has repeatedly produced **de facto compulsion** through administrative convenience — the manual route survives on paper but withers in practice. And biometric **authentication failure rates**, well documented in welfare delivery among manual labourers, the elderly and those with worn fingerprints, would reproduce exactly the exclusion the author wants to avoid, only through a different mechanism and with less visibility than a door-to-door enumerator refusing a name.

A third, quieter objection: the author's age-inference argument establishes that a person is *over 18*, not that they are a *citizen* — which is the very question the Supreme Court said belongs to the MHA. Efficiency in verifying identity does not resolve the eligibility question the SIR was designed around.

### Mains value-addition points

- **The one-line thesis to carry into an answer:** Aadhaar can make the roll *cleaner* (de-duplication, ghost removal) but cannot make it *correct* on citizenship — those are different problems, and conflating them is the core analytical error in the debate.
- **Exclusion symmetry:** manual SIR excludes through documentary burden; biometric SIR excludes through authentication failure. Both are exclusion errors; the second is simply harder to see and harder to appeal. Use this whenever a question asks about technology in welfare or rights delivery.
- **Institutional line:** *Article 324* (EC) → *RP Act 1950* (rolls) → *Citizenship Act 1955* + MHA (citizenship) → *Aadhaar Act 2016, s.9* (identity only). Reproducing this chain correctly signals command of the statutory architecture.
- **Compare NPR and NRC** precisely — NPR enumerates all "usual residents"; NRC (piloted in Assam) attempts to identify citizens. Examiners reward the distinction; most candidates blur it.
- **Design fix worth proposing:** an **opt-in linkage with a statutory non-derogation clause** — a legislated guarantee that non-linkage cannot be a ground for deletion, plus mandatory publication of authentication-failure rates disaggregated by district and age. Accountability through transparency, not through prohibition.

## 3. Twenty-five years after 9/11 — India's counter-terrorism doctrine and the Af-Pak residue

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Internal Security** (linkages between development and spread of extremism, role of external state and non-state actors, challenges to internal security through communication networks, money-laundering and terror financing, security agencies and their mandate); **GS2 — International Relations** (India and its neighbourhood, effect of policies of developed countries on India's interests)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Two pieces this week — a *Hindu* lead on India's evolving counter-terror strategy and an *Indian Express* op-ed by **Anju Gupta**, a security analyst and former Director-General of Police — converge on the same claim from different directions: the American-led "**Global War on Terror**" framing has wound down doctrinally, but the **Af-Pak-origin threat to India has neither ended nor changed character**. The *Hindu* piece traces India's own shift from diplomatic restraint to **active deterrence** and argues the new **PRAHAAR** framework must be judged by whether it prevents attacks, not by whether it retaliates for them. The implicit warning in both: India's threat cycle was never synchronised with Washington's, so India's posture cannot track shifts in US doctrine.

### Key data & facts

- **Origins:** Al Qaeda founded by Osama bin Laden in **Peshawar, around May 1988**, out of the foreign-fighter mobilisation of the Afghan jihad against Soviet occupation (1979–89), facilitated in part by a fatwa from **Abdullah Azzam** after his move to Islamabad in **1981**.
- **9/11:** roughly **3,000 killed**, including 2,600 Americans. Earlier AQ-linked attacks: **1993 WTC bombing**, **1998 US embassy bombings** (Kenya, Tanzania), **2000 USS Cole**.
- **Arc of the WoT:** 2001 US-led invasion of Afghanistan, around **50 countries** contributing under US/NATO-led efforts → **2003 Iraq**, drawing only British, Australian and Polish troops (the narrowing of consensus) → **2011** bin Laden killed at Abbottabad → **2013–19** AQI morphs into ISIS and the territorial caliphate is defeated by **March 2019** → **2021** US withdrawal, Taliban 2.0.
- **India's own timeline predates it:** Rajiv Gandhi's assassination (**1991**), **1993** Mumbai serial blasts, **1998** Coimbatore blasts, the **1999 IC-814 hijacking** (which forced the release of Masood Azhar), the **December 2001** Parliament attack and **Operation Parakram**, then Akshardham, Varanasi, Pune, Delhi, and **26/11** in 2008 — after which India again declined military retaliation.
- **The shift to active deterrence:** **Uri (September 2016)** → surgical strikes across the LoC; **Pulwama (February 2019)** → Balakot air strikes; **Pahalgam (April 2025)** → **Operation Sindoor**, launched **6–7 May 2025**, followed by a limited India–Pakistan conflict; **Red Fort attack, November 2025**.
- **PRAHAAR** — India's **National Counter-Terrorism Policy and Strategy**, launched **February 2026**, built on *whole-of-government* and *whole-of-society* approaches, with four priorities: dismantling networks, preventive military capability, de-radicalisation and social integration, and disrupting terror financing.
- **Multilateral traction:** a paragraph on the **Pahalgam attack** was included in the **BRICS New Delhi Declaration** (12 September 2026); India has pressed the same line through the **SCO**. **UNSC Resolution 1373 (2001)** remains the anchor for international obligations on terror financing and legal cooperation.
- **Structural shift in the threat:** from centralised organisations to **decentralised networks and lone actors**, using encrypted communication, digital propaganda, **drones**, cyber operations and increasingly **AI**.

### The other side / counter-argument

The considered scepticism about active deterrence runs along three lines. **First, deterrence is unverifiable.** Attacks that do not happen cannot be attributed to a strike that did, so the claim that Balakot or Sindoor "restored deterrence" is an inference, not a finding — and the Pahalgam and Red Fort attacks sit awkwardly with it. **Second, escalation risk is real and asymmetric.** Operation Parakram's stalemate was not a failure of will but a recognition that nuclear deterrence and international pressure narrow the space for conventional retaliation; each successful crossing of that threshold lowers the bar for the next crisis, on both sides. **Third, the doctrine is heavily weighted toward the kinetic quarter of PRAHAAR.** De-radicalisation, social integration and terror-financing disruption are the three legs that receive the least budget and the least public accounting, yet the *Hindu* piece itself argues success will be measured by prevention, which is overwhelmingly their domain.

Anju Gupta's sharper point is analytical rather than operational: India faces a **protracted, state-linked regional phenomenon rooted in the 1980s**, only loosely coupled to the rise-and-decline cycle of the global WoT narrative. Groups that assumed the identities of **Lashkar-e-Taiba** and **Jaish-e-Mohammad** emerged from Pakistani mobilisation for the Afghan jihad and have been sustained as instruments of state policy. Reading India's threat through an American doctrinal lens — "the war is over" — is therefore a category error.

### Mains value-addition points

- **The distinction that earns marks:** "the *global war* has ended" and "the *threat* has ended" are different propositions. Terrorism has not declined; it has **decentralised** — from hierarchy to network to lone actor. Capability per actor falls; attribution difficulty and frequency rise.
- **Judge PRAHAAR against its own four pillars.** A strong answer scores the doctrine leg by leg: network dismantling (strongest), preventive capability (demonstrated), de-radicalisation (thinnest institutionally), terror financing (dependent on FATF-type external cooperation). This structure works for almost any internal-security question on a government framework.
- **Technology as a double edge:** encrypted communication and AI lower the cost of planning and propaganda; the same technologies raise detection capability. The deciding variable is **intelligence-agency capacity and inter-agency fusion**, not the technology itself — hence NATGRID, NIA and MAC coordination are the operative institutions to name.
- **The double-standards argument** — India's consistent multilateral position that terrorism cannot be sorted into "good" and "bad" — is the bridge from GS3 to GS2 IR. The BRICS declaration paragraph on Pahalgam is the current, citable instance.
- **Development–extremism linkage** is in the syllabus wording itself. Note that the same week's youth-protest commentary (below) traces LWE's growth to unaddressed grievance — a useful internal cross-reference if a question asks about extremism broadly rather than cross-border terrorism specifically.

## 4. Access to AI as a security question — can a grouping substitute for a supply chain?

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Science & Technology** (developments and applications, indigenisation of technology, cyber security, awareness in IT); **GS3 — Economy** (critical minerals, supply-chain resilience); GS2 — IR (institutions of global governance) as a secondary hook

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

*The Hindu*'s editorial after the 18th BRICS Summit argues that **timely and comprehensive access to AI is now a national-security question for developing countries**, and that trade disputes should not be allowed to choke its spread. Its prescription: BRICS should become a forum for making **defensive AI capabilities broadly and cheaply available**, on the premise that AI's risks should not be distributed along a "haves and have-nots" hierarchy. The editorial reads India as taking a middle path — joining no protest against US-led models, while warning against the "**weaponisation of technology and minerals**".

### Key data & facts

- **18th BRICS Summit**, chaired by India; the **140-paragraph New Delhi Declaration** adopted **12 September 2026**. China takes the chair for 2027. Members committed to implement the **BRICS Leaders' Statement on Global Governance of AI** (Rio, 2025) and called for wider access to AI resources.
- **Tech initiatives announced:** a proposed **BRICS Startup Innovation Fund**, a **Risk Lab** proposed for GIFT City, and cooperation on DPI, cyber security, submarine cables and quantum.
- **The access disruption the editorial cites:** a brief "global pull out" of two frontier model families in mid-2026 to comply with **US Department of Commerce export controls** — suspended **12 June 2026**, controls lifted **30 June**, access restored **1 July 2026**. Short, but it demonstrated that a single national export-control decision can interrupt AI services for users in other countries.
- **Earlier precedent:** the US **AI Diffusion Rule (January 2025)** placed India in a middle "**Tier 2**" for advanced AI-chip exports; it was **rescinded in May 2025**.
- **India's own base:** **IndiaAI Mission**, approved March 2024, outlay about **₹10,372 crore** (subsidised GPU compute, foundation models, AIKosh datasets, skilling). The **India AI Impact Summit** (Bharat Mandapam, February 2026) produced a New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact endorsed by **88 countries and international organisations**, structured around three "Sutras" — **People, Planet, Progress**.
- **Summit lineage worth memorising:** Bletchley Park (UK, 2023) → Seoul (2024) → Paris AI Action Summit (February 2025, co-chaired by India) → New Delhi (2026).
- **Overlapping governance tracks:** the UN's **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI** and **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** (created by UNGA, 2025), and **GPAI** (launched 2020; India a founding member).
- **The minerals link:** "weaponisation of minerals" ties AI capability to **gallium, germanium and rare earths** — which connects this piece directly back to the MMDR debate in section 1.

### The other side / counter-argument

The editorial's prescription runs into four problems it does not fully answer.

**Intra-BRICS asymmetry.** China's AI capacity far exceeds every other member's. An "open-source community" led in practice by one member risks substituting dependence on Chinese models and standards for dependence on American ones — a materially worse outcome for India given the strategic relationship, and one that a declaration cannot legislate away.

**Openness cuts both ways.** Freely distributable capable models spread **offensive** cyber capability to non-state actors as readily as defensive capability to states — precisely the risk the editorial flags. The security case for open weights is therefore conditional, not general.

**Consensus deficit.** BRICS has **no charter and no secretariat** and works by consensus; its May 2026 foreign ministers' meeting ended without a joint statement. Converting declarations into funded programmes has been slow — the companion *Hindu* lead this week on the **New Development Bank** made this case at length: roughly **50% of NDB outstanding bonds are dollar-denominated**, local-currency lending stood at about **22% by mid-2025** against a 30% target for 2026, the **Contingent Reserve Arrangement has never been activated**, and drawings above 30% of allotted share still require an IMF programme.

**Capability gap.** The editorial concedes open models trail frontier systems. If defensive AI must be at the frontier to be useful against frontier-enabled offence, then an open-model bloc may be structurally one generation behind the threat.

### Mains value-addition points

- **Reframe "technology access" as "supply-chain sovereignty".** The chain runs: **critical minerals → chips → compute → models → applications**. Export controls can be applied at *any* link. India's exposure is concentrated at the mineral and chip links — which is why the MMDR critical-minerals objective and the AI-access debate are the same policy problem viewed from two ends.
- **India's actual strategy is dual-track, not open-source.** It maintains US-led technology partnerships while co-authoring BRICS AI commitments — **multi-alignment applied to technology**. Name it as such; it is the strategic-autonomy doctrine transposed from defence to tech.
- **Deployment-led advantage:** for an economy of India's structure, returns come less from building frontier models than from **applying** AI to agriculture, health, Indian-language public services and DPI. Argue capability where the marginal return is highest, rather than symmetric competition with the frontier.
- **Concrete, low-controversy cooperation** is the defensible ask from a consensus-bound grouping: AI for cyber defence, multilingual datasets, and **shared evaluation and safety testing**. Link BRICS AI work to NDB financing for compute and to DPI-style open standards India already exports.
- **Domestic institutional prerequisite:** access decisions should rest on domestic risk assessment, which means strengthening the **AI Safety Institute** and **CERT-In**. A country that cannot assess a model's risk cannot make an informed sovereignty argument about it.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS3 — Internal Security**)

> *Paper rotation check: the last four packs used GS1 (society, 20 Aug), GS2 (judiciary, 27 Aug), GS4 (ethics, 3 Sep) and GS2 (IR, 10 Sep). GS3 is due, and internal security specifically has not carried the weekly question since well before that run. Directive rotation: recent weeks used* examine, critically examine, assess, evaluate, discuss, elucidate, critically analyse *— so this week uses* **analyse**.

**Q. "India's shift from strategic restraint to active deterrence has changed the cost calculus of cross-border terrorism, but not its underlying architecture."**

**Analyse this statement. In your answer, assess whether the National Counter-Terrorism Policy and Strategy (PRAHAAR) adequately addresses the non-kinetic dimensions of the threat. (15 marks, 250 words)**

**Directive note.** *Analyse* asks you to break the subject into its constituent parts and show how they relate — it is structural, not evaluative. So the answer must separate **cost calculus** (what retaliation changed) from **architecture** (what it did not: sponsorship, recruitment pipelines, financing, ideological supply). Do **not** write this as a "surgical strikes: achievements and challenges" answer; the axis is the *gap between the two halves of the quoted proposition*. The second sentence uses *assess*, which does require a verdict — so end with a qualified judgement on PRAHAAR's non-kinetic legs, not a listing. A common trap: reciting the 1993–2025 attack timeline. Use two or three dates as evidence, no more; the examiner is testing whether you can distinguish a change in *response* from a change in *cause*.

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

**1. Introduction — establish the two variables, take a position (≈35 words).**
Frame the doctrinal break precisely: Uri (2016) and Balakot (2019) crossed thresholds that Operation Parakram (2001–02) did not, and Operation Sindoor (May 2025) institutionalised the crossing. State up front that you accept the proposition in substance — the cost calculus has demonstrably shifted, the sponsorship architecture has not.

**2. What active deterrence *has* changed.**
Attribution and response are now near-automatic and publicly owned; the political cost of inaction has risen; retaliation has been normalised below the nuclear threshold and survived international scrutiny. Multilateral traction has followed — the Pahalgam paragraph in the BRICS New Delhi Declaration (2026) and consistent SCO positioning on "double standards" indicate the diplomatic cost to the sponsor has risen alongside the military one.

**3. What it has *not* changed — the architecture.**
Groups that emerged from the 1980s Afghan-jihad mobilisation and assumed the identities of Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammad remain instruments of state policy; the IC-814 release of Masood Azhar (1999) and the persistence through Pahalgam (April 2025) and the Red Fort attack (November 2025) show the supply side intact. Add the structural shift: centralised organisations have given way to **decentralised networks and lone actors** using encrypted communication, drones and AI — against which deterrence-by-punishment has no addressee. This is the analytical core of the answer.

**4. PRAHAAR assessed leg by leg (February 2026, whole-of-government and whole-of-society).**
Network dismantling and preventive military capability are the developed legs. **De-radicalisation and social integration** — education, employment, community engagement, rehabilitation — and **terror-financing disruption** are institutionally thinner and, in the case of financing, dependent on external cooperation India does not control. Note the internal contradiction worth marks: the framework's own stated measure of success is *prevention*, which sits almost entirely in the two weakest legs.

**5. Way forward and conclusion — qualified verdict (≈40 words).**
Maintain deterrence but shift the marginal rupee to intelligence fusion (NATGRID, MAC, NIA coordination), counter-radicalisation capacity, and financing interdiction through FATF-type mechanisms. Conclude: deterrence has raised the price of an attack without reducing the number of people willing to carry one out — the doctrine is necessary, and on its own, insufficient.
