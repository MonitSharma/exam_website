## 1. India's growth has weathered the shocks — but the export gains are partly luck

**Source:** Op-ed by **Dharmakirti Joshi, Chief Economist, Crisil**, 12 November 2026. (Newspaper not confirmed by my sources this run — attribute as "Joshi, Crisil", not to a specific paper.)

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Indian Economy** (primary: growth and development; mobilisation of resources; changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth; effects of liberalisation; infrastructure; inclusive growth; investment models); **GS3 — Science & Technology** (R&D spending, innovation capacity) as a secondary hook; **GS2 — IR** (effect of policies of developed countries — tariff wars — on India's interests).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Joshi argues that the shocks of the past two years — tariff wars, West Asian conflict, Strait of Hormuz disruption — did not produce the global slump that was feared, and India's growth has held up well. But he warns that a good part of the recent export surge rests on temporary advantages (refinery outages abroad, high commodity prices, a weaker rupee) rather than on genuine cost and quality competitiveness. His prescription: the coming Budget should treat **manufacturing competitiveness** as its first priority, because the windfall will not repeat.

### Key data & facts (as cited by the author / Crisil)

- GDP growth of about **7%** expected this fiscal; average **CPI inflation about 5.3%**, with upside risks.
- **Merchandise exports up 19%** in April–September.
- **Petroleum exports up 37% in dollar terms and nearly 50% in rupee terms** over April–September — the single clearest marker that the surge is partly windfall.
- **Manufacturing at roughly 16–17% of GVA**, against the long-standing **25% goal**.
- **Logistics costs about 7.8–8.9% of GDP** — the standard comparison is ~8% for developed economies, so this is the structural cost penalty.
- **R&D spending about 0.6–0.7% of GDP** — far below the 2%+ typical of manufacturing exporters.
- **PLI schemes: 14 sectors, ₹1.97 lakh crore** outlay.
- Corporate health is good: **Crisil credit ratio 2.18x in H1**, up from 1.50x in H2 of the previous year; **median debt-to-equity near 0.5x**; revenue up about **16–16.5%** in July–September.
- But **net foreign capital outflows in the first eight months exceeded the whole of last year's**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- The "luck" reading can be overdone. A **19% export rise** with a healthy corporate balance sheet and a 2.18x credit ratio is not purely a refinery-outage story; electronics and pharma gains reflect real capacity built over a decade.
- A **weaker rupee is a competitiveness instrument**, not only an accident. Export economies in East Asia used precisely this channel; the criticism that gains are "exchange-rate driven" applies to almost every successful export take-off.
- Blaming logistics and R&D is the standing diagnosis in every Budget commentary; the binding constraint may instead be **scale and land/labour market frictions**, which the Budget cannot fix with outlays.
- **Borrowing-financed consumption** supporting demand is less durable than income-financed consumption — so the growth number itself may flatter the underlying position (a point that cuts *against* the author's optimism on growth, not just his caution on exports).
- If the **global AI-investment boom cools**, the main prop under world demand weakens, and no amount of domestic competitiveness reform saves export volumes in the short run.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Distinguish "export growth" from "export competitiveness."** Value-addition line: *"A 19% export rise driven by a 37% jump in petroleum shipments measures the world's supply disruption, not India's productivity."* Use this to separate cyclical from structural in any Indian-economy answer.
2. **The three-cost framing** for any question on manufacturing: **logistics (~8–9% of GDP), capital, and compliance** — against R&D at **0.6–0.7% of GDP** on the capability side. This is a reusable four-number template.
3. **The 16–17% vs 25% GVA gap** is the cleanest one-line statement of the Make in India shortfall — pair it with PLI's 14 sectors and ₹1.97 lakh crore to argue that incentives have bought capacity but not yet competitiveness.
4. **Twin-deficit watch:** net foreign capital outflows in eight months exceeding the whole previous year is the external-vulnerability hook for any question on capital flows, rupee management or external-sector resilience.

## 2. Beyond emergency curbs — Delhi's air needs an airshed, not a GRAP stage

**Source:** *The Indian Express*, op-ed, 7 November 2026, by **Gufran Beig**, founder project director of **SAFAR** (System of Air Quality and Weather Forecasting And Research).

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Environment** (primary: environmental pollution and degradation; conservation; environmental impact assessment); **GS2 — Governance** (government policies and interventions in design and implementation; statutory and regulatory bodies — CAQM; cooperative federalism, since public health and agriculture are State List subjects); **GS3 — Agriculture** (cropping patterns, paddy transplanting calendar, residue management); **GS3 — Disaster Management** (public-health emergency response) as a minor hook.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Beig's case is that GRAP is a **peak-shaving instrument misused as a policy**: it can clip the top off an episode but cannot touch a pollution problem whose sources span the entire Indo-Gangetic airshed. He argues for **airshed-based, science-led planning** — source apportionment at airshed scale, better fire detection, and an agricultural calendar fix — rather than another round of emergency curbs each November.

### Key data & facts (as cited by the author)

- **Measures confined to Delhi address only about 50–70% of the sources** that affect the city's air — the single most quotable number in the piece.
- **NIAS modelling attributed roughly 20% of Delhi's PM2.5 on 1 November to stubble burning** — i.e. the majority of the load is year-round and local/regional, not seasonal fire.
- **Over 90% of stubble fires in 2024 and 2025 occurred after 3 p.m., against under 3% in 2021.** Polar-orbiting satellites such as **MODIS** overpass earlier in the day, so official fire counts systematically **undercount** — a deliberate adaptation to monitoring, not a data accident.
- India's **24-hour PM2.5 standard is 60 µg/m³**; the **WHO 2021 annual guideline is 5 µg/m³**.
- The **National AQI** covers **eight pollutants** on a **0–500** scale; **GRAP has four stages**; the **Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM)**, under the 2021 Act, replaced the EPCA.
- The **rice–wheat window is only 2–3 weeks**, and the **Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act, 2009** pushed paddy transplanting to mid-June — which pushes harvest and residue burning into the **low-wind early-winter** period. The groundwater law and the air law pull in opposite directions.
- The author notes the peak season arrived about **two weeks late** this year, linked to El Niño and late rain.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **GRAP is not meant to be a plan.** It is an emergency public-health trigger, and judging it by whether it fixes the airshed is a category error; without it, peak exposure days would be worse.
- **Airshed governance has no institutional home.** Air quality, public health and agriculture sit with States; an airshed authority either duplicates CAQM or needs constitutional space nobody has designed. Beig's own piece leaves the fiscal and institutional architecture largely unspecified — the honest criticism of the prescription.
- **Satellite undercounting cuts both ways.** If fire counts are undercounts, then the 20% attribution for 1 November may itself be understated, which strengthens rather than weakens the case for crop-residue action.
- **Standards comparison is unfair as a target.** Holding India's 60 µg/m³ against the WHO's 5 µg/m³ compares a 24-hour standard with an annual guideline; WHO interim targets are the realistic benchmark.
- There is a **political-economy objection**: farmers' burning is a rational response to a 2–3 week window and to machinery costs; "science-led planning" that does not pay for the alternative will not change behaviour.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **The airshed argument in one line:** *"Delhi can act on at most half to seven-tenths of what it breathes."* That 50–70% figure converts a vague plea for regional cooperation into a measurable governance claim.
2. **Policy-contradiction example** (works in GS2 *and* GS3 answers): the **Subsoil Water Act, 2009** saved groundwater by delaying transplanting, and in doing so moved residue burning into the worst meteorological window. Use it as the standard illustration of **single-objective regulation producing cross-sectoral harm**.
3. **Monitoring as a governance problem:** 90%+ of fires after 3 p.m. vs under 3% in 2021 shows **behavioural adaptation to a measurement regime**. This is a transferable point for any answer on data-driven governance, EPR verification, or compliance-by-gaming.
4. **Institutional ladder to quote:** EPCA → CAQM (2021 Act) → GRAP's four stages → NAAQS/AQI (8 pollutants, 0–500). Pair with the ask: airshed-level source apportionment and year-round source control, not seasonal curbs.

## 3. Caste and class — the creamy layer, applied inconsistently

**Source:** *The Hindu*, **editorial**, 11 November 2026 ("Caste and class"), read alongside this week's digest treatment of the same editorial.

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Social Justice / Polity** (primary: welfare schemes for vulnerable sections and the performance of these schemes; mechanisms, laws, institutions and bodies constituted for the protection of vulnerable sections; Fundamental Rights — Articles 14, 15(4), 16(4); separation of powers and judicial review of executive instructions); **GS1 — Indian Society** (caste, social stratification, social empowerment); **GS2 — Governance** (executive instructions vs statutory rules, transparency of commission reports).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The editorial accepts that excluding the **creamy layer** is integral to OBC reservation — the quota is meant to address caste hierarchy *and* class inequality, not to make income differences inside OBC families irrelevant. Its complaint is about **inconsistent application**: the Union government relied on a **2004 salary-based clarification** that added conditions absent from the governing **1993 DoPT Office Memorandum**, and in doing so kept genuine claimants out of jobs they had already earned. It calls for restoring the 1993 design, protecting pending candidates, and publishing better caste-wise socio-economic data.

### Key data & facts

- **Indra Sawhney (1992)**: 27% OBC quota upheld, **with** creamy-layer exclusion; total reservation capped at **50%**.
- **DoPT Office Memorandum of 8 September 1993**: six categories; for salaried families the test is **occupational status**, and **salary and agricultural income are not counted** under the income test.
- **Income ceiling trajectory**: ₹1 lakh (1993) → ₹2.5 lakh (2004) → ₹4.5 lakh (2008) → ₹6 lakh (2013) → **₹8 lakh (2017)** — unrevised since.
- ***Union of India v. Rohith Nathan*** (**11 March 2026**): the 2004 letter cannot add conditions that the 1993 OM does not contain. Follow-up on **6 November 2026**: the Centre moved to finalise allocation for **958 CSE 2025 candidates**.
- From around 2015, DoPT used the 2004 clarification to deny **dozens of OBC candidates who had already cleared the Civil Services Examination**.
- **OBC share of central government posts: 26.3%** (DoPT Annual Report 2024-25) — against a 27% quota, and declining at higher levels.
- **Justice G. Rohini Commission** (constituted 2017, report submitted **31 July 2023, still not public**): reportedly found roughly **97% of central jobs and seats going to about 25% of OBC sub-castes**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The creamy-layer filter can be too fine.** Caste discrimination does not switch off above an income line; a salaried OBC officer's child may still face exclusion in ways a non-OBC peer does not. A strict filter risks treating reservation as a pure poverty programme and reading Article 16(4) out of the Constitution.
- **An aggressive filter leaves reserved seats unfilled**, which defeats the quota's own purpose — a real administrative outcome, not a hypothetical.
- Equally, the **Rohini finding cuts the other way**: if ~25% of sub-castes capture ~97% of the benefit, the problem is **intra-OBC concentration**, and sub-categorisation (not creamy-layer tinkering) is the real remedy. Keeping the report unpublished prevents that debate.
- **Executive instruction vs rule-making**: the government's defence is that the 2004 letter clarified an unworkable 1993 test. The constitutional answer is that a clarification cannot narrow a right; but the policy problem it was addressing — a salaried family above the ceiling escaping the test on a technicality — is genuine.
- The **₹8 lakh ceiling has not moved since 2017**, so inflation has quietly tightened the filter without any decision being taken. Both sides can cite this.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **The two-axis framing**, usable in almost any reservation answer: *reservation addresses caste hierarchy (a status disability) and class inequality (a resource disability); the creamy layer is the device that keeps the second from swallowing the first.* Anchor with Indra Sawhney 1992.
2. **"26.3% against 27%, and falling at higher levels"** is the sharpest available evidence that representation is a *pipeline* problem, not only an entry problem — use it for questions on substantive vs formal equality.
3. **Rule-of-law point:** *Rohith Nathan* (2026) is a clean, current example of **subordinate executive clarification being struck down for adding conditions to a governing instrument** — transferable to GS2 answers on delegated legislation and administrative overreach.
4. **The data gap:** the Rohini report unpublished since July 2023, and the ₹8 lakh ceiling frozen since 2017, together make the case that **Census 2027's caste count alone will not fix OBC policy** — what is missing is caste-wise socio-economic data plus political willingness to publish it.
5. **Remedy to quote:** supernumerary posts for candidates wrongly denied, 1993 rules applied to pending cases — a specific, administratively feasible ask, which marks out a good answer from a general one.

## 4. Keralam at 70 — human development without waiting for income

**Source:** *The Hindu*, op-ed, 11 November 2026, by **Nissim Mannathukkaren** (Dalhousie University).

**GS mapping:** **GS1 — Indian Society** (primary: population and associated issues; role of women and women's organisation; social empowerment; effects of globalisation; urbanisation and migration); **GS2 — Governance / Social Justice** (issues relating to the development and management of social sectors — health, education, human resources; welfare schemes; devolution and local government); **GS3 — Indian Economy** (inclusive growth, remittances, fiscal stress of a welfare state, ageing and human capital); **GS1 — Geography** (ecological fragility of the Western Ghats coast) as a secondary hook.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Mannathukkaren's claim is that Keralam disproves the sequencing assumption in Indian development thinking — that human development must wait for income growth. Through democratic mobilisation, land reform, public provisioning and distribution, a State that was among India's poorest in 1956 became among its richest, with social indicators ahead of much wealthier States. He is explicit about the bill now coming due: **ageing, fiscal stress, ecological risk, and dependence on out-migration**.

### Key data & facts (as cited by the author)

- Keralam was **among India's poorest States in 1956** and is now **among the richest** by per-capita measures.
- **Women with 10+ years of schooling: 87% in Keralam vs 38% in Gujarat** — the comparison is deliberately with a high-income, high-growth State, not with a laggard.
- **Child marriage: 2.9% in Keralam vs 18% in Gujarat.**
- The State's standing challenges as the author lists them: an **ageing population**, **fiscal stress**, **ecological risk**, and **dependence on migration** (and therefore on remittances and on other countries' labour markets).

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The "Kerala model" is partly a remittance model.** Gulf remittances financed consumption, housing, health and education spending for four decades. Critics argue the causal weight given to public action understates an external windfall that other States could not replicate.
- **High development, weak production.** Kerala's industrial and employment record is thin; high human development coexists with high educated unemployment and out-migration of exactly the people the system educated — an argument that the model exports its success rather than compounding it.
- **Fiscal sustainability**: a welfare state with the country's highest committed expenditure ratios and an ageing population faces a squeeze that the 1957–1990 model never had to solve. The author concedes this; opponents say it is not a footnote but the main fact about the next 70 years.
- **Ecological limits**: the 2018 floods and repeated landslide seasons show that a densely settled, highly modified coastal-Ghats landscape carries risk that development indicators do not capture.
- **Comparator choice matters.** Gujarat is a fair comparator on income but not the only one; States like Tamil Nadu and Himachal Pradesh achieved similar social outcomes with different mixes, so "democracy and distribution" may be necessary rather than sufficient.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **The sequencing argument**, as a quotable thesis: *human development need not follow income growth — it can lead it.* This is the cleanest available Indian counter-example to the trickle-down sequencing assumption, and it works in GS1 (society), GS2 (social sector) and GS3 (inclusive growth) answers.
2. **The 87% vs 38% schooling comparison** is a high-value single statistic because it pits social outcome against income rank — exactly what questions on "growth vs development" are asking for.
3. **Demographic transition case study:** Keralam is India's preview of ageing — low fertility, high longevity, labour out-migration. Use it in any population-and-development answer instead of generic "demographic dividend" language.
4. **Honest-balance line** (examiners reward this): *the Kerala model's achievement was distribution; its unfinished problem is production and fiscal sustainability.* Concede the remittance critique explicitly rather than defending the model wholesale.
5. **Link to local government:** the 1996 People's Plan Campaign and 73rd/74th Amendment devolution are the institutional mechanism behind the outcomes — a specific, citable channel rather than a vague "political consciousness".

## GS Mains practice question — rotated paper: **GS3 (Indian Economy — manufacturing competitiveness and the external sector)**

> **"India's recent export performance reflects the world's disruptions more than India's competitiveness." Critically examine this assertion, and suggest what would make Indian manufacturing structurally competitive. (15 marks, 250 words)**

Directive words: **"critically examine"** — so you must test the assertion on evidence, come down on a side, *and* carry the suggestion limb. Do not write a balanced non-answer: a "critically examine" question with a prescriptive second limb expects a verdict plus a short, costed-sounding set of measures.

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Frame the claim precisely (intro, ~30 words).** Define the distinction the question turns on: **export growth** (a volume/value outcome) versus **export competitiveness** (unit cost, quality, and the ability to hold share when the windfall ends). State your verdict in the intro — partly true, and true for the composition of the gains rather than for the whole.

2. **Evidence FOR the assertion.** Merchandise exports **up 19%** (April–September) but **petroleum exports up 37% in dollar terms and nearly 50% in rupee terms** — the surge is concentrated where foreign refinery outages and commodity prices did the work. Add the exchange-rate channel and the fact that **net foreign capital outflows in eight months exceeded all of the previous year**, i.e. markets are not pricing a durable competitiveness gain.

3. **Evidence AGAINST the assertion.** Growth of about **7%** through tariff wars, a West Asian conflict and Hormuz disruption is itself evidence of resilience. Corporate balance sheets are strong (**credit ratio 2.18x in H1 vs 1.50x**, median **debt-to-equity ~0.5x**, revenue up **~16%** in July–September). A decade of capacity-building under **PLI (14 sectors, ₹1.97 lakh crore)** shows up in electronics and pharma, which no refinery outage explains.

4. **Diagnose the structural gap — the pivot of the answer.** **Manufacturing at 16–17% of GVA against the 25% target**; **logistics at 7.8–8.9% of GDP** against roughly 8% in developed economies; **R&D at 0.6–0.7% of GDP** against 2%+ in established manufacturing exporters. Frame these as a **cost penalty** (logistics, compliance, capital) plus a **capability deficit** (R&D, scale, skills). One line of political economy: incentives have bought capacity, not yet competitiveness.

5. **Way forward and verdict (conclusion).** Four specific measures: shift from output-linked incentives towards **cost-reduction and productivity** (logistics corridors, port turnaround, compliance decriminalisation); **raise R&D intensity** with private-sector co-funding rather than outlay alone; **scale and factor-market reform** (land assembly, labour-code implementation) because incentives cannot substitute for scale; and **diversify export composition** away from petroleum-and-commodity dependence. Verdict line: the assertion is right about the *composition* of this year's gains and wrong about the *capacity* built over a decade — the task is to convert a windfall into a cost advantage before the windfall reverses.

**Keyword bank for the answer:** unit labour cost, logistics intensity, R&D intensity, terms-of-trade windfall, export basket diversification, GVA share, PLI, factor-market frictions, external vulnerability, twin deficits.
