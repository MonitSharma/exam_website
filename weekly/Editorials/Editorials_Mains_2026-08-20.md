## 1. Counting caste again — Census 2027 and the return of the 1931 question

**Peg:** The Ministry of Home Affairs notified the **40-question population-enumeration schedule** on **14 August 2026**, confirming that caste will be recorded for *every* respondent, not just SC/ST. **Phase-II enumeration began on 17 August 2026** in Ladakh and the snow-bound non-synchronous areas of J&K, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, with a 15-day self-enumeration window (17–31 August) ahead of house-to-house enumeration from 1–30 September and a revision round 1–5 October.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)
The supporting case, made across this week's commentary, is that Indian social policy has been running for three-quarters of a century on caste numbers from **1931** — a dataset older than the Constitution itself. Enumerating caste in a regular decennial Census, rather than through a one-off socio-economic survey, would give reservation policy, welfare targeting and sub-categorisation their first authoritative denominator, and would make caste inequality statistically visible rather than merely asserted.

### Key data & facts
- **1931** is the last Census with comprehensive caste enumeration; it estimated OBCs at roughly **52%** of a then-population of ~271 million. Caste enumeration beyond SC/ST was dropped from **1951**, on the stated reasoning that counting caste would deepen social division.
- Census 2027 is India's **first fully digital Census**, conducted in two phases, with an **optional self-enumeration** facility via an official web portal.
- The caste column is **open-ended**: enumerators are instructed to record caste as the respondent declares it, in their own words, rather than selecting from a pre-fixed list.
- The schedule notified on 14 August runs to **40 questions**, and also seeks documentary/digital-footprint details of respondents.
- The Supreme Court **dismissed a plea (April 2026)** seeking a direction to the Centre to stop caste enumeration.
- Reference point for the reservation debate: the **Indra Sawhney (1992)** ceiling on reservations, and the **Rohini Commission (2017)** on OBC sub-categorisation, which has lacked usable data.

### The other side / counter-argument
Two distinct objections, worth keeping separate in an answer:

1. **Sociological/ideological.** Anand Teltumbde's *The Caste Con Census* argues the modern caste count is a colonial echo — British administrators began systematic caste enumeration in **1871**, partly as a response to cross-caste unity after 1857 — and that counting caste hardens it into a permanent political identity rather than dissolving it. On this view a census cannot annihilate what it institutionalises.
2. **Technical/administrative.** Critics of the *method*, not the principle, point out that an **open-ended caste column without a pre-prepared list** creates serious usability problems: the same jati recorded under dozens of spellings, sub-caste and surname confusion, and no reliable way to map declarations onto existing SC/ST/OBC state lists. Data that cannot be standardised cannot be used for policy — which is the whole justification for collecting it.

There is also the political-economy worry that fresh numbers will generate immediate demands for **proportionate reservation**, putting the *Indra Sawhney* cap under pressure.

### Mains value-addition points
- Frame it as **"the measurement problem in social justice"**: India's affirmative-action architecture is unusually data-poor relative to its ambition — the denominator is from 1931, the beneficiary lists are administrative, and the two have never been reconciled.
- Useful conceptual pairing: **Béteille's** caution that official categories can *create* the groups they claim to describe, versus **Ambedkar's** position that caste must be named and measured before it can be dismantled. Naming this tension earns marks in a GS1 society answer.
- The **open-ended question is the sleeper issue**. Distinguish "should we count caste" (settled, politically) from "can we count it usably" (unsettled, technically). Most answers will miss the second.
- Link to **evidence-based governance**: post-2027 caste data will also become the base for delimitation and women's-reservation implementation debates — so the stakes go beyond welfare targeting.

**Maps to:** GS1 — Indian Society (social empowerment, caste, social stratification); spills into GS2 (social justice, welfare schemes) and GS3 (data as public infrastructure).

## 2. The Green India Mission audit — when planting trees isn't restoring forests

**Source:** The Hindu editorial, **18 August 2026**, on the CAG performance audit of the Green India Mission (GIM).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)
The CAG's decade-long audit shows a near-total collapse between GIM's ecological ambition and delivery on the ground. The editorial's sharper point is that the failure is not merely one of money or coordination but a **category error** — treating tree-planting drives as equivalent to genuine forest restoration, so that even "successful" plantation numbers do not translate into functioning ecosystems.

### Key data & facts
- The CAG audited GIM planning, implementation and monitoring across **16 States/UTs** for **2015-16 to 2024-25**.
- GIM **missed its forest-cover target by 97.57%**.
- **Funding gap:** against a proposed ₹**10,600 crore**, only ₹**1,149.14 crore** was received as budgetary support over the ten years — roughly **11%** of the requirement.
- **Weak convergence:** CAMPA, MGNREGS, Nagar Van Yojana and School Nursery Yojana were found operating without adequate integration with GIM.
- **Data reliability:** achievements appeared overstated because KML files used for validation were not properly verified. GIS assessment of sampled sites found **~70% showed no discernible change** attributable to GIM.

### The other side / counter-argument
The defence is partly structural: GIM is a **mission riding on other people's budgets** — it was designed as a convergence umbrella, so its own budget line was never meant to carry the load, and States hold the implementing machinery under the concurrent-list division on forests. On this reading the audit indicts India's afforestation *architecture* rather than GIM specifically. There is also a genuine methodological caveat: a ten-year GIS window may be too short to register slow ecological succession in degraded landscapes.

### Mains value-addition points
- Use as the standing example of **"monitoring by input rather than outcome"** — saplings planted and hectares notified are inputs; canopy density, survival rate, native-species mix and ecosystem services are outcomes. The 97.57% shortfall is the gap between the two.
- Strong link to **India's NDC commitment** on an additional carbon sink through forest and tree cover: a plantation-heavy strategy that fails on survival will not deliver the sink either.
- **Compensatory afforestation critique:** CAMPA plantations frequently substitute monoculture and non-native species for felled natural forest — biodiversity and hydrological function are not fungible with tree count.
- Governance point: independent, GIS/remote-sensing-based **third-party verification** should be a funding condition, not a post-hoc audit finding.

**Maps to:** GS3 — Environment & Ecology (conservation, environmental governance); GS2 (audit institutions, CAG as accountability mechanism).

## 3. The Mecca Pact and India — hedging, not realignment, in West Asia

**Source:** Indian Express opinion, **17 August 2026**, on the shift in West Asia's security architecture and whether India has a coherent response; supported by the wider strategic commentary on the pact.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)
West Asia is undergoing a once-in-a-generation change in its security architecture. India's decade of steady gains in the region — energy, diaspora, I2U2, IMEC — is fading in relative terms as Pakistan re-enters the Gulf security conversation, and it is not clear that New Delhi has an active, independent regional strategy rather than a reactive one.

### Key data & facts
- The **Mecca (Makkah) Joint Defence Agreement** was signed on **7 August 2026** by **Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Pakistan** — a trilateral collective-defence compact treating an armed attack on one as an attack on all.
- It **builds on the September 2025 Saudi–Pakistan Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement**, now widened to include Türkiye's NATO-integrated military industry.
- Division of labour: **Türkiye** supplies drones, missiles and electronic-warfare systems; **Pakistan** supplies training, manpower and operational expertise; **Saudi Arabia** supplies capital and strategic depth.
- Analysts characterise it as **strategic hedging** rather than realignment — Riyadh is not abandoning Washington, but is buying optionality across the US, China, Türkiye, Pakistan and India simultaneously.

### The other side / counter-argument
The deflationary reading — argued in the strategic press as "deterrence theatre" — is that the pact is long on declaration and short on machinery: no integrated command, no basing arrangements, no defined trigger, and three signatories with divergent threat perceptions (Saudi's is Iran and the Houthis, Türkiye's is Syria/Eastern Mediterranean, Pakistan's is India). Collective-defence clauses without joint planning have historically been unenforceable. Some commentary also reads it as *increasing* India's value to Riyadh and to Israel, not decreasing it, precisely because Gulf hedging rewards multiple partners.

### Mains value-addition points
- Deploy **"strategic hedging"** as the analytical frame: middle powers in a multipolar system diversify security patrons rather than switching them. This is the same logic India itself uses — which limits how much India can object to it.
- India's structural stakes in the Gulf are the ones to cite: **energy imports, remittances, and the ~9 million-strong diaspora** in the GCC, plus IMEC's western leg running through the region.
- The real risk is not the pact's Article-5 language but the **normalisation of Pakistan as a Gulf security provider**, which gives Islamabad diplomatic and financial depth independent of the US and China.
- Policy line for an answer: India's response should be **capability-and-connectivity based** (defence-industrial offers, IMEC delivery, maritime security in the Arabian Sea) rather than declaratory objection.

**Maps to:** GS2 — International Relations (India and its neighbourhood; bilateral/regional groupings affecting India's interests); GS3 (energy security).

## 4. Taxing the IPL to fund science — why the R&D shortcut fails

**Source:** The Hindu editorial, **18 August 2026**, on proposals to levy special taxes on entertainment and sport to cross-subsidise scientific research.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)
The recurring proposal to tax "non-essential" but profitable sectors — cinema, tourism, apparel, professional sport — to fund research is economically fallacious: it assumes those profits come *at the expense of* science, ignoring the cascade effects of commercial activity, and it treats a **structural** problem (absorptive capacity, bureaucratic friction, weak private R&D) as a **revenue** problem.

### Key data & facts
- India's **GERD** has historically hovered around **0.6–0.7% of GDP**, against a global average of ~1.8% and the ~2% target set by successive S&T policy documents. It fell from ~0.8% (2005-06) to ~0.6% (2020-21), with estimates around **0.9% for 2025-26**.
- International comparison (2022): **China ~2.4%, USA ~3.5%, Israel ~5.6%** of GDP.
- Composition shift: **private industry's share of GERD reached 51.8% in 2023-24** — the first time private spending exceeded combined Centre and State spending.

### The other side / counter-argument
The case for earmarked levies is not absurd: hypothecated taxes create **predictable, ring-fenced multi-year funding**, which is exactly what research needs and what annual budget cycles fail to provide — the same logic behind the cess-funded Research Development and Innovation corpus and the Anusandhan National Research Foundation model. Defenders would also argue that the "cascade effects" objection applies to *any* tax, so it cannot be a decisive argument against this one.

### Mains value-addition points
- The composition point beats the headline number: GERD crossing 51.8% private is the **structurally healthier signal**, and it suggests the binding constraint is now the **quality of public spending and ease of doing research**, not merely its quantity.
- Name the frictions concretely: **fund-release delays, GFR procurement rules for lab equipment, low PhD-to-industry mobility, and grant cycles shorter than research cycles**.
- Institutional hooks: **ANRF**, the **RDI scheme**, and the persistent gap between announced corpus and disbursed funds.
- Counter-intuitive framing that scores well: India's problem is less "too little money for science" than "**too little science-absorbing industry**" — the two are related but demand different instruments (tax incentives and public procurement of innovation, not levies).

**Maps to:** GS3 — Science & Technology (indigenisation, R&D achievements) and Economy (mobilisation of resources, public expenditure).

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS1 — Indian Society**)

> **Q.** Census 2027 will produce India's first comprehensive enumeration of caste since 1931. Examine the implications of this data for the design of social justice policy, and discuss the sociological concerns that such enumeration raises. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

*Directive check: "Examine" asks you to investigate the implications closely and weigh them; "discuss" asks for a balanced treatment of the concerns. The question deliberately has two commands — allocate roughly 60:40 and make sure the second half is genuinely sociological, not a repeat of the policy half.*

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Context in one line, then the data gap.** Caste enumerated comprehensively last in 1931 (OBCs ~52% of ~271 million); dropped from 1951 on the reasoning that counting divides. Since then, affirmative action has run on an ageing denominator — set this up as the problem the 2027 Census answers, not as background.

2. **Implications for policy design — be specific, avoid generalities.** (a) OBC **sub-categorisation** finally becomes evidentiable, taking forward the Rohini Commission's unfinished work; (b) welfare targeting can move from block categories to graded deprivation; (c) the reservation debate acquires a factual base, which cuts both ways — it will strengthen demands for proportionality and place the *Indra Sawhney* ceiling under pressure; (d) creamy-layer and intra-category capture become measurable rather than anecdotal.

3. **The methodological caveat that most answers will miss.** The caste column is **open-ended** — recorded as declared, without a pre-fixed list. Standardisation across jati names, spellings, surnames and State-specific lists is a live risk; unusable data would defeat the exercise's own justification. Flag this as a *design* problem, distinct from the *principle*.

4. **The sociological concerns — the second command word.** The Teltumbde/critical position: enumeration has a colonial lineage (systematic counting from 1871, after 1857) and risks hardening caste into a permanent administrative and electoral identity. Set against this the counter-position, associated with Ambedkar's approach, that invisibility protects the status quo — what is not counted cannot be redressed. Add Béteille's caution that official categories can consolidate the groups they claim only to record.

5. **Balanced conclusion.** Data is necessary but not sufficient: the Census can supply an accurate map of inequality, but whether that map is used to dismantle hierarchy or to entrench mobilisation depends on the institutional design that follows — transparent publication, statutory sub-categorisation processes, and periodic review. Close on the distinction between **recognising** caste and **reifying** it.

*Optional value-addition if you have room: one line on the Census as data public-infrastructure — the same enumeration will underpin delimitation and women's-reservation implementation debates, so its integrity has consequences well beyond welfare.*
