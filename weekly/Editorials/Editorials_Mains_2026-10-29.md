## 1. Beyond Kudankulam's fence — the periphery is the perimeter

**Source:** The Hindu, op-ed, **27 October 2026**, headlined *"Beyond Kudankulam's fence, a cybersecurity challenge."*

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Internal Security** (primary: challenges to internal security through communication networks, basics of cyber security, security agencies and their mandate, role of external state and non-state actors); **GS2 — Governance** (statutory and regulatory bodies, government policies and interventions in design and implementation, transparency and accountability); **GS3 — Energy** (nuclear energy, infrastructure) as the setting.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India protects the nuclear plant itself well, but not the ecosystem of EPC contractors, vendors and cloud providers that holds the plant's engineering data. The Kudankulam files that surfaced were taken from a contractor's records, not from the plant's control systems — so a hardened physical and operational perimeter was bypassed entirely. Because obligations on these third parties rest mostly on **commercial contracts** rather than on a statutory cybersecurity duty, the periphery stays weak however strong the centre is.

### Key data & facts

- The ransomware group **World Leaks** published about **19,000 files (≈14.3 GB)**, spanning roughly **2016 to 2025** — reported contents include engineering drawings, supplier information, inspection records, meeting minutes and insurance documents.
- The data belonged to **Reliance Infrastructure**, the **EPC contractor for Units 3 and 4** (NPCIL states it won the **2018 Common Services Balance of Plant EPC contract** through public tender), and was held with the data-centre provider **Yotta**.
- **NPCIL's position:** the files relate to **non-nuclear / conventional** infrastructure; reactor **control and safety systems show no sign of breach**; the drawings derived from indicative tender specifications and were reviewed before acceptance.
- **Timeline point to get right:** the breach was **reported in mid-July 2026** (Yotta says it detected suspicious activity on a Reliance-hosted server in **May**); the **27 October op-ed is a retrospective policy argument**, not news of a fresh incident. Do not write it up as an October breach.
- **Precedent:** in **2019**, **DTrack** malware was found on Kudankulam's **internet-facing administrative network** — a different incident, and notably also *not* the operational technology.
- **Plant:** **six VVER-1000 reactors (6 × 1,000 MW)**; **Units 1 and 2 operating**. India's largest nuclear station.
- **Legal architecture:** **IT Act, 2000** — **Section 70** (protected systems), **Section 70A** (**NCIIPC** as the nodal agency for critical information infrastructure), **Section 70B** (**CERT-In**). The **DPDP Act, 2023** covers **personal data only**, so technical drawings and engineering records of a strategic facility fall **outside** it.
- **Remedies the author proposes:** extend security obligations to contractors and cloud providers; **data minimisation** at vendors; replace annual self-certification with **independent audits and red-teaming**; adopt **zero-trust** architecture; and a **single national framework for supply-chain security**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **NPCIL's distinction is technically real, not spin.** Reactor instrumentation and control at Kudankulam is **air-gapped**; design-stage drawings of balance-of-plant works are a very different asset class from live safety systems. An answer that implies the reactor was "hacked" is factually wrong and will be marked down.
- **Air-gapping has a cost that is rarely stated:** total isolation forecloses remote diagnostics, predictive maintenance and centralised monitoring, which themselves have safety value. "Just air-gap everything" is not a free recommendation.
- **The contract route is not inherently weak** — it is faster to amend than a statute, can be tailored per project, and already carries liquidated damages. The author's real complaint is about **enforcement and audit**, which a statute does not automatically supply either.
- **Over-regulating vendors has a competition cost.** Heavy, continuously audited security obligations are affordable for large EPC firms and not for smaller specialised suppliers; the predictable result is concentration in strategic procurement, which is its own security risk.
- **Disclosure is genuinely two-edged.** Faster mandatory breach reporting improves systemic learning but also confirms to an attacker what was successfully taken. CERT-In's six-hour reporting rule is already contested on exactly this ground.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"The perimeter is now wherever the data is."** This is the week's sharpest security formulation and it generalises far beyond nuclear plants — to defence shipyards, railway signalling, power dispatch centres, hospital networks and payment systems. A hardened facility whose drawings sit on a commercial cloud has moved its perimeter to that cloud without deciding to. Use it to open any GS3 cyber answer.
2. **The regulatory gap is precisely nameable, which is what scores.** Three instruments exist — **Section 70/70A/70B** for the *operator*, the **DPDP Act** for *personal* data, and **contracts** for *vendors* — and the intersection, **non-personal strategic data held by a third party**, is governed by none of them. Naming a gap with that precision beats listing agencies.
3. **"You can outsource the work; you cannot outsource the statutory duty."** The accountability principle to carry into GS2 answers on regulation and into GS4 on probity in public procurement. It is the same structure as the input-versus-outcome distinction the 15 October pack drew about CSR, and the self-certification-versus-audit point in the 12 October RTI item: **compliance paperwork is not assurance**.
4. **Data minimisation is the cheapest control available and the least used.** Most of these 19,000 files had no operational reason to remain on a vendor's server in 2026 at all. "Retention is exposure" is a one-line argument usable in cyber, in privacy and in e-governance answers alike.
5. **Nuclear-specific hook worth one clause:** India's nuclear expansion — the push toward larger installed capacity, SMRs and private participation after the civil-liability and Atomic Energy Act amendments now in play — **multiplies the number of contractors with design-stage access**. The supply-chain problem scales with the programme, which is why the author wants the framework built now rather than after the next incident.

## 2. A ready solution to tackle anaemia — when the vehicle matters more than the nutrient

**Source:** The Hindu, op-ed, **27 October 2026**, headlined *"A ready solution to tackle anaemia."*

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Governance / Social Justice** (primary: issues relating to the development and management of social sectors — health, education, human resources; welfare schemes for vulnerable sections and their performance; government policies and interventions in design and implementation; centre–State relations in a shared-delivery scheme); **GS3 — Economy** (food processing and food security, PDS, human capital); **GS1 — Indian Society** (women, children and adolescents; nutrition and population issues).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

**Double Fortified Salt (DFS)** — salt carrying both iodine and encapsulated iron — is a proven, cheap, already-manufacturable tool against iron-deficiency anaemia, and India already has the one delivery platform that reaches almost every household: salt. Its slow uptake is therefore **institutional, not technical** — it is not written into the revised Anaemia Mukt Bharat strategy with dedicated financing, and PDS inclusion is left to individual States. The authors want DFS made a funded central component delivered through the **PDS, PM POSHAN and ICDS**.

### Key data & facts

**Burden (NFHS-5, 2019–21)**
- Children **6–59 months: 67.1%** · adolescent girls **(15–19): 59.1%** · women **(15–49): 57.0%** · pregnant women **52.2%** · men **25.0%**.
- **Prevalence rose among children and women compared with NFHS-4** — the single most important fact in the piece, because it means the existing instrument mix is not merely slow, it is losing ground.
- Historical anchor: NFHS-2 (**1998**) recorded about **56%** among adolescent girls aged 15–19 and about **69%** in tribal communities — i.e. roughly three decades of intervention with no durable decline.
- The economic burden of iron-deficiency anaemia is cited at about **4% of GDP** *(a widely quoted estimate; attribute it as cited rather than as settled national accounting)*.

**Why supplements alone have not worked**
- **CNNS** data: **vitamin B12 deficiency in about one-sixth of pre-school children and one-third of adolescents**, with widespread folate deficiency — so iron alone cannot close the gap.
- Only **6%** of children aged **6–23 months** received a **minimum acceptable diet**; only **9%** consumed iron-rich foods.
- Nearly **two-thirds of the rural poor cannot afford** a diet meeting national nutrition guidelines — the affordability constraint behind the dietary-diversity advice.
- **Anaemia Mukt Bharat (launched 2018)** runs a revised **7×7×7 framework**; the adolescent programme targets about **116 million** adolescents; in **Q2 FY 2024-25**, **15.4 crore** children and adolescents received IFA supplements. **PMSMA** provides antenatal care including anaemia screening on the **9th of each month**.

**The case for the salt vehicle**
- About **95% of households use iodised salt** — a near-universal distribution and compliance platform that took decades to build and is already paid for.
- DFS currently reaches an estimated **76 million people across four States** through safety-net programmes.
- A global review estimates DFS could reduce anaemia by **up to 21%**.
- Cost: about **₹14–20 per person per year**, with a **benefit–cost ratio of 4.2:1**.
- Formulation: developed by **ICMR-NIN, Hyderabad**, using **encapsulated iron** (encapsulation is what prevents the iron–iodine interaction and the colour and taste changes that killed earlier attempts).
- Companion vehicle: **rice is eaten by about 65%** of the population at about **6.8 kg per person per month**. Cited modelling: an annual outlay of about **₹2,800 crore** could avert about **16.6 million DALYs a year**; another estimate puts it at about **94.1 million fewer anaemia cases and ₹8,098 crore saved over five years**. *(These are the authors' cited projections — present them as modelled, not measured.)*

### The other side / counter-argument

- **India already exceeds the WHO salt ceiling of 5 g a day.** A programme whose vehicle is salt must be paired with sodium-reduction messaging, or the state ends up promoting consumption of the very thing it is trying to reduce. This is the most serious objection and a good answer raises it before the examiner does.
- **Haemoglobinopathies.** India has a high burden of **thalassaemia** and **sickle cell disease** (and the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission running to 2047), and iron loading is harmful in some of these patients. Universal fortification therefore requires **screening and clear labelling safeguards** — a real equity-versus-universality trade-off, not a technicality.
- **The diagnosis itself is contested.** A significant strand of Indian nutrition research argues the haemoglobin **cut-offs and venous-versus-capillary measurement** used in NFHS overstate anaemia prevalence — which is why the government has moved toward the **Diet and Biomarkers Survey in India (DABS-I)**. If the denominator is wrong, so is the programme's scale. Flag this honestly; do not resolve it.
- **Fortification can crowd out the harder reform.** The authors concede the point: DFS **complements and does not replace** dietary diversity, deworming, malaria and sanitation control, and treatment of non-nutritional causes. A cheap technical fix can absorb the political attention that food-system and income reform needs — the same critique made of ethanol in the 3 September pack and of input audit in the 15 October CSR piece.
- **Federal and market frictions.** **PDS commodity choice rests with States**, so a central "flagship" either needs money attached or it is advice; and India's salt industry includes many **small producers** who cannot meet fortification quality standards without support, so the policy has a small-enterprise cost too.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Choose the vehicle, not just the nutrient."** The portable idea of the week. India's nutrition failure is less about not knowing *what* to deliver than about choosing delivery routes that require daily compliance from the poorest households — a pill a day, a counselling visit, a diet they cannot afford. Salt and rice require **no behaviour change at all**. Carry this into any GS2 answer on scheme implementation: *the best instrument is the one that works when the beneficiary does nothing*.
2. **Rising prevalence is the argument, not the context.** Most answers open with "67.1% of children are anaemic". The sharper opening is that prevalence **rose between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 while supplementation coverage expanded** — which converts a statistic into a diagnosis of instrument failure. One sentence, and the answer is already analytical.
3. **Anaemia is a multi-deficiency problem wearing an iron label.** B12 in one-sixth of pre-schoolers and one-third of adolescents, plus folate deficiency, explains why iron-only IFA at scale underperforms. This is the strongest technical point available for a GS2 health answer and almost no candidate will have it.
4. **The equity paradox of universal fortification.** Universality is what makes DFS cheap and administratively simple — and it is exactly what creates the haemoglobinopathy risk, because a universal vehicle cannot ask who is taking it. The generalisable formulation: *universal delivery buys reach by giving up targeting, so it must buy back safety through screening and labelling.* Usable well beyond nutrition.
5. **A clean convergence example for GS2.** DFS touches the Ministry of Health (AMB), Consumer Affairs and Food & Public Distribution (PDS), Women and Child Development (ICDS, PM POSHAN), Education (PM POSHAN delivery), FSSAI (standards), ICMR-NIN (formulation) and the States (commodity choice). When a question asks why convergence fails, this is a better worked example than a generic list of ministries.
6. **Cost-effectiveness as an argumentative device.** **₹14–20 per person per year** against **4% of GDP** in losses, at a **4.2:1** benefit–cost ratio, is the kind of comparison that turns a welfare argument into an economic one — and the economic framing is what distinguishes a high-scoring GS2 answer from a sympathetic one.

## 3. Sixteen years of the Nutrient-Based Subsidy — and a worse NPK balance than when it began

**Source:** The Indian Express, op-ed, **26 October 2026**, by ICRIER authors, drawing on the ICRIER volume ***Healing Soils in India: For Better Crop Health and Human Nutrition*** (2026; authors **Biswabara Sahu, Ritika Juneja, Sachchida Nand and Ashok Gulati**). Read alongside the companion Indian Express piece of **23 October** on agriculture's "fossilisation and fuelisation".

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Agriculture** (primary: issues of farm subsidies and minimum support prices, public distribution system — objectives, functioning, limitations, revamping; cropping patterns; e-technology in the aid of farmers); **GS3 — Environment** (environmental pollution and degradation, soil health, nitrogen cycle); **GS2 — Governance** (government policies and interventions in design and implementation, DBT, centre–State implementation); **GS3 — Economy** (mobilisation of resources, subsidy rationalisation, import dependence).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The **Nutrient-Based Subsidy (NBS)**, introduced in **2010** precisely to correct India's nitrogen bias, has made it worse — because **urea was left outside the regime**. Urea's price is administratively fixed while phosphatic and potassic fertiliser prices track world markets, so the relative price the farmer actually faces pushes ever harder toward nitrogen. The authors' prescription is to stop subsidising the product and start subsidising the farmer: a **per-hectare, nutrient-neutral and crop-neutral DBT**, with urea brought into NBS and the savings redirected to micro-irrigation, soil testing and nutrient-efficient inputs.

### Key data & facts

**The imbalance**
- National **N:P:K use ratio ≈ 10.5 : 4.1 : 1**, against a derived agronomic norm of about **2.8 : 1.5 : 1**.
- Nitrogen's share of NPK consumption is back to roughly **67%** — about its **1970-71** level. Sixteen years of NBS have returned the ratio to where it started.
- Nitrogen application moved from about **7% below** the recommended dose to about **6% above** it (one digest renders the shift as **−7% to +9%**; treat the direction as solid and the exact endpoint as uncertain).
- **11 of 20 major States overuse nitrogen**, led by **Jharkhand (≈72%)** and **Punjab (≈65%)** above recommended doses.
- **Phosphorus** deficit narrowed from about **29% to 22%**; **potassium** deficit **deepened from about 48% to 72%**.
- Farmers apply about **9 kg of potash per hectare**. **⚠ Two secondary digests give the requirement as 22 kg/ha and 32 kg/ha respectively — do not cite a precise denominator.** Write it as *"roughly a third of agronomic requirement"*, which both versions support. **Every State under-applies potash**; Haryana and Jharkhand apply about a tenth of crop need.

**The price signal that explains all of the above (2010-11 to 2024-25)**
- **DAP** prices nearly **tripled**, to about **₹54/kg**.
- **MOP (muriate of potash)** rose about **six-fold**, to about **₹52/kg**.
- The price of **nitrogen in urea rose by about 1%**.
- *That three-line sequence is the entire argument. If you remember nothing else from this piece, remember that farmers did not become irrational — the relative prices did.*

**Fiscal and efficiency context**
- Fertiliser subsidy budgeted at about **₹1.71 lakh crore for 2026-27**, with the authors warning it could reach **₹2.5–3 lakh crore** if Strait of Hormuz disruption raises import costs.
- Crops take up only about **30–40% of applied nitrogen**; the rest is volatilised or leached. Urea is about **46% nitrogen**.
- From the **23 October companion piece**: fertiliser use (N+P+K) rose from about **0.07 MT (1950-51) to 33.5 MT (2025-26)**; **urea and DAP together supply about 67.5%** of nutrients; tractors rose from about **8,500 (1950-51) to over 1.2 crore**, with **11.6 lakh sold in 2025-26**; **sulphur import prices** rose from about **$100 to over $1,000 per tonne** since 2019-20; the **E20** ethanol blending target was **met in 2025**.
- **⚠ Irrigation-coverage discrepancy to note:** the 23 October piece gives about **56%** of gross cropped area irrigated; Ashok Gulati's **12 October** piece (in the 15 October pack) gave about **60%**. Both are in range depending on the year and series — cite "roughly 55–60%" rather than a point estimate.

**Existing instruments the authors say are under-used**
- **PM-PRANAM** (rewarding States that cut chemical fertiliser use), **Soil Health Cards**, **neem-coated urea**, **nano urea**, the **4R principle** (right source, right rate, right time, right place), pulses in rotation for biological nitrogen fixation.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The political economy is the binding constraint, and the authors do not solve it.** Urea decontrol has been attempted and abandoned repeatedly since the 1990s; the reviewer of the ICRIER volume makes exactly this criticism — the book explains what to do, not why earlier attempts failed or how a government survives doing it. A per-hectare DBT converts a hidden subsidy into a visible cash transfer whose nominal value is politically impossible to reduce later.
- **Per-hectare transfers have a distributional problem.** Area-based payment is regressive in proportion to landholding, and India's tenancy is largely unrecorded — so the transfer reaches the **landowner**, not necessarily the **cultivator**. With about **86%** of holdings small or marginal (Agriculture Census 2015-16), the per-head amounts are small while the administrative lift is large.
- **National averages conceal the agronomy.** The ICRIER volume's own reviewer notes it leans on aggregated national data, which hides variation in soil type, cropping pattern and rainfall. "Balanced NPK" is not one ratio: what Punjab's rice–wheat system needs is not what a rainfed Jharkhand plot needs, and a single national ratio target can itself misallocate.
- **Potash is almost entirely imported**, so cheaper potash is partly a foreign-exchange and supply-security decision, not only a subsidy decision. India has no significant domestic MOP resource; raising potash use raises import dependence in a market with very few suppliers.
- **The counter-case for cheap urea is not nothing.** It underwrites staple output and therefore food-price stability, and it is the one farm support that reaches rainfed smallholders without any paperwork. The honest framing is a **trade-off between static food security and dynamic soil and fiscal sustainability**, not a story of pure policy error.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Farmers respond to relative prices, not to advisories."** The single most transferable sentence of the week. The state has issued Soil Health Cards, promoted balanced nutrition and coated its urea, while leaving in place a price structure that makes nitrogen almost free and potash six times costlier. Where incentives and advice conflict, incentives win. Reusable in GS3 on agriculture and energy, GS2 on behavioural policy design, and in the essay paper.
2. **A reform can fail by being partial.** NBS was *designed* to fix the imbalance and worsened it, because **excluding the dominant product from a price-based reform converts the reform into a distortion**. This is a far better example than "implementation was weak", and it generalises: a partial GST, a partial labour code, a partial electricity-tariff reform all behave the same way.
3. **"Subsidise the farmer, not the fertiliser."** The cleanest statement of the instrument shift — from product-linked, manufacturer-routed subsidy to nutrient-neutral per-hectare DBT. Pair it with the piece's own weakness (tenancy and land-size distribution) and you have both limbs of a 15-marker.
4. **One fact that doubles as an environment answer:** only **30–40%** of applied nitrogen is taken up by crops. The remainder is the **nitrous-oxide, nitrate-leaching and eutrophication** story — N₂O being roughly **273 times** CO₂ in global warming potential over a century. So fertiliser subsidy reform is simultaneously fiscal policy, soil policy and **climate mitigation**, which is exactly the kind of cross-domain link that lifts a GS3 answer.
5. **The "fossilisation and fuelisation" frame (23 October) is worth memorising as a pair.** Indian agriculture is squeezed from both ends by the same oil price: *fossilisation* — dependence on crude and gas for machinery, fertiliser and pesticides — and *fuelisation* — high crude prices pulling grain and cane toward ethanol. A deficient monsoon (piece 4) tightens both at once. The policy conclusion is **flexible blending**: treat blending targets as contingent on the harvest rather than fixed, and prioritise food over fuel in a deficit year.
6. **Cross-reference the examiner will notice:** this is the **input-versus-outcome** distinction again — ₹1.71 lakh crore of input subsidy measured by tonnes sold rather than by soil health or yield response, exactly as the 15 October pack argued about CSR spending and the 12 October RTI item argued about compliance audits. A candidate whose frameworks recur across domains reads as someone who thinks, not someone who revises.

## 4. "The other monsoon" — a deficit year, a strong El Niño, and a forecast nobody can act on

**Source:** The Hindu, **editorial, 26 October 2026**, on the northeast (retreating) monsoon.

**GS mapping:** **GS1 — Geography** (primary: important geophysical phenomena; climatology — monsoon mechanism, ocean–atmosphere interaction, El Niño/ENSO; distribution of key natural resources — water); **GS3 — Disaster Management** (disaster and disaster management, urban flooding, early warning); **GS2 — Governance** (urban local bodies and municipal capacity, centre–State–city coordination) as the delivery limb.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The **northeast monsoon** — which delivers most of Tamil Nadu's annual rain — is intrinsically harder to forecast than the southwest monsoon, and a **strong El Niño** widens the uncertainty further, making a swing from drought to deluge possible within the same season. Because the **2026 southwest monsoon failed**, the south enters this season already water-stressed; the editorial's point is that a dry start is **not** evidence of a dry season, and Chennai in particular must prepare for flood risk even while reservoirs are low.

### Key data & facts

- **Southwest monsoon 2026** ended about **12.6% short** — about **759.4 mm against a normal of 868.6 mm** — the **driest in 11 years**. *(Independently corroborated across multiple news outlets; the 868.6 mm figure is IMD's 1971–2020 long-period average, which is a good internal consistency check.)*
- **IMD's October–December outlook** for south Peninsular India is **"normal", defined as 88–112% of LPA** — a band of roughly ±12%, against IMD's roughly **±5%** error for the southwest monsoon. **This is the editorial's real point: the forecast is honest, and it is also too wide to plan against.**
- **Tamil Nadu, Puducherry and Karaikal receive about 48%** of their annual rainfall in **October–December**; the south Peninsula as a whole about **30%**.
- Historical anchor: on **1 December 2015**, **Chennai** recorded its heaviest **24-hour rainfall in over a century**; the floods killed **at least 500** people and displaced about **18 lakh**.
- **Mechanism to state correctly in an answer:** the northeast monsoon is the retreating-monsoon phase, when the ITCZ shifts south and north-easterly winds pick up moisture crossing the **Bay of Bengal**, delivering it to the leeward (Coromandel) coast. Its rain is largely **cyclone- and depression-driven**, i.e. a few discrete systems rather than a broad advancing current — which is precisely why seasonal totals are less predictable than the southwest monsoon's.
- **ENSO linkage, stated with its proper caveat:** El Niño is generally associated with a **weaker southwest monsoon** and a **wetter northeast monsoon** — but it raises **variance**, not just the mean. Warmer central and eastern Pacific waters alter Walker-circulation subsidence over the Indian landmass; the resulting northeast-monsoon signal is a tendency, not a forecast.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **A wide forecast band is scientific honesty, not institutional failure.** Punishing IMD for a ±12% range would push it toward false precision. The editorial's implicit answer is the right one — if the forecast cannot narrow, the **preparedness threshold** must move: plan for the upper bound of the range rather than the central estimate.
- **ENSO is not destiny.** El Niño years have produced both severe Chennai floods and poor northeast monsoons. IOD phase, Madden–Julian Oscillation timing and Bay cyclogenesis frequency all intervene. A GS1 answer that writes "El Niño ⇒ heavy northeast monsoon" as a rule is wrong; the examinable statement is about **amplified variability**.
- **The binding constraint in Chennai is hydrological and administrative, not meteorological.** Encroached and shrunken wetlands (Pallikaranai), construction on floodplains, a drainage network sized for a different rainfall regime, and reservoir operating rules that are optimised for storage during a drought year rather than for flood buffering. Better forecasts do not drain a filled-in marsh.
- **The two risks pull operating decisions in opposite directions, and this is the genuinely hard part.** After a 12.6% deficit, the rational instinct is to **store every drop**; flood safety requires **pre-release ahead of a heavy event**. A reservoir manager facing a ±12% seasonal forecast is being asked to choose between water security and flood security on information that does not distinguish them. That tension, named explicitly, is the best thing you can write about this editorial.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"A forecast too wide to act on is a preparedness problem, not a prediction problem."** The sharpest line here, and it transfers to heat action plans, glacial-lake outburst warnings, cyclone tracks and air-quality forecasting alike: where uncertainty is irreducible, policy must shift from *predicting the event* to *becoming insensitive to which outcome occurs*. That is the whole case for no-regret adaptation, and it is a strong GS3 disaster-management opening.
2. **Drought and flood are the same governance failure in one season.** This is the counter-intuitive framing examiners reward. India habitually treats them as separate departments with separate budgets, yet Chennai 2015 and Chennai 2019 were four years apart in the same city. One sentence: *a city that cannot store water also cannot drain it, because both capacities were built over.*
3. **Use the northeast monsoon to show real geographical understanding.** Most candidates know only the southwest monsoon. Being able to say *why* the retreating monsoon is harder to forecast — cyclone-driven, discrete systems, leeward coast, short season, ±12% band versus ±5% — is a cheap and very visible differentiator in GS1.
4. **The ENSO caveat is itself a scoring point.** Writing "El Niño increases variance rather than shifting the mean" shows you understand what a climate signal is. Add that the **southwest and northeast monsoons respond to El Niño in opposite directions**, which is why a single ENSO forecast cannot drive a single national water plan.
5. **Link to the fertiliser piece deliberately.** A **12.6% deficit** is simultaneously an irrigation-demand shock, a fertiliser-offtake shock (nutrient uptake depends on soil moisture, so the same urea does less work in a dry year) and an **ethanol-versus-food** question under E20. The "flexible blending" recommendation from the 23 October piece exists precisely for a year like this. Two pieces, one answer — that is how a weekly pack should be used.

## GS Mains practice question — rotated paper: **GS2 (Governance — health and nutrition policy)**

> *Paper rotation check: the last four packs carried **GS3 internal security (17 Sep)**, **GS1 society (5 Oct)**, **GS3 economy (15 Oct)** and **GS4 ethics (22 Oct)**. GS2 last carried the weekly question on **10 September** (IR) — the longest gap, and no GS2 **governance** question has appeared in this run at all. Piece 2 supports it properly rather than by retrofit. Directive rotation: recent packs used* examine, critically examine, assess, evaluate, discuss, elucidate, critically analyse, analyse, comment *and* critically evaluate *— so this week uses the unused* **"to what extent"** *construction, which forces a graded judgement rather than a list.*

> **Q.** Anaemia prevalence in India has not declined across successive National Family Health Surveys, even as the architecture of nutrition schemes has expanded. **To what extent** can large-scale food fortification delivered through existing safety-net programmes address this, and what must accompany it? **(15 marks, 250 words)**

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Open by naming the puzzle, not the number.** Coverage expanded — Anaemia Mukt Bharat's 7×7×7 framework since 2018, 15.4 crore children and adolescents reached with IFA in a single quarter of FY 2024-25, antenatal screening through PMSMA — while **prevalence rose** between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 (children 6–59 months 67.1%, women 57.0%, adolescent girls 59.1%). One line of diagnosis: the shortfall is in the **delivery instrument**, not in coverage or intent.

2. **Why supplementation under-performs — three reasons, one sentence each.** *(a)* **Daily-compliance dependence:** a pill-a-day instrument fails exactly among the households with the least time and the weakest contact with the health system. *(b)* **Wrong single nutrient:** anaemia in India is multi-deficiency — B12 deficiency in about one-sixth of pre-school children and one-third of adolescents, plus widespread folate deficiency (CNNS) — so iron alone cannot close the gap. *(c)* **The dietary-diversity advice is unaffordable:** only 6% of children aged 6–23 months get a minimum acceptable diet and nearly two-thirds of the rural poor cannot afford a diet meeting national guidelines, so counselling without income or food-system change is advice against a budget constraint.

3. **State the case for fortification at its strongest — the vehicle argument.** Fortification works *because it asks nothing of the beneficiary*. Salt already reaches about 95% of households, rice about 65% of the population at about 6.8 kg per person per month, and both already travel through the PDS, PM POSHAN and ICDS. DFS costs about **₹14–20 per person per year** at a **4.2:1** benefit–cost ratio, can reduce anaemia by **up to 21%** on global evidence, already reaches about **76 million** people in four States, and uses an **ICMR-NIN encapsulated-iron** formulation that solves the stability and palatability problems that defeated earlier attempts. Against an iron-deficiency burden cited at around **4% of GDP**, this is among the highest-return public-health instruments available.

4. **Then bound the claim — this is where "to what extent" is actually answered.** Four limits, honestly stated: *(i)* the **vehicle is salt**, and Indian intake already exceeds the WHO's 5 g/day ceiling, so fortification must be paired with sodium reduction or it works against another health goal; *(ii)* **haemoglobinopathies** — thalassaemia and sickle cell disease — require screening and labelling safeguards, which is the price of universality; *(iii)* **measurement** — contested haemoglobin cut-offs and venous-versus-capillary sampling mean the baseline itself is under revision through DABS-I, so the programme's scale is uncertain; *(iv)* **non-nutritional causes** — malaria, worm load, sanitation, blood loss — are untouched by any fortificant. Verdict sentence: fortification can deliver a **substantial fraction** of the achievable reduction at very low cost, but it cannot deliver the **whole** of it, and treating it as a complete answer is the risk.

5. **Close on what must accompany it, framed as governance rather than as a wish-list.** **Financing:** PDS commodity choice rests with States, so a central component without dedicated funds is advice, not policy. **Standards and small producers:** accredited testing plus support for small salt producers, or the quality floor excludes the smaller industry. **Convergence with accountability:** Health (AMB), Food & Public Distribution (PDS), WCD (ICDS, PM POSHAN), Education, FSSAI and the States each hold one lever and none holds the outcome — so the monitoring metric must be **haemoglobin outcomes, not tonnes of fortified salt distributed**. End with the transferable line: *the most effective welfare instrument is the one that works when the beneficiary does nothing — but only if it is measured by what changes in the beneficiary, not by what the state dispatched.*

*Traps in this question: **(a)** writing a GS3 food-security answer about PDS mechanics and buffer stocks — the paper is asking about **policy design and delivery institutions**, so PDS appears as a **vehicle**, not as a subject; **(b)** answering "yes, fortify everything" — the directive is "to what extent", so an answer with no stated limits cannot score above the middle band however accurate its facts; **(c)** omitting the haemoglobinopathy and salt-intake objections, which are the two that an informed examiner is waiting for; **(d)** spending words on the history of anaemia programmes instead of on the instrument comparison. Budget your words roughly 40 : 90 : 70 : 50 across points 1–2, 3, 4 and 5 — the graded judgement in point 4 is where the marks sit.*

## Verification notes

Read these before putting any figure in an answer.

1. **Kudankulam timeline.** The breach was **reported in mid-July 2026**; the 27 October op-ed is a **policy retrospective**. Writing it as an October incident is a factual error. NPCIL's position — conventional/non-nuclear data only, control and safety systems unaffected — is **technically substantive** and should be stated, not dismissed.
2. **Potash requirement — conflicting figures.** Two secondary digests of the same Indian Express op-ed give the requirement as **22 kg/ha** and **32 kg/ha** against an application of **9 kg/ha**. The application figure is consistent; the denominator is not. **Write "roughly a third of agronomic requirement"** and cite no point figure.
3. **Nitrogen deviation endpoint.** Digests render the shift as **−7% → +6%** and **−7% → +9%**. The direction (from under-application to over-application) is solid; the endpoint is not. Say "from about 7% below the recommended dose to above it".
4. **Irrigated share of gross cropped area.** 23 October gives about **56%**; Ashok Gulati's 12 October piece gave about **60%**. Both are defensible on different series and years — cite **"roughly 55–60%"**.
5. **GERD as a share of GDP.** The 24 October AI piece cites **0.84% (FY24)**; the 15 October pack flagged a range of **0.6–0.7%** and corrected a digest that printed an impossible "84% of GDP". Attribute **0.84% to the authors** of that op-ed, and never present both figures in one answer as if they were the same series.
6. **"4% of GDP" for iron-deficiency anaemia.** A widely cited estimate from the nutrition-economics literature, not an official national-accounts figure. Write "estimated at around 4% of GDP" and attribute it.
7. **DFS impact projections.** The **₹2,800 crore / 16.6 million DALYs** and **94.1 million cases / ₹8,098 crore over five years** figures are **modelled projections cited by the authors**, not measured outcomes. Present them as such; the measured figures are the **76 million people reached**, the **₹14–20 per person per year** cost and the **up-to-21%** reduction from global review evidence.
8. **Anaemia prevalence baseline is itself contested.** The haemoglobin cut-off and venous-versus-capillary measurement debate, and the move to **DABS-I**, mean NFHS prevalence may be overstated. The NFHS-5 numbers are correctly quoted here; the caveat belongs in the answer, not in a footnote.
9. **ENSO.** Do **not** write "El Niño means a strong northeast monsoon". The defensible statement is that El Niño is associated with a **weaker southwest monsoon** and a **tendency** toward a wetter northeast monsoon, and above all with **greater variance** — which is the editorial's actual argument.
10. **Monsoon deficit figure.** The **12.6% shortfall, 759.4 mm against an 868.6 mm normal, driest in 11 years** is independently corroborated across several news outlets, and 868.6 mm is IMD's published 1971–2020 LPA. Safe to cite.
11. **No quotation in this pack is presented as a verbatim quote from any editorial.** All arguments are paraphrased and attributed to the piece and the paper. Where a formulation is this pack's own ("the perimeter is now wherever the data is", "choose the vehicle, not just the nutrient"), it is a study aid, not something to attribute to the author in an answer.
12. **Sourcing caveat, stated plainly.** Several of these pieces were read through UPSC editorial-digest summaries rather than the newspapers' own pages. Headlines, dates and papers are recorded as those digests give them, and the major quantitative claims were cross-checked against independent reporting where available (the monsoon deficit, the Kudankulam breach, the ICRIER authorship). Figures still carrying any ambiguity are flagged above rather than smoothed over.
