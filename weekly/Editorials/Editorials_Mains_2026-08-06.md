## 1. Fiscal federalism — efficiency versus equity

**Source:** The Hindu, Lead article (~6 August 2026) · *GS2 (federalism; devolution of powers and finances; Finance Commission) + GS3 (economy — public finance, fiscal policy)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The 16th Finance Commission (FC-16), covering 2026–31, has kept the States' share of the divisible pool unchanged at 41% but has restructured grants in a way that shifts the Commission's centre of gravity from **equalisation to performance**. By discontinuing Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs), sector-specific and State-specific grants, FC-16 treats States as broadly comparable fiscal units. The editorial's core claim is that this conflates **inefficiency with structural disadvantage** — and that when it is paired with the Union's continued freedom to raise cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool, the burden of fiscal adjustment falls asymmetrically on the States.

### Key data & facts

- FC-16 report **tabled in Parliament on 1 February 2026**; award period **2026-27 to 2030-31**.
- States' share of the divisible pool held at **41%** — the same as FC-15 — despite **18 States demanding 50%**.
- Total grants: **≈₹9.47 lakh crore** (FC-15: ≈₹10.1 lakh crore). Their share of total FC transfers falls to **8.3%**.
- Grants are now confined to **local bodies and disaster management**. Per PRS, local-body grants are **₹4.4 lakh crore (rural) + ₹3.6 lakh crore (urban)**, split **80% basic / 20% performance-linked**. *(The Hindu piece cites a slightly lower local-body figure of ~₹7.2 lakh crore; use the PRS split, which reconciles with the ₹9.47 lakh crore total once disaster-management grants are added.)*
- **Discontinued:** Revenue Deficit Grants, sector-specific grants, State-specific grants — all three retained by FC-14 and FC-15.
- Devolution formula: weight on **income distance cut from 45% to 42.5%**; a new **10% weight on contribution to GDP**.
- **Article 275** is the constitutional basis for grants-in-aid; the editorial reads it as an explicit acknowledgement that formula-based devolution alone cannot handle inter-State disparity.
- FC-16's proposed **"grand bargain"**: the Union gradually merges cesses and surcharges into the divisible pool, in exchange for States accepting a lower headline devolution share.

### The other side / counter-argument

The case *for* FC-16's approach is a moral-hazard argument, and the editorial concedes it is legitimate. RDGs are an open-ended transfer to States that run revenue deficits — which rewards weak own-revenue mobilisation and soft budget constraints, and penalises States that taxed and spent prudently. Performance- and compliance-linked local-body grants (tied to water, sanitation, revenue mobilisation and audited accounts) are a real accountability gain in a tier of government notorious for unaudited books. And holding devolution at 41% while adding a GDP-contribution weight is defensible as recognising that high-output States generate the pool everyone draws from. The honest framing is a trade-off, not a verdict: **equalisation without conditionality entrenches dependence; conditionality without equalisation entrenches disparity.**

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Fiscal discipline must distinguish inefficiency from structural disadvantage."** This one line is the entire editorial and works as a thesis sentence in any Centre–State finance answer.
- **The cess asymmetry is the sharpest institutional point available:** States are being asked to internalise hard budget constraints while the Union retains an off-pool revenue instrument. Naming this as *vertical imbalance being reinforced rather than corrected* is a high-value formulation.
- **The conditionality–autonomy trade-off at the third tier:** ₹8 lakh crore to local bodies is the largest-ever local devolution, but 20% is performance-tied and the basic component carries compliance conditions. More money with less discretion is not straightforwardly more decentralisation — a point that cross-links to the 74th Amendment material from the 30 July pack.
- **Closing line worth memorising:** fiscal federalism is ultimately an instrument of *national integration*; its legitimacy rests on States perceiving the system as both efficient and fair.

## 2. Jammu and Kashmir — the elusive quest for Statehood

**Source:** The Hindu, Lead article (~5 August 2026) · *GS2 (federalism; Union Territories; Centre–State relations; constitutional morality) + GS3 (internal security — linkages between development and extremism) + GS1 (regional alienation)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Seven years after the August 2019 changes to Article 370 and the withdrawal of Statehood, the editorial argues the security-first model has hit its ceiling. Violence has not ended and has spread to districts previously less affected; the promised economic dividend has not arrived; and restored elections without restored powers produce **representation without accountability**. Its central claim is that Statehood should be understood not as an administrative housekeeping matter but as a **confidence-building instrument** — the mechanism by which grievance gets a peaceful channel.

### Key data & facts

- The 2019 package: alteration of **Article 370**, withdrawal of Statehood, bifurcation into Union Territories, alongside large-scale troop deployment, preventive detentions, curfews and communications shutdowns.
- **J&K's per-capita income relative to the national average has declined** since 2019; unemployment remains high, and is **particularly acute among graduates**.
- Substantial executive power sits with the **Lieutenant-Governor**, an appointee answerable to the Union rather than to the electorate — the structural source of what the editorial calls the accountability deficit.
- Assembly elections have been restored and pluralist political forces continue to contest and participate — the editorial reads this as evidence of democratic resilience, not of normalisation achieved.

### The other side / counter-argument

The Union's position, which the editorial does not fully engage, is sequencing rather than principle: Statehood restoration has been publicly committed to, but the government's argument is that it should follow demonstrated stability rather than precede it, and that premature devolution of police and public-order powers in a theatre facing cross-border militancy carries real security cost. Supporters also point to measurable post-2019 gains — record tourist arrivals in some years, extension of central laws and welfare entitlements previously blocked by the special-status framework, and panchayat/DDC elections that created a third tier where none functioned. And the Supreme Court in *In re: Article 370* (2023) upheld the abrogation while directing that Statehood be restored "at the earliest" — so the constitutional question is settled even as the political timeline is not.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Elections without powers produce representation without accountability"** — a portable line for any answer on Union Territories, Delhi's NCT dispute, or the LG-versus-elected-government question.
- **Reframe the security–development–democracy relationship as three legs, not a sequence.** The editorial's structural claim is that security and development are both *necessary and insufficient* without political legitimacy; answers that present them as stages ("first security, then development, then democracy") miss the argument.
- **Pair this with pick #1 explicitly.** FC-16 is the *fiscal* asymmetry; J&K is the *political* asymmetry. Together they let you argue in a GS2 answer that federal stress in India today is less about the formal constitutional scheme than about **where discretion sits** — off-pool cesses in one case, LG powers in the other.
- **Constitutional-process point:** "constitutional processes must remain as important as constitutional outcomes" — usable in any answer on constitutional morality, and it pairs with the SC's *fair-and-reasoned* citizenship standard noted in the 23 July pack.

## 3. NATO enters a new strategic era of transformation

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (~5 August 2026) · *GS2 (IR — effect of policies of developed countries on India's interests; important international groupings)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

For the first time since 1949, Washington is asking Europe not merely to spend more but to assume **primary responsibility** for its own conventional defence, while the US repositions itself as strategic enabler, nuclear guarantor and political convener. The op-ed argues this is redistribution, not retreat — and that the consequence for India is a genuine, sustained reallocation of American strategic attention toward the Indo-Pacific.

### Key data & facts

- The **US National Security Strategy (November 2025)** retains NATO as America's principal alliance but signals reduced US commitment as Europe's primary security guarantor; endorses **5% of GDP** as the new burden-sharing benchmark.
- US troop strength in Europe peaked at **~100,000** after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine; during 2025-26, **5,000 troops withdrawn from Germany**, a planned **4,000-troop Poland deployment cancelled**, and **Brigade Combat Teams cut from four to three** — bringing presence to **75,000–80,000**, near pre-war levels.
- **Ankara Summit (July 2026):** reaffirmed **Article 5**; pledged **€70 billion** in military assistance to Ukraine for 2026 with comparable support planned for 2027; endorsed European-led long-range precision-strike development and a stronger NATO defence-industrial base.
- **Hague Summit (2025):** commitment to at least **3.5% of GDP** core defence spending by 2035, with progress reviewed in 2029. **Germany** has doubled defence spending since 2023.
- The op-ed's three-phase framing: **NATO 1.0 (1949–91)** US-led Soviet deterrence → **NATO 2.0 (1991–2022)** eastward expansion, out-of-area operations, falling European spending → **NATO 3.0 (post-2022)** Europe owns conventional defence, US retains nuclear deterrence, intelligence and global power projection.
- Lord Ismay's original formulation — keep "the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down" — is updated by the author to: **Russia deterred, America engaged, Europe prepared.**

### The other side / counter-argument

The sceptical reading is that Europe's constraint is capability, not budget, and money does not buy an ecosystem. Europe remains dependent on the US for **intelligence, satellite support, command-and-control, strategic airlift, missile defence and the nuclear umbrella** — none of which is procurable on a 2035 timeline. There is also a credibility risk: a visible drawdown can be read in Moscow as a weakening of Article 5 well before European capability catches up, making the transition itself the danger. The counter-counter, which the op-ed quotes from Poland's Foreign Minister, is that Europe does not need to match American power — only to be stronger than Russia.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Redistribution, not retreat"** is the crisp characterisation to lead with; it avoids the lazy "American decline" framing that examiners see constantly.
- **The India payoff is two-track:** (i) more sustained US bandwidth in the Indo-Pacific improves the regional balance against Chinese assertiveness; (ii) a rearming Europe becomes a *second* supplier pole for defence manufacturing, AI, cybersecurity, semiconductors and space — which is itself an instrument of strategic autonomy, since autonomy is a function of *number of options*, not of distance from everyone.
- **Useful analytical distinction:** alliance *burden-sharing* (who pays) versus alliance *capability-sharing* (who can actually do the thing). Europe is solving the first faster than the second. This distinction transfers to answers on Quad, I2U2 and BRICS.

## 4. A climate-resilience pathway between India and China

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (~6 August 2026) · *GS2 (IR — India and its neighbourhood; bilateral relations) + GS3 (environment — climate adaptation, disaster management; economy — climate finance)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India and China face near-identical climate stress — a delayed monsoon followed by intense rainfall flooded Mumbai, Surat, Assam and Odisha this year, while Guangxi, Shaanxi and Gansu saw comparable deluges. The op-ed argues that disaster resilience and urban adaptation are a **low-risk, low-politics entry point** for rebuilding bilateral trust, precisely because they are technical, municipal and mutually beneficial rather than zero-sum.

### Key data & facts

- A **Chinese climate envoy visited New Delhi in April 2026**, reviving the question of climate as a cooperation channel.
- India–China climate and disaster-management engagement dates from the **early 1990s to 2020**: joint statements and MoUs covering floods, earthquakes and droughts, pledging joint research, **hydrological and seismic data sharing**, and exchange of governance practice.
- **Sister-city agreements:** Delhi–Beijing, Mumbai–Shanghai, Chennai–Chongqing — largely dormant in practice.
- **Water-sharing agreements between the two countries lapsed in 2022** — leaving the Brahmaputra/Sutlej hydrological data question unresolved.
- Complementarity claimed: **China** has embedded resilience into core urban and rural planning using data-driven approaches (sponge cities, drainage modernisation); **India** is stronger on adaptive, community-led governance — early warning systems, **heat action plans**, cool roofs, nature-based solutions.
- Both economies rely overwhelmingly on **public funds** for adaptation; the op-ed calls for blended finance, **municipal bonds** and credit-enhancement tools, all held back by weak local capacity and regulatory gaps.

### The other side / counter-argument

The realist objection is that the asymmetry is the problem, not the weather. China is the **upper riparian** on the Brahmaputra and has begun construction on very large hydropower capacity in the Yarlung Tsangpo bend; in that structural position, "data sharing" is a concession China grants and withdraws as leverage — which is exactly what the lapse of the 2022 MoUs demonstrates. Sequencing cooperation from the technical to the political also has a poor track record here: the 1990s–2010s MoUs coexisted with, and did not soften, the boundary dispute. A sharper version of the objection: functional cooperation on adaptation may *legitimise* the relationship without changing its terms.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Climate change respects no borders"** is true but weak on its own; the examinable version is the **functionalist hypothesis** — that cooperation in low-politics domains spills over into high-politics trust. State the hypothesis, then test it against the 1990s–2020 MoU record, which is the more sophisticated answer.
- **The riparian asymmetry is the fact that decides the answer.** Any India–China cooperation answer that omits the upper-riparian position and the lapsed 2022 water MoUs is missing the load-bearing constraint.
- **Municipal-level engagement is the genuinely novel policy suggestion** — sister-city technical exchange on drainage, sponge-city design and waste management sidesteps the strategic relationship entirely and is politically cheap. Good as a "way forward" bullet.
- **Climate-adaptation finance** is a reusable GS3 point on its own: public funds cannot carry adaptation, and the missing infrastructure is municipal creditworthiness — which loops back to FC-16's local-body grants in pick #1.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS2 — Federalism and Centre–State relations**)

> **"The Sixteenth Finance Commission has shifted the centre of gravity of Union–State fiscal relations from equalisation to performance."** Critically analyse this proposition in the light of the discontinuation of revenue deficit grants and the continued exclusion of cesses and surcharges from the divisible pool. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

*Maps to: GS2 — functions and responsibilities of the Union and the States; issues and challenges pertaining to the federal structure; devolution of powers and finances; constitutional bodies. Directive **"critically analyse"** is deliberately fresh — recent weeks used examine / critically examine / discuss / elucidate. Note what "critically analyse" demands: break the proposition into its components, weigh evidence on both sides, and land on a reasoned position. An answer that only lists FC-16's recommendations will not clear the analysis bar.*

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Frame the constitutional purpose before the critique.** Open with the Finance Commission's Article 280 mandate and Article 275's grants-in-aid as an explicit constitutional acknowledgement that formula-based devolution cannot, by itself, equalise across States with different geographies, demographies and social-sector obligations. Establish equalisation as the historical default — FC-14 and FC-15 both retained RDGs plus sector- and State-specific grants — so the shift is real and needs justifying.

2. **Establish the shift with evidence.** Devolution held at **41%** despite 18 States seeking 50%; grants down to **≈₹9.47 lakh crore** from ≈₹10.1 lakh crore and to **8.3%** of total FC transfers; RDGs, sector-specific and State-specific grants all discontinued; grants now confined to local bodies (**₹4.4 lakh crore rural + ₹3.6 lakh crore urban**, 80:20 basic-to-performance) and disaster management; **income-distance weight cut 45% → 42.5%**, a new **10% GDP-contribution weight** added. The direction of travel is unambiguous.

3. **Concede the case for the shift — this is what earns the "critically".** RDGs create moral hazard: an open-ended deficit transfer weakens the incentive to mobilise own revenue and softens the budget constraint, penalising prudent States. Performance-linked local-body grants tied to audited accounts, water and sanitation are a genuine accountability gain in the least-audited tier of Indian government. Rewarding GDP contribution recognises that high-output States generate the pool. A pure-equalisation regime is not costless.

4. **State the counter-case as an institutional asymmetry, not a grievance.** Fiscal weakness is not always fiscal failure — hill-State infrastructure costs, north-eastern connectivity constraints, Kerala's human-capital burden and Punjab's food-security role are structural, and RDGs existed to address exactly these. The asymmetry is sharpened by **cesses and surcharges remaining outside the divisible pool**: States face hardening budget constraints while the Union retains an off-pool revenue instrument. FC-16's proposed grand bargain — merging cesses in exchange for a lower headline devolution share — trades a certain reduction for an uncertain gain. The net effect risks reinforcing vertical imbalance rather than correcting it. Add the third-tier point: more money with tighter conditionality is not the same as more decentralisation.

5. **Land a reasoned position and close on principle.** Argue for a *both/and* design rather than a swing between poles: retain need-based equalisation for demonstrably structural disadvantage while layering performance incentives on the remainder; make the cess-merger commitment binding and time-bound rather than aspirational; keep an untied core within local-body grants to protect autonomy; and build an objective test that separates structural constraint from managerial failure, so that discipline does not become a proxy for punishment. Close on the integrative point — fiscal federalism is an instrument of national integration, and its legitimacy depends on States perceiving the system as simultaneously efficient and fair.

## Next step

Write this answer in 250 words, hand-timed, then run the **`mains-answer-feedback`** task on it. Two things to watch on this particular question: (i) "critically analyse" is not "discuss" — if your fourth paragraph doesn't contain a real concession to the *other* side, you have written a one-sided answer; and (ii) the cess-and-surcharge point is where the marks separate, so don't let it become a throwaway line at the end.
