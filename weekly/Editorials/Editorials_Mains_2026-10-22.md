## 1. Expanding the Lok Sabha without expanding Parliament's capacity

*The Hindu, unsigned editorial, ~19 October 2026 — "Revamp functioning of Parliament"*

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Polity & Governance** (primary: Parliament and State legislatures — structure, functioning, conduct of business, powers and privileges; separation of powers; delimitation and representation; parliamentary committees); **GS2 — Constitutional amendment** (procedure, special majority); **GS1 — Indian Society** (regional disparity in representation) as a secondary hook.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Delimitation is being debated almost entirely as a question of *how many* seats each State gets, when the binding constraint on representation is Parliament's *working capacity*. Raising the Lok Sabha from 550 to 850 while sitting days, Question Hour and committee scrutiny continue to erode would multiply MPs without multiplying the ability of any MP to question the executive, debate a Bill or amend a Budget. The editorial's position is not that expansion is wrong, but that expansion without procedural reform converts a representation gain into a representation illusion.

### Key data & facts

**The expansion proposal and its legislative history**
- The **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** was introduced in the Lok Sabha on **16 April 2026** by the Law Ministry, and was **negatived on 17 April 2026 — 298 in favour, 230 against**: a simple majority, but well short of the **two-thirds of members present and voting** that Article 368 requires. A constitutional amendment failing on the floor is itself a rare and examinable fact.
- Its stated purpose was to operationalise the **one-third reservation for women** under the **106th Amendment (2023)** without waiting for the next census and a fresh delimitation, which the Bill's statement of objects said would "delay" women's participation.
- The companion **Delimitation Bill, 2026** would raise the Lok Sabha maximum from **550 to 850** — States up to **815** (from 530) and UTs up to **35** (from 20). The **Rajya Sabha stays at 250**, so the Lok Sabha–Rajya Sabha ratio moves from about **2.2:1 to 3.3:1** — which also shifts the arithmetic of a joint sitting under **Article 108** towards the lower House.
- Consequential effect most candidates miss: the **Article 75(1A)** cap on the Council of Ministers at 15% of Lok Sabha strength would rise from **81 to 122** ministers.
- A **special session in the last week of October 2026** has been widely expected to take up delimitation and women's reservation again. **[verify — reported, not notified at the time of writing.]**

**The freeze being unfrozen**
- Seat allocation has been frozen on **1971 population** by the **42nd Amendment**, extended by the **84th Amendment (2001)** until "the first census taken after 2026".
- The **2027 Census has a reference date of 1 March 2027**, with data expected around **December 2027**. The last Delimitation Commission was constituted in **2002** and its orders were finalised in **2008**. The next general election is due in **2029**, which PRS reads as too soon for a 2027-census-based delimitation to be completed.
- **Read this distinction carefully, because it is where most answers go wrong.** Two different exercises are in play: (i) *reallocating the existing 543 seats* by population, which on 2011 figures would move **Tamil Nadu 39 → 32, Kerala 20 → 15, Uttar Pradesh 80 → 89, Bihar 40 → 46, Rajasthan 25 → 30**; and (ii) *expanding the House to 815 State seats*, reported as a **flat 50% increase for every State**, which would leave relative shares roughly intact while every State gains in absolute terms. The southern objection bites hard on (i) and much less on (ii) — so an answer that cites the 39 → 32 numbers as "what the Bill does" is describing the wrong scenario.

**The capacity data (the editorial's real point)**
- **97.98 crore** voters across **543** constituencies (ECI Atlas, 2024) — about **18 lakh** voters per constituency on average, against **Malkajgiri (Telangana) at 37.8 lakh** and **Lakshadweep at 57,953**.
- Lok Sabha rules allow up to **20 starred and 230 unstarred questions** per sitting day.
- **Question Hour ran for 60% of its scheduled time in the 17th Lok Sabha**, and for about **nine minutes — roughly 1% — in the 2026 Monsoon Session**. **[verify]**
- In the 17th Lok Sabha, **35% of Bills were passed after less than an hour of debate**, and about **80% of the Union Budget** was passed through the **guillotine**.
- **Article 85(1)** caps the gap between sessions at six months but prescribes **no minimum number of sitting days**. The **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution** recommended at least **120 sitting days** a year.
- Maximum membership of a parliamentary committee is **31**, which the editorial suggests reviewing upward alongside any expansion.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The representation case for expansion is genuinely strong**, and the editorial concedes it. An average of 18 lakh voters per MP is far beyond any comparable democracy; a 37.8 lakh-voter constituency cannot be serviced, and freezing seats on 1971 population indefinitely penalises population growth in States that were not consulted about the freeze.
- **The freeze was itself an exception, not the constitutional norm.** Article 82 contemplates readjustment after *every* census; the 42nd and 84th Amendments suspended it as a deliberate incentive for population stabilisation. The constitutional default is re-allocation, which puts the burden of justification on those wanting the freeze continued.
- **Capacity and size are partly substitutes, not only complements.** More MPs mean more committee slots, more constituencies small enough to be worked, and more legislators available for scrutiny — some of the capacity problem is *caused* by too few members for too much work.
- **The procedural failures have a political, not a numerical, cause.** Question Hour collapses because it is suspended or disrupted, and Bills pass without debate because the majority chooses to pass them that way. Fixing sitting days by law does not fix either. A sceptic would say the editorial is prescribing procedure for a problem of political will.
- **The women's-reservation sequencing cuts both ways.** The government's argument is that waiting for census-plus-delimitation delays women's representation by years; the opposition's is that the 33% quota should be delinked from delimitation entirely and implemented now. Both positions are *pro*-reservation; the disagreement is about whether delimitation is a precondition or a pretext.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Representation is a function of voice, not only of headcount."** This is the week's single most portable line for GS2. One MP in a 543-member House with a nine-minute Question Hour has more formal entitlement than real capacity; adding 300 colleagues does not change the second. Use it to open any answer on parliamentary reform, and note that **the same logic applies to State legislatures**, whose sitting days are even lower.
2. **The rejected amendment is a rare live example of Article 368 doing its job.** 298–230 is a majority of the House that still fails a special majority. Cite it in answers on the amending power, on why constitutional entrenchment matters, and on the difference between a government's parliamentary strength and its constitutional strength — far better than the usual textbook recitation of Kesavananda.
3. **Two reform lists, not one.** Keep them separate in the answer: *representation* reforms (delimitation basis, House size, women's reservation, delinking the 106th Amendment from the census) and *capacity* reforms (minimum statutory sitting days, protected Question Hour, pre-legislative scrutiny, mandatory committee referral of Bills, larger and better-staffed committees, curbs on the guillotine). The editorial's argument is precisely that India is doing the first list while the second decays — a structure that writes itself into intro-body-conclusion.
4. **The federal-equity point, framed honestly.** Population-based re-allocation penalises exactly the States that achieved demographic transition fastest — a *success penalty*. The cleanest formulation: *India is being asked to decide whether representation tracks numbers or tracks the social contract that asked some States to reduce their numbers.* Offer the standard menu of fixes (flat proportionate expansion that preserves relative shares; a floor for States that lose; strengthening the Rajya Sabha as the chamber of federal equality) rather than taking a side.
5. **The Article 75(1A) consequence is a differentiator.** Almost no answer notices that expanding the House mechanically expands the permissible size of the Council of Ministers from 81 to 122. Add one sentence on it and you have shown you read the Bill rather than the headline.

## 2. Demography, data, and the question of who the state is counting

*The Indian Express, unsigned editorial, ~21 October 2026 — "Demography, data and the politics of population"*

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Governance & Rights Issues** (primary: statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies; government policies and interventions in design and implementation; Fundamental Rights — Articles 14, 15 and 21; citizenship; e-governance and its limitations); **GS4 — Ethics** (ethical concerns in governance, probity, information sharing and transparency, confidentiality, use of technology); **GS1 — Indian Society** (population and associated issues, communalism, secularism); GS3 — Internal Security (border management, illegal migration) as the government's own framing.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The editorial argues that the **High-Level Committee on Demographic Changes (HLCDC)** is largely redundant — India already has a Census, a Civil Registration System, the NFHS and several administrative databases — and that its composition contains no professional demographer, while its method substitutes religion-wise comparison for demographic science. Combined with the Census, the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls and a possible NRC, it warns that the output could function as a profiling database rather than a study. Its prescription is institutional separation: keep population science, electoral administration and law enforcement apart, and deal with illegal immigration through immigration law rather than religious comparison.

### Key data & facts

**What the committee is**
- Constituted by an **MHA notification of 26 May 2026**, following the Prime Minister's announcement of a **"High-powered Demography Mission" on 15 August 2025** and Cabinet approval on **11 September 2025**. It must submit a **final report within one year**.
- **Chair:** Justice (Retd.) **Prakash Prabhakar Naolekar**. **Member Secretary:** Joint Secretary (Foreigners-I), MHA. **Members:** the **Census Commissioner**, **Durga Shankar Mishra** (retired IAS), **Balaji Srivastava** (retired IPS), and **Dr Shamika Ravi** (economist, PM's Economic Advisory Council). The editorial's central structural objection: **no demographer**, and a composition weighted towards home-affairs and security experience.
- **Terms of reference** include examining demographic change "including illegal immigration"; studying causes such as birth-rate differentials and cross-border movement; **analysing population change across religious and social groups**; recommending a permanent mechanism for identifying, detaining and deporting illegal immigrants; and strengthening border control and identification systems. It may requisition information from any ministry, State government, public authority **or individual**.

**What it has asked for**
- A reported **35-point questionnaire** seeking **15 years of religion-wise data** from States, for **Hindus, Sikhs, Christians, Muslims, Parsis and Buddhists**, covering **birth registrations, school enrolment, property transactions and vehicle registrations**; "unusual" increases in **Aadhaar, voter-ID and ration-card registrations**; **district-wise religious structures** built over 15 years; **conversions among Scheduled Tribe communities**; voter registrations of people lacking previous-generation birth certificates or family records; and details of **detention centres, deportations and repatriations**. It also asks whether new legislation is needed to strengthen surveillance (The Hindu, 29 September 2026).
- **States have reportedly been unable to answer**, because Indian administrative records — births, school admissions, vehicles, property — are **not maintained by religion**. This is the most analytically important fact in the whole story: the data being requisitioned largely does not exist in the form requested, which means any answer must be *constructed*.
- The committee's website carries a public **"share your inputs"** section inviting individuals and organisations to submit "evidence, data or documented experience" of demographic change in their area — explicitly including **videos and photographs** — with **no verification process or safeguards described** (reported by 15 October 2026). Critics have compared it to the use of **Form 7** (objection to another person's enrolment) during the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls.

**The infrastructure that already exists**
- The **Census** (reference date 1 March 2027; data expected around December 2027; religion is already a Census item), the **Civil Registration System** for vital statistics, the **National Family Health Survey** run with **IIPS Mumbai**, and administrative databases including Aadhaar, ration cards and electoral rolls.
- Constitutional provisions invoked: **Articles 14** (equality before law), **15** (non-discrimination on grounds including religion) and **21** (life and personal liberty, read with privacy after *Puttaswamy*).
- Historical comparator: the **Sachar Committee (2005)**, constituted to study the social and economic status of Muslims — criticised at the time from the opposite direction as "minority appeasement". That symmetry is useful and should be stated.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The underlying concern is legitimate and the editorial concedes it.** Undocumented immigration, forged documents and fraudulent enrolment in benefit and voter databases are real governance problems, and a State is entitled — on any theory of sovereignty — to know who its residents are. Border districts do experience settlement patterns that administrative data captures poorly.
- **Studying group-wise differentials is not inherently illegitimate.** Indian public policy routinely disaggregates by caste, religion and tribe — NFHS, the Sachar Committee, SC/ST/OBC welfare data, minority scholarship schemes. A rule that forbids religion-wise analysis would also forbid the evidence base for affirmative action. The objection has to be about *purpose and consequence*, not about disaggregation as such.
- **A judicially chaired committee with a Census Commissioner on it is not a security agency.** The chair is a retired Supreme Court judge, the body can only recommend, and its report will be a public document subject to scrutiny, litigation and parliamentary questioning.
- **Public consultation is normal practice.** Law commissions, parliamentary committees and regulators routinely invite public submissions; inviting inputs is not by itself the same as inviting denunciation. The sharper version of the criticism is narrower and stronger: an open portal is defensible for *views*, questionable for *allegations about identifiable third parties*, and indefensible without a verification standard and a right of reply.
- **Redundancy is a weaker objection than it looks.** Committees are routinely set up to synthesise data that already exists; the Sachar Committee itself mostly reanalysed Census and NSS data. The real question is not whether the committee duplicates the Census but whether its *framing* — change attributed in advance to "external abnormal factors" — predetermines its finding.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"The data being asked for does not exist in the form requested — so it will have to be constructed."** This is the most powerful single sentence available on the issue, and it is a *methodological* point, not a political one, which is exactly why it scores. Where records are not kept by religion, a religion-wise series can only be produced by inference, proxy or name-based classification — all of which carry error rates that fall unevenly across groups. Carry this into any GS2 answer on evidence-based policy and any GS3 answer on statistical governance.
2. **Separation of functions is the reusable constitutional principle.** Population science (Census/NFHS), electoral administration (ECI) and law enforcement (MHA) serve different purposes, answer to different authorities, and acquire information under different guarantees. **Purpose limitation** — data collected for one purpose not being repurposed for another — is the governance doctrine here, and it links directly to the **DPDP Act, 2023** and to *Puttaswamy*'s proportionality test. Usable far beyond this case: welfare databases, health records, CCTV, facial recognition.
3. **A strong answer names the standard rather than the villain.** Four tests you can apply to any state data exercise, in this order: a **statutory basis** for collection; a **defined and narrow purpose**; **proportionality** between the information taken and the harm addressed; and **verification plus a right of reply** before anything adverse follows for an individual. Then apply them to the facts. This converts a politically charged topic into a doctrinal answer — the safest and highest-scoring way to write it.
4. **The GS4 framing — and the reason this is the week's ethics material.** A portal inviting citizens to submit photographs and videos as "evidence" about their neighbours raises three classical ethics problems at once: the **ethics of the unverified anonymous accusation**; the **asymmetry of power** between an accuser with a form and a subject who is never told; and the **public servant's duty of confidentiality and impartiality** when acting on material of unknown provenance. Note the distinction a good GS4 answer makes: the ethical defect is not in *collecting* information, it is in **acting on information the subject cannot see, contest or correct**.
5. **The Sachar symmetry is a free marker of balance.** One clause — *a comparable exercise two decades ago was attacked from the opposite direction* — shows the examiner you are reasoning about the principle rather than the party. Keep it to one clause, and let the four tests in point 3 do the work.

## 3. Carbon pricing: join the rule-makers, or trade inside their rules?

*A deliberate pair, read together:*
*(a) The Hindu, unsigned editorial, ~19 October 2026 — "A case for joining the global carbon coalition"*
*(b) The Indian Express, unsigned editorial, ~16 October 2026 — "Turning green trade rules into India's green industrial advantage"*

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Environment & Climate Change** (primary: conservation, environmental pollution, climate change and international agreements; carbon markets); **GS3 — Indian Economy** (industrial policy, infrastructure, energy, investment models, effects of liberalisation, exports); **GS2 — International Relations** (institutions of global governance, effect of policies of developed countries on India's interests, CBDR-RC).

*Continuity note: the 10 September pack flagged the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme as a theme to "keep on the radar… likely to be a lead theme once the first compliance cycle data appears." That cycle has now closed, and two papers wrote on it in the same week. This is the follow-through.*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The two pieces answer the same question from opposite ends. **The Hindu** argues the risk of staying outside the new **Open Coalition on Compliance Carbon Markets (OCCCM)** exceeds the risk of joining it: the rules of interoperability between national carbon markets are being written now, and India — with a live compliance market of its own — should be in the room rather than a rule-taker later. **The Indian Express** argues the more immediate opportunity is domestic: instead of treating the EU's **CBAM** as a trade barrier to resist, treat the carbon price it creates as a **demand signal that finances Indian green industry** in ammonia, sustainable aviation fuel, green steel, aluminium and electrical equipment. Together: *contest the rules abroad, and monetise them at home.*

### Key data & facts

**India's own carbon market**
- The **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)** is operational. Nearly **500 companies across seven industrial sectors** filed their **first compliance reports on 31 July**; the first credits were expected in **October**, with trading on power exchanges anticipated between **November and January**. **[verify the credit-issuance and trading dates — these were forward-looking at the time of writing.]**
- The CCTS underpins India's **Nationally Determined Contributions** under the Paris Agreement and the **net-zero-by-2070** target. More than **50 countries** now have some carbon-pricing measure.

**The coalition**
- The **OCCCM** was proposed by **Brazil at COP30 in Belém (2025)** and formally constituted on **7 May 2026 in Florence** by the **EU, Brazil and China**, which signed its Terms of Reference. **Brazil holds the presidency** for an initial two-year term, with **China and the EU as co-chairs**.
- **Seven members** beyond the founders — **Canada, France, Germany, New Zealand, Norway, Turkey and the United Kingdom** — and **twelve observers**, including ICAP, IETA, UNDP, UNEP, WRI, the Green Climate Fund and the Climate Club.
- Its **first work plan was adopted at the Carbon Market Conference on 15 September 2026 in Wuhan**. Focus: **MRV** (monitoring, reporting and verification), common carbon-accounting standards, market integrity and interoperability — described as "a collaborative framework without relying on unilateral trade sanctions."
- **India has not joined.** The coalition does not impose emissions targets and does not explicitly address CBAM.

**The industrial opportunity**
- In major traded **electrical-equipment** categories worth roughly **$500 billion**, **China holds about 30%** of exports and **India under 2%**.
- India supplies nearly **20% of the UK's non-European transformer imports**, but has a negligible share of the wider European market.
- The **EU's ReFuelEU Aviation** framework began with a **2% SAF blending requirement in 2025**, rising over time — a mandated, price-insensitive demand curve for sustainable aviation fuel.
- A carbon price of roughly **$30–50 per tonne** is suggested as the level that would actually shift investment in steel, hydrogen and fertiliser. The piece recommends **sector-level emissions targets rather than plant-specific ones**, plus digital compliance and MRV infrastructure so Indian exporters can evidence their carbon content.
- **CBAM** addresses **carbon leakage** — production shifting to jurisdictions with weaker climate rules.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Rule-taking is a real risk in both directions.** International coordination on MRV and accounting standards could entrench methodologies suited to capital-rich economies with mature measurement infrastructure, and effectively raise the cost of compliance for Indian firms. The Hindu's own caveat is that India should assess the coalition's governance and safeguards *before* joining, and protect policy autonomy and **CBDR-RC** / climate-equity principles.
- **The equity objection to CBAM has not gone away** because the Indian Express has found a way to profit from it. A unilateral border measure that charges developing-country exporters for emissions embedded in production, while the revenue accrues to the importing bloc, is in tension with differentiated responsibility however cleverly it is arbitraged. India's formal position in trade fora remains that CBAM is discriminatory — and an answer that forgets this will read as naive.
- **Green production is more capital-intensive**, so the transition cost arrives before the export premium. Without concessional climate finance and technology transfer, a price signal alone finances nothing: it just raises costs for firms that cannot borrow.
- **Protectionism can hide inside a standard.** Compliance cost, documentation and verification requirements are themselves a barrier, and they fall hardest on smaller exporters — the same rules-of-origin and compliance problem the India-UK CETA piece raises this week (see quick reads). A standard that only large firms can evidence is a market-access restriction with a climate justification.
- **Note the tension between the two pieces, because it is the best thing in this pair.** The Hindu wants India inside a coalition whose explicit purpose is to *avoid* unilateral trade measures; the Indian Express wants India to build an industrial strategy that *depends on* those unilateral measures persisting. Both cannot be fully right about the direction of travel.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Rule-taker or rule-maker" is the frame, and it generalises.** The same argument structure applies to AI governance, digital trade, critical-minerals supply chains and pharmaceutical standards: where standards are being written, non-participation is not neutrality — it is delegation. One sentence of this frame, then the specifics, is a strong GS3 or GS2 opening.
2. **Distinguish a carbon price as a cost from a carbon price as a signal.** The exam-grade formulation: *a border carbon measure is a tax on India's emissions intensity and simultaneously a subsidy to whoever lowers it first.* Use it to show you can argue both the equity objection and the industrial opportunity without contradiction — which is precisely the skill a 15-marker on CBAM is testing.
3. **MRV is the unglamorous answer, so say it.** Interoperability, mutual recognition and border measures all reduce to whether India can *measure and certify* embedded carbon at firm level. Digital MRV infrastructure is therefore trade policy, not environmental housekeeping — and the "sector-level rather than plant-level targets" recommendation is a concrete, citable design choice that lets small plants comply without plant-specific baselines.
4. **Three numbers worth memorising as a set:** **under 2%** of a **$500 billion** electrical-equipment market against China's **30%**; **$30–50/tonne** as the price at which heavy industry actually reinvests; **~500 firms across 7 sectors** in the CCTS's first compliance cycle. One line of ambition gap, one of policy threshold, one of domestic readiness.
5. **Hold the honest caveats.** India is outside a coalition founded by the **EU, Brazil and China** — a grouping that cuts across the usual developed/developing axis and therefore cannot be dismissed as a rich-country club; equally, a coalition whose co-chairs include the authors of CBAM is not a neutral forum. Both facts belong in the same answer.

## 4. A contested UNSC seat — when the cost of winning is the constituency you win it with

*The Hindu, unsigned editorial, ~16 October 2026 — "A contested UNSC seat poses a test for India"*

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — International Relations** (primary: important international institutions, agencies and fora — their structure and mandate; bilateral, regional and global groupings involving India; India's neighbourhood and extended neighbourhood); **GS4 — Ethics** (ethics in international relations; the relation between national interest and stated principle) as a secondary but genuine hook.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India's bid for a ninth non-permanent Security Council term (2028–29) is contested by **Tajikistan**, which has never served. India can contest, persuade Tajikistan to withdraw, withdraw itself, or split the term — and each option trades an immediate electoral result against India's standing as a spokesman for the Global South and for equitable representation. The editorial's point is that a country whose entire UNSC-reform case rests on *under-represented states deserving a seat* pays a price for defeating an under-represented state to take one.

### Key data & facts

- The election falls in **June 2027**, for the **2028–29** term. Victory requires a **two-thirds majority of the UN General Assembly — at least 129 of 193 votes**.
- India has served on the Council **eight times**, most recently in **2021–22**. It polled **187 votes in 2010** and **184 in 2020** — among the strongest results on record, which is exactly what makes a contested race awkward.
- **Tajikistan has never served on the Council**, and can campaign on precisely that: that membership should rotate to states that have never held it.
- **Withdrawal precedents exist**: Kazakhstan stood down in **2010** and Afghanistan in **2013**.
- **Contested races are not safe**, even for large states: Kyrgyzstan defeated the Philippines, and **Portugal and Austria defeated Germany** in the Western European and Others Group — the standard cautionary example of a major power losing a UNGA ballot.
- **Meetings between Indian and Tajik leaders in September 2026** did not produce a withdrawal.
- India maintains facilities at **Ayni** and **Farkhor** in Tajikistan — so the bilateral relationship carries a strategic cost of a bruising contest, over and above the multilateral one.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **India's claim is not merely a claim of turn-taking.** A country of 1.4 billion people, a top-five economy and a leading troop contributor to UN peacekeeping has a representational case that does not reduce to how recently it last served. On any population- or contribution-weighted view, India is the *under*-represented party.
- **Withdrawal has its own reputational cost**, and may be read in two unflattering ways at once: as a diplomatic defeat, or as India conceding that its Global South leadership is rhetorical enough to be traded for a quiet year.
- **Asian Group practice is consensus endorsement**, not contest; the real failure here is that the group did not settle a single candidature, and the editorial's four options are all downstream of that. A fifth option — building an Asian Group mechanism for orderly rotation — is more durable than any of them.
- **A split or shared term is neater on paper than in practice.** A one-year stint buys little: Council influence comes from continuity on files, chairing subsidiary bodies and sitting through a full cycle of mandate renewals.
- **Separate the UNSC-reform argument from the non-permanent-seat argument.** India's permanent-seat case rests on the Council's structural unrepresentativeness; a non-permanent seat is an ordinary competitive election inside the existing structure. Conflating them is an error the editorial avoids and most answers do not.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Credibility is an asset that can be spent, and winning can spend it."** The sharpest line of the week for GS2 IR, and a natural GS4 bridge: India's influence in the UNGA rests substantially on being trusted by small states, so a contest won against a small state can cost more than the seat is worth. Equally usable in reverse — conceding on demand teaches others that India yields to pressure.
2. **Use this against the "voting strength equals influence" assumption.** 187 and 184 votes were earned by goodwill, not extracted by weight. The examinable insight: *in a one-state-one-vote forum, India's power is reputational, which makes it depletable.*
3. **The institutional fix outscores the tactical one.** Any of the four options is a one-off; an **Asian Group rotation and endorsement mechanism** is a reform. Answers that end with a process recommendation rather than a preferred outcome read as more mature — and the structural diagnosis (no agreed group mechanism) is the actual cause.
4. **A clean GS4 pairing.** This is a real-world instance of **means–ends** reasoning in statecraft, and it pairs naturally with the Gandhian means-ethics material in the **5 October** pack: if the end is legitimate leadership of the Global South, can it be reached by a means that overrides a Global South claimant? Note the honest complication for balance: a state is also a trustee of its own citizens' interests, so "sacrifice the national interest for moral consistency" is not automatically the ethical answer either.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS4 — Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude**)

> *Paper rotation check: the last four packs carried **GS4 (3 September)**, **GS3 internal security (17 September)**, **GS1 society (5 October)** and **GS3 economy (15 October)**. GS4 is the longest-overdue paper and this week's material supports it properly — piece 2 is a live ethics case, not a retrofit. Directive rotation: recent weeks used* examine, critically examine, assess, evaluate, discuss, elucidate, critically analyse *and* analyse *— so this week uses* **comment**.

**Q.** Recent official exercises in demographic and electoral verification have sought information in categories the state does not ordinarily maintain, and have invited unverified submissions from members of the public about other individuals. In this context, comment on the ethical obligations that bind a public servant entrusted with collecting and acting upon personal information about citizens. **(10 marks, 150 words)**

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Open by locating the duty, not the controversy.** A public servant handling citizens' data holds it in **trust**, not in ownership — the citizen usually cannot refuse to supply it and cannot see what is done with it. That asymmetry of power and information is what generates the ethical obligation, and it exists independently of whether any particular exercise is well intentioned. *(One or two sentences. Do not name parties or take a political side anywhere in this answer.)*
2. **State the obligations as a short, named set.** **Purpose limitation** (data acquired for one purpose is not repurposed for another); **proportionality** (no more information than the stated harm requires); **confidentiality** (information about an identifiable person is not disclosed or circulated beyond the purpose); **impartiality and non-discrimination** (categories used must not themselves prejudge who is suspect); and **accountability** (a record of what was collected, from whom, and on what authority). Name them — an examiner can see a framework faster than an argument.
3. **Address the hard case directly: the unverified third-party submission.** The ethical defect is not that a citizen may complain — it is that an adverse consequence may follow from material the subject cannot see, contest or correct. Hence the public servant's minimum duties here: **verify before acting**, **give notice and a hearing** before any adverse action, **never treat volume of complaints as evidence**, and **refuse to act on anonymous allegations** against identifiable individuals. Note the institutional corollary: a collection mechanism without a verification standard shifts the burden of proof onto the citizen, which is the inversion that makes it unethical.
4. **Give the categorisation problem its own point, because it is the subtlest one.** Where records are not maintained in the category demanded, the data must be **constructed by inference or proxy** — and inference carries error that falls unevenly across groups, so the classification can manufacture the very pattern it claims to discover. The ethical duty on the official is **candour about method and uncertainty**: reporting what the data cannot show is as much a part of probity as reporting what it does. Add one line of **means–ends** reasoning: a legitimate end (enforcing immigration law, maintaining an accurate roll) does not sanitise a means that treats a class of citizens as presumptively suspect.
5. **Close on the resolution available to an official who objects.** Distinguish **lawful dissent from disobedience**: record reasons in writing, seek instructions in writing, propose a narrower and proportionate alternative, insist on verification and data-protection safeguards, escalate through hierarchy — and treat resignation as a last resort, not a first gesture. One closing line tying it back: the test of integrity in data handling is whether the citizen would accept the process as fair **if they could see all of it** — which is simply the publicity test applied to administration.

*Common traps in this question: (a) drifting into a GS2 answer about Articles 14/15/21 and privacy law — cite them in a clause at most, because the paper is asking about the official's obligations, not the Constitution's; (b) naming political parties or the committee's members, which gains nothing and risks everything; (c) listing ethics vocabulary without applying it to the two specific facts given (categories the state does not maintain; unverified public submissions). Apply every principle you name to one of those two facts.*
