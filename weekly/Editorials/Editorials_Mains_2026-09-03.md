# Editorials -> Mains — week of 3 September 2026

*Coverage window: 28 August – 3 September 2026 · The Hindu and The Indian Express*

**This week's thread:** four pieces, four different ways the Indian state knows a problem is coming and still fails to act on it in time — a Himalayan disaster foretold, an ethanol target that outran its feedstock, a water crisis answered with mega-projects instead of demand reform, and a mental-health burden with a four-tier delivery system that most sufferers never touch. Deliberately steered away from recent packs (caste census, Green India Mission, judiciary/tribunals, labour law, space policy, youth unemployment).

## 1. Nepal's catastrophe and India's Himalayan unpreparedness

**Source:** The Hindu · Editorial page op-ed · Gopalkrishna Gandhi (former diplomat and Governor) · 31 August 2026
**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Disaster Management** (primary); GS1 — Geography (Himalayan geomorphology); GS2 — IR (India–China transboundary rivers)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The 26 August mass-movement disaster in Nepal is not a neighbour's tragedy but a warning India is structurally unequipped to receive. Gandhi's charge is *epistemic*: Indian earth scientists already possess the relevant knowledge of Himalayan hazard, but it sits in what he calls an **"acharya-mushti"** — the teacher's closed fist — legible to specialists and conferences, unavailable to the communities actually standing downstream. The Himalaya, he argues, are not six national silos but "one living rock," seismically and glaciologically indifferent to political boundaries.

### Key data & facts

- **The event (26 Aug 2026):** At ~08:37 a mountainside in the **Langtang Himal** failed. Within about seven minutes a wall of rock, ice and liquefied sediment had travelled ~22 km across the Nepal–China border at close to **190 km/h**. Early tolls ran to hundreds dead and hundreds-to-thousands missing; figures were still moving as of early September.
- **Important technical correction (verify this before you write it):** despite widespread "GLOF" labelling, glaciologists analysing the event concluded it began as a **rock-and-ice avalanche**, not a classical moraine-dammed glacial lake outburst. A *secondary* lake then ponded behind the debris and burst days later, producing a second flood wave. Use the term **"cascading multi-hazard"** — it is more accurate and it reads better than a mislabelled GLOF.
- **Historical anchors:** Nepal–Bihar earthquake **1934** (M 8.0, ~10,000 dead in Bihar); Assam earthquake **1950** (M 8.6 — the Brahmaputra was temporarily landslide-dammed and then released catastrophically). Precedent for seismically triggered water disasters in the northeast.
- **Medog / Motuo:** China's project at the great bend of the Yarlung Tsangpo, ~**60 GW** across a five-dam cascade, up to ~300 billion kWh a year; groundbreaking 19 July 2025; sits near the active **Paizhen fault**. The river enters India as the Siang. Arunachal CM Pema Khandu has publicly called it an "existential threat" to downstream tribes.
- **Static hooks:** BIS seismic zones II–V (Himalayan belt and NE India almost entirely Zone V); **Dam Safety Act, 2021** (NDSA, NCDS, mandatory Emergency Action Plans); **DM Act, 2005** (NDMA/SDMA/NDRF, 16 battalions).

### The other side / counter-argument

Gandhi's prescriptions — "calibrated but time-bound decongestion" of Himalayan settlements, and a "civilian surgical strike" on dangerous built infrastructure — are the weakest part of the piece. Depopulating hill agglomerations is a livelihoods and federalism nightmare: these are State-subject settlements with tourism-dependent economies, and forced relocation without alternative employment produces its own humanitarian cost. Second, a hazard-communication push has diminishing returns without last-mile capability — Nepal's own early-warning network could not have outrun a 190 km/h debris flow that covered 22 km in seven minutes. The binding constraint may be **physics and response time**, not public awareness. Third, on Medog: India has no treaty leverage. There is no Brahmaputra water-sharing treaty, only expired-and-renewed hydrological data-sharing MoUs, so "raise it frankly with China" is an aspiration, not an instrument.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Frame disasters as "cascading multi-hazards", not single events.** Langtang: slope failure → debris flow → landslide-dammed lake → delayed outburst. India's DM architecture is organised hazard-by-hazard (flood, earthquake, landslide) and struggles with chains that cross those categories.
2. **The knowledge-to-community gap is the actionable reform.** Sendai Framework Priority 1 is "understanding disaster risk" — India's gap is not data generation (GSI, NRSC, NDMA atlases exist) but translation into locally usable, vernacular, community-owned preparedness. Cite the "acharya-mushti" image; it is memorable and examiner-friendly.
3. **Transboundary hydrology is now a national-security question, not an environment question.** Medog's location — a 60 GW cascade near an active fault, upstream of Indian territory — converts a climate risk into a strategic one. Argue for institutionalised data-sharing and a joint early-warning protocol as India's realistic ask.

## 2. Ethanol at 20% — when energy self-reliance collides with the food plate

**Source:** The Indian Express · *From Plate to Plough* · Ashok Gulati & Tanmoy Adhikari (ICRIER) · 31 August 2026
**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Economy / Agriculture** (food security, inflation, energy policy); GS2 — Governance (regulatory design)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

A one-month sugar price spike has exposed the structural fault in India's ethanol programme: with blending targets raised faster than feedstock supply could grow, energy policy now bids directly against the food market — and because the state controls every link of the sugar–ethanol chain (cane price, sales quotas, imports, exports, feedstock allocation), the resulting spike is a **policy failure, not a market failure**. The authors' rule: food must remain the higher-order priority.

### Key data & facts

- **The spike:** the all-India *modal* retail price of sugar moved from roughly **₹45/kg (24 July) to ~₹65/kg (24 August)** — the authors' 44% figure. Note that the all-India *average* retail series shows a gentler move (~₹48/kg on 20 July to ~₹56/kg on 20 August); prices were still reported as the highest on record. **Use the modal figure but flag which series you are quoting** — examiners reward that precision, and the two numbers are often confused in coaching material.
- **Opening stocks:** the 2025–26 sugar year (Oct–Sep) opened with only **5 MT** carry-over against **8 MT** the previous year, versus a strategic norm of ~7.5–8 MT.
- **Production:** 2025–26 estimate cut from **34.3 MT to 30.6 MT** (red rot and top borer damage); only 27.35 MT produced by June against a July–September historical average of ~0.38 MT — the authors expect a further revision to 28–29 MT.
- **Ethanol diversion:** ~**2.75 MT** of sugar equivalent diverted; blending went **1.53% (2013–14) → ~5% (2019–20) → 20% (2025–26)**.
- **Government response:** duty-free import of **1 MT of raw sugar**, plus stock limits. The authors call this "too little, too late" and want **3–4 MT of refined sugar** imported with the refined-sugar duty cut from **100% to 0–5%**.
- **The trade-off doesn't disappear, it relocates:** FCI rice (genuine surplus exists, but FCI must charge at least MSP rather than sell below cost); **maize** — the least-bad option, but Indian yields are ~**3.5 t/ha vs ~11 t/ha in the US**, and maize is **60–65% of poultry feed by weight**, so maize inflation transmits straight into eggs, broiler and milk; imported ethanol as the safety valve.
- **Static hooks:** FRP under the Sugarcane Control Order, 1966 vs State Advised Price; Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (stock limits, release orders); GEAC and the fact that only **Bt cotton** is commercially approved — the authors pointedly raise whether India will permit **GM maize**.

### The other side / counter-argument

This is contested, and the counter is well-sourced. **The government, ISMA and AIDA all argue ethanol diversion is not the primary driver** of the 2026 spike: sugar diverted to ethanol actually *declined* year-on-year, and the majority of India's ethanol now comes from **grain, not cane**. On this reading the spike is explained by excess rainfall at harvest, red rot and top borer, tighter global supply, festive-season restocking, and speculative hoarding — with ethanol a convenient scapegoat for a weather-and-disease shortfall. A second counter: the EBP has real, quantifiable wins — reduced crude import bill, lower vehicular CO and particulate emissions, and faster cane-arrear clearance for farmers, since ethanol gives mills a cash-flow stream independent of sugar sales. Dismantling that to fix a one-season shortfall is a poor trade. A third: on GM maize, the authors treat approval as a productivity question, but the objections (seed-market concentration, refuge management, contamination of non-GM supply chains) are institutional, and India's regulatory capacity is precisely what the Bt brinjal moratorium was about.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Policy failure, not market failure" is the sentence to carry.** Where the state fixes the input price (FRP/SAP), the output quota, the trade regime *and* the feedstock allocation, it has also assumed ownership of the price outcome. Good line for any question on administered prices.
2. **Targets without feedstock planning is a recurring Indian pattern.** 20% blending was achieved ahead of the 2030 target — but capacity planning ran ahead of agronomy. Generalise this: EV targets without battery minerals, green hydrogen targets without cheap renewable power. It is a strong cross-cutting example.
3. **Every biofuel feedstock has a shadow price in the food basket** — cane in sugar, rice in the buffer, maize in eggs and chicken. Argue for a published **net-energy-balance** assessment per feedstock plus OMC flexibility to switch sources, rather than fixed physical allocations.

## 3. River-linking is not the answer — demand management must come first

**Source:** The Hindu · Editorial / analysis · T. Ramakrishnan · 3 September 2026
**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Federalism** (inter-State river water disputes); **GS3 — Environment** (water resources, conservation)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The claim that interlinking rivers can end India's water stress for a century is both technically contested and politically naïve. The editorial's proof is institutional rather than hydrological: if India cannot resolve the comparatively simple **Pennaiyar** dispute — six years, eleven negotiation meetings and a Supreme Court order later, the tribunal still isn't constituted — it has no business promising Brahmaputra-to-Cauvery transfers.

### Key data & facts

- **Trigger:** the Union Home Minister, at the Southern Zonal Council in Mamallapuram, called for linking rivers from the Brahmaputra to the Godavari and Cauvery.
- **NRLP scale:** 30 links (Himalayan + Peninsular components) under NWDA, ~**173 BCM** of inter-basin transfer; ₹5.5 lakh crore at 2002 prices.
- **Pennaiyar timeline:** Tamil Nadu invoked the 1892 inter-State agreement and sought a tribunal in **Nov 2019**; two negotiation committees, eleven meetings, no resolution; SC directed tribunal formation within a month in **Feb 2025**, extended by six months — still not constituted. The Centre's suggestion of merging it into the **Mahadayi Tribunal** is, the editorial argues, untenable under the **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956**.
- **Ken–Betwa Link:** ₹**44,605 crore**; submerges ~**10,000 ha** of the **Panna Tiger Reserve**; tribal protests in Chhatarpur.
- **Demand-side arithmetic:** agriculture takes ~**80%** of India's freshwater; drip/sprinkler covers only ~**12 mn ha of ~97 mn ha** irrigated area, and a shift from flood irrigation could cut agricultural demand **30–50%** — a larger gain than any link. Groundwater extraction ~**230 BCM/yr** (world's largest); **65%** of blocks in Punjab, Haryana and western UP over-exploited. Urban non-revenue water losses **30–40%**.
- **Ecology:** Kerala's objection to the Pamba–Achankovil–Vaippar link rests on the **Vembanad–Kol** Ramsar site (1,512 km², India's largest).

### The other side / counter-argument

The pro-linking case is not frivolous and you should concede it before rebutting. India's rainfall is extraordinarily concentrated in time and space — the Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna basin carries a genuinely disproportionate share of national runoff while peninsular basins face chronic deficit, and no amount of drip irrigation moves water across that geography. Second, demand management has been the stated policy for three decades (PMKSY, "per drop more crop", micro-irrigation subsidies) and has under-delivered because it requires changing millions of individual farmer decisions, whereas supply-side projects require one decision. Third, the ecological objection cuts both ways: over-extraction of groundwater is *also* an ecological catastrophe, arguably a larger one, and surface transfer that displaces tubewell pumping has a real environmental credit. Fourth, "surplus is seasonal" is an argument for better storage and operating rules within a link, not necessarily against the link.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Institutional capacity is the binding constraint, not engineering.** Use the Pennaiyar sequence as a one-line evidence chain: *2019 request → 11 meetings → 2025 SC order → still no tribunal.* It converts an abstract federalism argument into a fact.
2. **"Surplus" is a static concept applied to a stochastic system.** A basin classified surplus can be in deficit in 30–40% of years; once a link canal creates downstream dependence, lean-year allocation becomes a new dispute. River-linking may *manufacture* inter-State conflicts as fast as it resolves water scarcity.
3. **Sequence the reform:** price/meter agricultural power via DBT → expand micro-irrigation → revive tanks, johads and baoris under MGNREGS/PMKSY-WDC → and only then evaluate individual links on standalone cost–benefit. Sequencing arguments score better than either/or arguments.

## 4. Mental health as public-health infrastructure, not charity

**Source:** The Hindu · Opinion · Indu Bhushan (founding CEO, National Health Authority / Ayushman Bharat) & Anisha Padukone (CEO, The Live Love Laugh Foundation) · 3 September 2026
**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Social Justice / Health governance** (primary); GS3 — human capital and health expenditure

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

With a treatment gap of **84.5%**, mental health is not a niche welfare concern but a systemic public-health failure with measurable costs to productivity and to NCD control. The authors reframe it as a **return on human-capital investment**: because mental disorders and non-communicable diseases are mutually reinforcing, ignoring mental health actively undermines the NCD agenda India is already funding under Ayushman Bharat.

### Key data & facts

- **Burden:** National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 — ~**10.6% current** adult prevalence, **13.7% lifetime**; treatment gap **84.5%**, rising above 85% for common conditions. The widely quoted "1 in 7 Indians (~200 million)" comes from the **GBD India study (Lancet, 2017)**, not the NMHS — keep the two sources distinct.
- **Workforce:** **0.3 psychiatrists per 1,00,000** against the WHO minimum of 1; fewer than 9,000 psychiatrists for 1.4 billion people, heavily urban-concentrated.
- **Trend:** mental disorders' share of India's total disease burden roughly **doubled over three decades**; globally mental disorders account for ~14% of years lived with disability.
- **Infrastructure that exists:** **1.73 lakh+** Ayushman Bharat Arogya Mandirs with mental health in the essential service package; **DMHP** in 90%+ districts (piloted in Bellary, 1982); **Tele-MANAS** (launched Oct 2022, all States/UTs, 24×7 multilingual).
- **Comorbidity multiplier:** over **60%** of primary-care attendees have a diagnosable mental disorder but present with physical complaints; elevated depression/anxiety in diabetes (~30%) and hypertension (~25%), worsening medication adherence.
- **The economic case:** a 2025 PGIMER–NIMHANS modelling study estimated that integrating universal depression screening into primary care could yield net savings of **₹291–482 billion a year (0.21–0.32% of GDP)**.
- **Models cited:** ASHAs (~1 million strong) trained on PHQ-9 screening, validated in Madhya Pradesh; Zimbabwe's **Friendship Bench**; Gujarat's **Atmiyata**. **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** mandates insurance parity — implementation is partial, and **PM-JAY covers inpatient psychiatric care but not OPD**, which is where most contact happens.

### The other side / counter-argument

The task-shifting model the authors propose is not costless. ASHAs are already carrying maternal health, immunisation, NCD screening, TB case-finding and survey duties on honoraria-based, non-salaried terms; adding mental-health screening without pay revision and supervision risks producing box-ticking rather than care, and there is a real clinical hazard in lay screening without a functioning referral chain — you identify cases you cannot then treat, which is arguably worse than not screening. Second, the treatment gap figure carries a definitional problem: it is anchored to a **biomedical, facility-based** notion of "treatment", and a share of distress in India is addressed through family, community and faith networks, so 84.5% overstates the population that is both untreated *and* seeking treatment. Third, the ₹291–482 billion savings estimate is a *model* output on assumed adherence and screening yields, not observed savings — cite it with attribution and hedging, never as a fact. Fourth, extending PM-JAY to OPD raises the standard moral-hazard and fiscal-sustainability question that has kept OPD out of the scheme across all conditions, not just psychiatry.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Mental health is an NCD-control input, not a parallel programme.** The bidirectional causation (depression worsens diabetes adherence; chronic disease triggers depression) means siloed vertical programmes structurally under-perform. This is a strong "integration of health services" argument usable in any health-governance answer.
2. **Coverage ≠ utilisation.** India has built four tiers (ASHA → Arogya Mandir → Tele-MANAS/district hospital → NIMHANS/IHBAS) and still has an 84.5% gap. Use this as a general template for Indian welfare delivery: infrastructure creation is the easy half; demand generation, stigma reduction and continuity of care are the hard half.
3. **The financing gap is at the OPD, not the ward.** Mental illness is chronic and outpatient by nature; an insurance architecture built around hospitalisation is structurally mismatched to it. Concrete, specific, and it names a fixable policy design flaw.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS4 — Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude**)

> *Recent weeks have used GS2 (federalism), GS3 (food security), GS1 (society) and GS2 (judiciary). Rotating to GS4 — last used in the pack of 30 July.*

**Q. "Expert knowledge that never reaches the people it could protect is not neutral — its retention is itself an ethical choice."**

**In the light of this statement, examine the ethical obligations of scientists, technical experts and civil servants in communicating known risks to vulnerable communities. What dilemmas arise when such communication may cause panic, economic loss or diplomatic friction? (250 words, 15 marks)**

*Directive words: **examine** (the obligations) + the second part demands you surface and resolve **dilemmas** — so this needs a stated resolution, not just a listing.*

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Establish the ethical foundation.** Knowledge asymmetry creates a duty. Ground it in three sources: (a) the **fiduciary duty** of a public servant — the citizen cannot self-assess seismic risk and must rely on the state; (b) **informed consent** — a community cannot exercise autonomy about where it lives if the risk is withheld; (c) the **precautionary principle**, already part of Indian environmental jurisprudence (*Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum*, 1996). Open with Gandhi's *acharya-mushti* image and name it as an ethical, not merely administrative, failing.

2. **Name the obligation precisely — and distinguish it from data-dumping.** The duty is not to publish, it is to make risk **actionable**: vernacular, community-owned, tied to a rehearsed response. Publishing a hazard atlas that no gram panchayat can read discharges a bureaucratic duty while failing the ethical one. Distinguish **transparency** (availability) from **communication** (comprehension) — this distinction is the answer's spine.

3. **Set out the dilemmas honestly (four, briefly).** (i) *Truth vs panic* — probabilistic warnings can trigger disproportionate flight, land-value collapse, or alarm fatigue that degrades the next warning; (ii) *Utility vs candour* — a State government fearing tourism collapse may suppress a landslide-susceptibility finding; (iii) *National interest vs disclosure* — publicising a downstream dam vulnerability may be read as strategic signalling, as with Medog; (iv) *Scientific integrity vs institutional loyalty* — the expert who dissents publicly risks career and access, which is where whistleblower ethics enters.

4. **Offer a resolution framework, don't just balance.** Argue that these dilemmas are resolved by **procedure**, not by heroic individual judgment: pre-committed disclosure protocols (what gets released, at what confidence threshold, through which channel), so the choice is made *before* an interest is at stake; graded, probabilistic communication with stated uncertainty rather than binary alarm; a statutory internal-dissent channel so the expert is not forced to choose between silence and going public; and community co-production, which converts recipients into participants and lowers the panic risk. Note that **panic risk is empirically overstated** — the disaster-sociology literature finds communities respond more rationally to honest risk information than officials expect, and this evidence-based point will lift the answer.

5. **Conclude with the asymmetry of consequences.** Weigh the cost of over-warning (economic and reputational, recoverable) against under-warning (irreversible loss of life). Where consequences are asymmetric and irreversible, the ethical default must be disclosure with honest uncertainty. Close by connecting individual professional ethics to institutional design — Sendai Priority 1, the Dam Safety Act's Emergency Action Plan mandate, and Section 30 duties of District Disaster Management Authorities all *presume* a communicating expert. The ethics and the statute point the same way.
