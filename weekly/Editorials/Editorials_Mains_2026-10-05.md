## 1. "Two or three?" — the three-language formula, back in court

**Source:** The Hindu, **Editorial, 5 October 2026**, titled *"Two or three?"*, written against the Supreme Court's order of **28 September 2026** on CBSE's three-language scheme.

**GS mapping:** **GS1 — Indian Society** (primary: diversity of India, language and regional identity, factors in national integration); **GS2 — Governance** (education policy, statutory and regulatory bodies, judicial review of executive policy, centre–State relations in a Concurrent List subject)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The editorial's position, as reported, is that the Supreme Court's **repeated** interventions are not really about language at all — they are a verdict on CBSE's **planning**. A policy pushed into classrooms without textbooks, trained teachers or an assessment design behind it generates exactly the "rough edges" the Court keeps having to sand down. The editorial's implied standard: a three-language mandate can be defended on integration grounds, but not while the implementation machinery is missing.

### Key data & facts

- **The order (28 September 2026):** a bench led by **CJI Surya Kant**, with **Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana**, directed CBSE to extend to **Class 6** the relaxation in evaluation already available to Class 7 under the three-language formula. Justice Bagchi floated a **"soft launch"** for Class 6 from **1 January 2027** instead of the originally mandated date.
- **The CBSE scheme (2026–27 session):** three languages labelled **R1, R2, R3**; **at least two must be Indian languages**; a foreign language may occupy **R3 only** when R1 and R2 are both Indian. During transition, teachers with **"functional proficiency"** are permitted, and **R3 is assessed by internal school evaluation, with no Class X board examination**.
- **Petitioners' case:** violation of **Articles 14, 19, 21 and 21A** — mandating a subject that lacks textbooks, trained teachers and an assessment framework amounts to substandard education, incompatible with the right to free and compulsory education of acceptable quality.
- **Policy lineage:** the **Three-Language Formula of 1968** (National Policy on Education), carried forward by **NEP 2020**, which requires at least two of the three languages to be **native to India** and states that **no language will be imposed** on any State.
- **The standing counter-example:** **Tamil Nadu's two-language policy** (Tamil + English), in place since the 1960s and never brought into line with the formula.
- **Constitutional scheme to hold:** Education is in the **Concurrent List** (Seventh Schedule, Entry 25, moved there by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**). **Article 29(1)** protects a section's right to conserve its language; **Article 350A** directs instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage; **Article 343** makes Hindi in Devanagari the official language of the Union, and the **Official Languages Act, 1963** continued English indefinitely alongside it. The **Eighth Schedule** currently lists **22** languages.

### The other side / counter-argument

The case for the mandate is not weak and must appear in any answer. **One**: multilingualism has demonstrated cognitive and labour-market returns, and the children who lose most from a two-language ceiling are those whose families cannot buy a third language privately — so the mandate is redistributive, not merely symbolic. **Two**: NEP 2020 already concedes the federal objection by requiring no imposition and allowing States to choose the three; the quarrel is with *CBSE's* operational design, not with the formula. **Three**: on the judicial-review point, the Union's position is that curriculum design is executive and expert territory, and that a court calibrating the start date of a syllabus is institutionally the wrong forum — the remedy for bad planning is better planning, not judicial supervision. **Four**: Tamil Nadu's own experience cuts both ways — the two-language policy coexists with high human-development outcomes, which weakens the claim that a third language is developmentally essential.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **Separate the three questions the controversy fuses.** *Is a third language desirable?* (pedagogy) · *May the Union mandate it?* (federalism, Concurrent List, NEP's own no-imposition clause) · *Can it be mandated without the means to teach it?* (administrative capacity, Article 21A quality standard). Most answers collapse all three into a Hindi-imposition argument and lose marks. Naming the three and answering each separately is the structural move that lifts the answer.
2. **"Capacity is part of the right."** Article 21A guarantees education of a quality that makes the right meaningful — so a scheme that permits "functional proficiency" teachers and no board assessment for R3 is vulnerable on *implementation* grounds even if it survives on *policy* grounds. This converts a vague society answer into a rights-based one.
3. **Language as a federal safety valve, not a federal problem.** India's record — the 1963 Act's indefinite continuation of English, Article 29(1), Article 350A, the Eighth Schedule's expansion from 14 to 22 — shows integration has been bought through **accommodation**, not uniformity. Best single line for a GS1 integration answer: *India has integrated by conceding linguistic claims, not by overruling them.*
4. **The sequencing failure is the generalisable lesson.** Mandate first, capacity later is the same failure pattern as any under-resourced entitlement. Usable as a GS2 implementation example well beyond education.

## 2. Gandhi beyond 1947 — the Mahatma as institution-builder, not only as protester

**Sources:** The Hindu, **op-ed, 2 October 2026**, on Gandhi's post-1947 global afterlife (reported under the heading *"Gandhi beyond 1947 / the Mahatma's unfinished work on the world stage"*); and The Indian Express, **opinion, ~2 October 2026**, *"Gandhi as co-traveller"*, on reading Gandhian thought for democratic institution-building. The two pieces run on different axes and are best used together.

**GS mapping:** **GS1 — Modern Indian History** (primary: the freedom struggle, its leaders, their contributions and the global diffusion of Indian political thought); **GS4 — Ethics** (moral thinkers, means and ends, probity); **GS2 — Polity** (Directive Principles, decentralisation)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The Hindu piece argues Gandhi's significance did not end at 1947 — satyagraha became an **exportable political technology**, taken up directly by the American civil-rights movement and later by movements with no Indian connection at all; its implied counterfactual is what Gandhi might have done internationally had he lived past January 1948. The Express piece argues the opposite emphasis: Gandhi is over-read as a protester and under-read as an **institutional thinker**, and his ethic of **conditional loyalty** — an institution earns allegiance only so long as it serves the public good — speaks directly to inequality, media capture and political centralisation today.

### Key data & facts

- **Diffusion:** the **Dharasana Satyagraha (1930)** drew international press coverage that established Gandhi as a global symbol of non-violent resistance. **Martin Luther King Jr., Bayard Rustin and James Lawson** studied and applied Gandhian method directly — Rustin and Lawson as the movement's principal trainers in non-violent discipline. A **world conference on non-violence was held in 1949**, the year after Gandhi's assassination. Later non-violent mobilisations in **East Timor** and the **First Intifada** are cited as evidence of continuing transmission.
- **Gandhi's own texts:** ***Hind Swaraj* (1909)** — his critique of modern civilisation, which he never substantively revised; his self-description as a **"philosophical anarchist"**. The Express piece invokes a **1925** formulation that institutional loyalty endures only so long as the institution aids the nation's growth (reported paraphrase, not a verified quotation — do not reproduce it as a quote).
- **Constitutional traces:** **Article 40** (organisation of village panchayats), **Article 43** (cottage industries), **Article 47** (prohibition), **Article 48** (cow protection and animal husbandry) in the Directive Principles; the **73rd Amendment, 1992** gave Gram Swaraj statutory institutional form.
- **International recognition:** the **UN General Assembly resolution of 2007** designated **2 October** the **International Day of Non-Violence**.
- **Companion peg the same week:** **2 October is also Lal Bahadur Shastri's birth anniversary** — useful for a GS4 answer pairing Gandhian means-ethics with Shastri's personal probity and the shift to agricultural self-reliance.

### The other side / counter-argument

The honest critiques belong in the answer. **One**: Ambedkar's objection that village self-government romanticises a unit that was, in his words to the Constituent Assembly, a site of caste domination — which is why the Constitution put panchayats in the non-enforceable Directive Principles rather than in Part III. **Two**: Nehru's and the planners' view that *Hind Swaraj*'s rejection of industrial modernity was unusable for a poor state needing heavy industry — Gandhi's economics was effectively set aside in practice even as his symbolism was adopted. **Three**: non-violence's scope condition — it has historically worked against adversaries sensitive to publicity and domestic opinion, far less so against regimes indifferent to both, which bounds the "exportable technology" claim. **Four**: on institution-building specifically, Gandhi dissolved rather than built — his instinct after 1947 was to wind up the Congress as a political body, which is thin ground for a theory of institutional design.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **The two readings are a ready answer structure.** Gandhi-as-**method** (satyagraha as transferable technique) versus Gandhi-as-**ethic** (conditional loyalty, purity of means). Most GS1 answers only do the first; opening with the distinction and devoting a paragraph to each is the differentiator.
2. **"Conditional loyalty" is the single most portable idea here** — it reframes institutional accountability as an *ethical* obligation on the office-holder rather than only a legal one. Directly usable in GS4 on probity and institutional integrity, and in GS2 on why institutions decay without being formally dismantled.
3. **Means–ends inseparability** is the cleanest GS4 framework from the week: for Gandhi the means were constitutive of the end, not instrumental to it — so an unethical means does not produce a compromised good outcome, it produces a different outcome. Pair with the satyagrahi's duty of self-introspection (checking one's own complicity before accusing another).
4. **For GS1, lead with transmission, not hagiography.** The examinable content is *how* an idea travelled — press coverage of Dharasana, trained organisers like Rustin and Lawson carrying technique into Montgomery and Nashville, the 1949 conference, the 2007 UN designation. A chain of transmission scores; a tribute does not.

## 3. Rising seas, fixed baselines — the UN declaration and India's eroding coast

**Source:** The Indian Express, **Explained / expert column (late September – 1 October 2026)**, on the UN General Assembly's **first declaration on sea-level rise**, adopted **24 September 2026**.

**GS mapping:** **GS1 — Geography** (primary: coastal processes and landforms, deltas and island systems, geophysical phenomena); **GS3 — Environment & Disaster Management** (climate change, conservation, coastal regulation, adaptation finance); **GS2 — International Relations / International Law** (UNCLOS, statehood, CBDR-RC)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The column's argument is that the declaration solves a **legal** problem while leaving the **physical** one untouched: submergence will not automatically extinguish statehood or the maritime zones measured from existing baselines, which protects island states and, for India, the vast EEZ generated by Lakshadweep and the Andaman & Nicobar islands. But India's own coastal regulation is **static while the coast is moving** — the no-development buffer is measured from **today's** High Tide Line and takes no account of the rate at which that line is retreating.

### Key data & facts

- **The declaration (24 September 2026, high-level UNGA meeting, New York):** physical disappearance of territory, or coastline change caused by sea-level rise, **does not automatically result in loss of statehood**; maritime zones established under UNCLOS may be **maintained despite coastline change**, with the right to update baselines retained. Championed by **AOSIS** / Small Island Developing States; **AOSIS Chair, Palau's President Surangel Whipps Jr.**, publicly stressed that the declaration cannot itself stop the seas rising. **It is a declaration, not a treaty — not legally binding**; effect depends on state and institutional practice. No formal opposition reported.
- **Rate of rise:** **2.1 mm/year (1993–2002)** → **4.7 mm/year (2015–2024)**; **~6 mm in 2024** alone, a record. Projected **38 cm** (lowest-emissions path) to **77 cm** (high-emissions) by **2100**, with rise continuing for centuries after net zero — the **commitment** problem.
- **India's exposure:** about **45 million** people at risk by **2050**; coastline remeasured at **11,098.81 km** and **1,298 offshore islands and islets** in the **2023–24** exercise (up sharply from the long-used 7,516 km, because of finer-resolution mapping of a crenulated coast — a measurement artefact, not erosion).
- **Erosion:** **NCCR's** long-period assessment finds about **one-third of the mainland shoreline eroding**; **234 sq km lost** against **231 sq km gained** nationally — a near-balance in aggregate that conceals severe local concentration. Roughly **400 km retreating faster than 5 m/year** and **~225 km at 3–5 m/year**; **West Bengal** has **170+ km** in the fastest-retreat category.
- **Sundarbans:** **Ghoramara** shrank from about **7.2 sq km (1972)** to **3.6 sq km (2022)**; **Lohachara** disappeared in the early 2000s.
- **Regulatory gap:** **CRZ Notification, 2019** sets a **No Development Zone of 50 m** in densely populated rural areas rising to **200 m** elsewhere, **measured from the High Tide Line**; the **hazard line** is used only for disaster planning, not to fix the buffer.
- **Adaptation finance:** UNEP's estimated need of about **$310 billion a year by 2035** against roughly **$26 billion a year** of international public adaptation finance (2023).
- **UNCLOS basics:** territorial sea **12 nm**, contiguous zone **24 nm**, EEZ **200 nm**, all measured from baselines — which is exactly why fixing baselines is the whole fight.

### The other side / counter-argument

**One**: fixing baselines permanently entrenches today's maritime map against a changed physical world, which coastal states on the losing side of a delimitation may eventually resist — a declaration cannot bind future dispute settlement. **Two**: a non-binding declaration risks substituting legal comfort for mitigation; island leaders themselves made this point. **Three**: on India's CRZ, the development lobby's case is real — shifting the buffer to a hazard-line or setback-rate basis would freeze large stretches of economically active coast, with compensation and livelihood consequences for fishing communities already squeezed by port and tourism projects. **Four**: the near-balance of 234 sq km lost against 231 sq km gained is used to argue that national-level alarm is overstated and the problem is local, warranting local works rather than a regulatory overhaul; the counter is that averages are irrelevant to a household in Ghoramara.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"A static line on a moving coast."** The sharpest formulation available this week: regulation anchored to a High Tide Line that is itself retreating at 5 m/year guarantees that the buffer shrinks every year without any decision being taken. One sentence that frames an entire GS3 answer on coastal regulation.
2. **Statehood decoupled from territory is a genuine doctrinal shift.** The classical Montevideo criteria assume defined territory; the declaration accepts continuity of statehood without it. This is the week's best GS2 international-law point and a rare chance to show doctrinal awareness rather than listing conventions.
3. **Distinguish the measurement artefact from the physical change.** The jump from 7,516 km to 11,098.81 km is finer mapping of a crenulated coast — not land lost or gained. Examiners notice candidates who can tell a data revision from a trend.
4. **Aggregate balance, local catastrophe.** 234 lost against 231 gained nationally, yet 170+ km of West Bengal in the fastest-retreat band. The best available illustration of why spatially averaged environmental statistics mislead — transferable to groundwater, air quality and forest-cover answers alike.
5. **Adaptation is the financing gap, not the knowledge gap.** $310 bn needed against $26 bn available is a ~12x shortfall; use it to argue that CBDR-RC's operative content today is finance and technology, not emission targets.

## 4. Household debt at 45.5% of GDP — borrowing against tomorrow

**Source:** The Hindu, **editorial/op-ed, 1 October 2026**, on rising household indebtedness (reported under the heading *"Household debt — financing today against tomorrow"*), written off **RBI** data.

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Indian Economy** (primary: mobilisation of resources, savings and investment, inclusive growth, financial inclusion, monetary and financial-stability policy); **GS2 — Governance** (social protection, consumer protection in digital lending)

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The piece argues that the headline debt ratio matters less than the **composition** of the borrowing. Debt taken against an asset — a house, a farm, a business — builds net worth; debt taken to smooth consumption in the absence of income security does not. Since the second kind is now the faster-growing kind, the policy answer is **income security and lending conduct**, not more credit availability.

### Key data & facts

- **Household debt at 45.5% of GDP** as of **September 2025** (RBI). *Caution: a widely recirculated claim that this was "2% of GDP in March 2021" is arithmetically implausible and could not be verified — do not use it. Use the composition shift below instead, which is verified and makes the same point better.*
- **Composition:** **non-housing retail loans = 58.4%** of household debt, up from roughly **50% in 2019–20**; **housing 26.3%**; **agriculture and business loans 15.3%**. The shift is **away from asset-backed and toward consumption credit**.
- **Household net financial savings** recovered to about **6% of GDP in 2024–25** (from ~5.2% in 2023–24) but remain **below pre-pandemic levels** — rising debt and weak net savings at the same time is the stress signal.
- **Channel:** fintech-originated lending — on the order of **13.2 crore loans** worth about **₹2.15 lakh crore** — plus **buy-now-pay-later** arrangements have collapsed the transaction cost of small-ticket borrowing. High volume, small average size, thin underwriting.

### The other side / counter-argument

**One**: 45.5% is **low by comparative standards** — well under advanced-economy levels and below several Asian peers; a country at India's per-capita income with shallow household credit penetration should expect the ratio to rise as a normal part of financial deepening. **Two**: rising non-housing retail credit partly reflects **formalisation** — borrowers moving from moneylenders and chit funds onto regulated balance sheets, which is a gain in consumer protection even where the purpose is consumption. **Three**: aggregate asset quality in retail has held up, so the distress inference runs ahead of the default data. **Four**: the opposite reading of the savings number — households shifting from financial savings into **physical assets and equity** is portfolio reallocation, not impoverishment.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Credit is not income."** Easier borrowing can look like demand recovery in the aggregate data while being consumption-smoothing by stressed households. The one-line framing for any GS3 answer on consumption-led growth.
2. **Composition is the diagnostic, not the level.** 58.4% non-housing against 26.3% housing says more than 45.5% of GDP does: asset-backed debt builds net worth, consumption debt only shifts it through time. This single comparison is the most examinable fact from the week's economy coverage.
3. **The twin-signal test for household financial stress** — debt rising *and* net financial savings below pre-pandemic levels, simultaneously. Either alone is ambiguous; together they are diagnostic. A reusable analytical tool, not just a fact.
4. **Links to pick 1 and the transmission channel.** Household balance sheets are where every welfare and employment question eventually lands — a household servicing consumption debt cuts education and nutrition spending first. Pairs directly with the intra-household transmission argument from the 27 August pack on youth unemployment.

## GS Mains practice question — rotated paper: **GS1 (Indian Society)**

> *Paper rotation check: the last four packs ran GS2 (judiciary, 27 Aug), GS4 (ethics, 3 Sep), GS2 (IR, 10 Sep) and GS3 (internal security, 17 Sep). GS1 is due — last used 20 August, and on caste, not language. Directive rotation: recent weeks used* examine, critically examine, assess, evaluate, discuss, elucidate, critically analyse, analyse *— so this week uses* **comment**.

**Q. India's language policy has been less a settled constitutional principle than a continuously renegotiated political settlement. Comment, with reference to the three-language formula and the constitutional scheme on languages. (15 marks, 250 words)**

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

**1. Open by accepting the premise, and name the mechanism (≈35 words).**
Language in India was never settled by a founding decision; it has been settled repeatedly, each time by concession. Define the claim you will defend: accommodation, not uniformity, has been the integrative device. Do not open with the 1968 formula — open with the mechanism.

**2. Establish the constitutional scheme as a set of deliberate compromises, not a principle (≈60 words).**
Article 343 (Hindi in Devanagari as the Union's official language) set against the **Official Languages Act, 1963** continuing English indefinitely — a constitutional timetable overridden by statute after the 1965 agitations. **Article 29(1)** (right to conserve language), **Article 350A** (mother-tongue instruction at the primary stage), the **Eighth Schedule** expanding from 14 to 22 languages, and education's move to the **Concurrent List** by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**. Each is a negotiated instrument, which is precisely the point.

**3. Show the formula's own history of renegotiation (≈55 words).**
The **1968** Three-Language Formula, reaffirmed by **NEP 2020** with two explicit concessions written into it — at least two of the three languages Indian, and **no language imposed on any State**. **Tamil Nadu's** two-language policy has stood for six decades without being brought into line. A formula that accommodates its own largest exception is a settlement, not a principle.

**4. Use the current controversy as evidence — and separate the three questions (≈60 words).**
CBSE's **R1/R2/R3** scheme for 2026–27, the Supreme Court's **28 September 2026** direction extending Class 7's evaluation relaxation to **Class 6** and the suggested **1 January 2027** soft launch, and the petitioners' **Articles 14, 19, 21, 21A** challenge. Then separate: *desirability* (multilingual gains, and that the poor lose most from a two-language ceiling), *authority* (Concurrent List, NEP's no-imposition clause), *capacity* ("functional proficiency" teachers, no Class X board for R3). The litigation is about the third, not the first.

**5. Close by converting "unsettled" from a weakness into the finding (≈40 words).**
Renegotiation is not the failure of India's language policy; it is the method by which a 22-language polity has held together where imposition would have fractured it. The real risk is not that the settlement is reopened periodically, but that a mandate outruns the capacity to deliver it — turning a question of accommodation into a question of rights. End on the standard, not on a slogan.

*Trap to avoid: this is a GS1 society question, not a GS2 federalism answer. Articles and cases are evidence of negotiation here, not the subject matter. An answer that becomes a centre–State powers essay will cap around 7–8/15 however accurate it is.*
