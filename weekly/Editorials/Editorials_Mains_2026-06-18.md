## 1. India, the West and the G7 — strategic autonomy meets domestic reform

**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion (17 Jun 2026) · *GS2 (IR) + GS3 (Economy)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Despite the BRICS narrative, India's economic future remains deeply tied to the developed West, and the PM's seventh straight G7 appearance reflects that. The real determinant of India's great-power status is not diplomacy but *domestic transformation* — economic restructuring, defence modernisation and a technology base — and China's rise is the template: engage the West deeply while building national capability.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- 52nd G7 Summit at Évian, France; India's **13th appearance**, the PM's **7th consecutive** since 2019; engagement began in **2003** (Vajpayee invited at Évian).
- Nearly **one-third of India's merchandise exports** go to G7 countries; G7 is the principal destination for Indian students, professionals and skilled migrants.
- US economy ~**$31 trillion GDP**, still growing ~2.5%; Eurozone now barely **half** the US size; SpaceX valued ~**$2 trillion** (rise of "tech capitalism").
- China's GDP rose ~**$1.6 tn (2003) → ~$20 tn (2025)**; India's ~**$0.6 tn → under $4 tn** in the same period.

**The other side of the debate.** Critics of a West-tilt argue that over-reliance on Western capital and technology constrains strategic autonomy and exposes India to "America First" unilateralism and weaponised interdependence (tariffs, sanctions, tech controls); BRICS, the Global South and Russia ties remain essential hedges in a multipolar order.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- India's external strategy is shifting from *balancing* West vs BRICS to *broadening* options across Western economies — "issue-based" multi-alignment.
- The China lesson: strategic autonomy and deep Western economic engagement are *compatible*; pragmatism beats ideological rigidity.
- Frame the answer around the *primacy of internal capacity* — without manufacturing competitiveness and an innovation ecosystem, diplomacy alone cannot deliver power.

## 2. The long-term implications of the U.S.–Iran deal

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (17 Jun 2026) · *GS2 (IR) + GS3 (Energy security / Internal security)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** The U.S.–Iran MoU is a real step toward de-escalation, but mutual distrust, the nuclear-enrichment question and Hormuz keep durable peace uncertain. The conflict has already reshaped West Asia: it exposed the limits of military power, revived the case for multilateralism, and accelerated a rethink of energy security and regional alignments.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- The **Strait of Hormuz** carries roughly **one-fifth of global oil and gas**; its management has become both a flashpoint and a bargaining chip. (Cross-check: IEA estimated the blockade choked **>14 million barrels/day** of West Asian output; on 18 June, post-deal, **Brent fell ~1.4% to ~$78.41** and **WTI ~1.25% to ~$75.83**.)
- MoU signed **17 June 2026**; Iran agreed to **dilute its enriched-uranium stockpile**, with the IAEA set to implement (cross-checked against reporting).
- Conflict demonstrated the effectiveness of **asymmetric warfare** against a technologically superior adversary.

**The other side of the debate.** A ceasefire may *not* mean stability: weakening of Iran's networks could open space for non-state actors (IS, al-Qaeda); Gulf rivalries (Saudi–Emirati) may block a collective Hormuz framework; and Iran may simply shift deterrence from nuclear ambiguity to **maritime leverage** over Hormuz — a new, harder-to-regulate risk.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- "Limits of military power" + "return of multilateralism" — usable as a thesis on conflict resolution and the post-unipolar order.
- Energy-security takeaway for India: supply-chain resilience, strategic petroleum reserves, source diversification and faster energy transition are *strategic*, not just economic, choices.
- A cooperative **littoral-states framework for Hormuz** is a forward-looking, solution-oriented point examiners reward.

## 3. The 8th CPC — a chance to reform pay commissions

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (13 Jun 2026) · *GS2 (Governance) + GS3 (Public finance / Fiscal policy)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Debate on the 8th Central Pay Commission has fixated on fitment factors and pay hikes, but the deeper question is whether public-sector compensation is equitable, transparent and fiscally sustainable. The CPC should be the moment to fix structural flaws — not just revise numbers — ideally via a permanent **National Compensation Authority** instead of episodic commissions.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- No **common evaluation framework** across services to assess risk, responsibility, technical expertise and career progression; decisions lean on service-specific representations rather than objective benchmarks.
- **Fragmented pensions** — defined-benefit pensions, contributory schemes (NPS/UPS) and separate provisions for elected representatives coexist, raising fairness and uniformity concerns.
- **Fiscal sustainability & inter-generational equity**: rising spend on salaries, pensions and interest payments strains budgets; many countries have moved from periodic revisions to **institutionalised, independent review mechanisms**.

**The other side of the debate.** A permanent authority risks bureaucratic capture and rigidity; periodic commissions allow course-correction to changing economic conditions. Pay restraint must also be balanced against attracting talent into government and maintaining morale.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- Link to *fiscal federalism* — state finances mirror central pay revisions, so a structural fix has Centre–State spillovers.
- Use as a "governance reform" example: shift from *ad hoc* to *institutionalised, rules-based* mechanisms (parallels: FRBM, GST Council, MPC).
- Tie compensation reform to *institutional effectiveness and public trust*, not just employee welfare.

## 4. Our parched cities need to make every drop count — the circular water economy

**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion (16 Jun 2026) · *GS1 (Urbanisation) + GS3 (Environment / Water resources)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Recurring heatwaves and rising urban demand have exposed the fragility of India's city water systems. The fix is a shift from a linear "extract–use–discard" model to a **circular water economy** — systematically treating and reusing wastewater for non-potable needs — backed by city-level plans, private finance and functional sewage treatment.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- Per **CEEW (Council on Energy, Environment and Water)**, large-scale wastewater reuse can unlock an investment opportunity **exceeding ₹3 lakh crore** and **~1 lakh additional jobs by 2047**, while cutting urban freshwater stress.
- Example: **Thane** can bridge a **53 million-litres-per-day** water deficit through expanded reuse of treated wastewater.
- Stress cities flagged: **Narsinghpur (MP), Ahmedabad (Gujarat), Barmer (Rajasthan)**. Reuse uses: agriculture/horticulture, urban greening, construction, sanitation, industry (textiles), water-body rejuvenation.
- Policy backing: the *"Water, Nature, Progress"* study and the **Economic Survey 2025–26**.

**The other side of the debate.** Reuse faces real hurdles — public reluctance to use recycled water, under-performing/idle Sewage Treatment Plants, weak ULB finances and capacity, and tariff/pricing politics. Without dual-piping, monitoring and viability-gap funding, "circular water" can stay aspirational.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- Frame water reuse as a *triple dividend* — water security + green jobs + climate resilience — feeding the Viksit Bharat @2047 narrative.
- Financing model to cite: **blended finance** (public viability-gap + private capital) and a proposed **National Circular Water Mission**.
- Connect to AMRUT 2.0, Jal Jeevan/urban water, sponge-city ideas and SDG-6 for a layered answer.

## GS Mains practice question (this week's dominant theme)

> **Q.** The 2026 West Asia crisis and the subsequent U.S.–Iran understanding have once again exposed the fragility of India's energy security and the limits of military power in the region. **Critically examine** the vulnerabilities this episode revealed for India and the measures India should adopt to safeguard its energy and strategic interests. **(15 marks, 250 words)**
>
> *Directive — "critically examine": lay out the issue, weigh both sides/limitations, and arrive at a reasoned, balanced position.*
> *Maps to: GS2 (India and West Asia; effect of developed-country policies on India's interests) + GS3 (energy security).*

**Model-answer skeleton (5 points).**

1. **Intro / context (2–3 lines).** Frame the 2026 Iran war, the Hormuz disruption and the 17 June U.S.–Iran MoU; note Hormuz carries ~one-fifth of global oil & gas and India imports the bulk of its crude, much of it through this chokepoint.
2. **Vulnerabilities exposed.** High import dependence + single-chokepoint risk (Hormuz); oil-price volatility hitting the current-account deficit, inflation and the rupee; thin strategic-reserve cover; exposure of the ~9-million-strong Gulf diaspora and remittances; risk to connectivity plans like IMEC.
3. **Energy measures.** Diversify crude sources (US, Russia, Latin America, Africa); expand and fill Strategic Petroleum Reserves; accelerate the energy transition — renewables, green hydrogen, ethanol blending, nuclear — to cut structural import reliance.
4. **Strategic / diplomatic measures.** Maintain strategic autonomy via *de-hyphenated* ties with Israel, Iran and the Gulf (Saudi Arabia, UAE); act as a net security provider in the Indian Ocean Region; keep evacuation machinery ready (Operation Ganga/Kaveri template); back a cooperative Hormuz/littoral-states security framework.
5. **Conclusion.** "Limits of military power" → primacy of diplomacy, multilateralism and *self-reliance*; tie energy resilience to Atmanirbharta and Viksit Bharat @2047.
