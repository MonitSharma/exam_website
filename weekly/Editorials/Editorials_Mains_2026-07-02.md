## 1. Reimagining sovereign AI for India's strategic future

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed — Vivan Sharan (Partner) & Vedika Pandey (Manager), Koan Advisory (~1 Jul 2026) · *GS3 (science & technology, industrial policy, economic policy) + GS2 (technology diplomacy, effect of developed-country policies on India)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines):** The authors argue India must reject the false binary between globalisation and industrial policy on AI. India should keep using the best available foreign frontier AI models to build competitiveness now, while steadily deepening domestic capability — "backward linkages" to frontier tech and "forward linkages" via globally competitive AI products — so it isn't left strategically dependent on decisions made elsewhere.

**Key data points/facts:**
- Trigger: the US Commerce Department directed Anthropic to suspend foreign-national access to its Fable 5/Mythos 5 models (12 Jun 2026) on national-security grounds — showing how abruptly access to frontier AI can be withdrawn.
- India's Gross Expenditure on R&D (GERD) is only ~0.6% of GDP (Economic Survey 2025–26), with the private sector contributing just ~36–41% of it, against 70–79% in the US, China and South Korea.
- OpenAI alone is projected to spend ~$50 billion on compute this year — over 6x India's entire annual private R&D spend.
- Cautionary parallel: despite the PLI scheme for bulk drugs, India still sources ~65% of critical pharmaceutical APIs from China (NITI Aayog Trade Watch Quarterly) — industrial policy alone builds "footholds, not instant resilience."
- No Indian app currently ranks among the world's top 10 by downloads or revenue, while the Philippines' IT-BPM exports (~$40 billion) are already close to a sixth of India's.

**Main counter-argument / other side:** Critics would note the US export-control action against Anthropic is itself the biggest risk to the authors' own strategy — if Washington can abruptly cut off a close ally's access to frontier models, "using foreign AI now, building capability later" rests on an assumption of stable access that the very episode under discussion disproves. Also, India's low R&D-to-GDP ratio makes "deepening backward linkages" easier to state as policy than to fund at scale, and India's historically weak inter-ministerial coordination (visible even in the fragmented pharma-API response) makes a "whole-of-government AI strategy" harder to deliver than to propose.

**Mains value-addition points:**
- Use the "diffusion–dependence paradox" framing: using foreign AI to build the economic surplus needed to depend on it less later — good for any answer on technology and strategic autonomy.
- Cite the pharma-API parallel (65% China-sourced APIs despite PLI) as a ready-made illustration that industrial policy alone doesn't create instant resilience — reusable across GS3 self-reliance/Atmanirbhar answers.
- Government risk-sharing tools worth naming: export credit guarantees and hybrid-annuity models, both proven Indian instruments that could be extended to de-risk private AI investment.

## 2. Missile asymmetry on the LAC — the case for an Integrated Rocket Force

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed — Harinder Singh, retired Lieutenant General & independent analyst (30 Jun 2026) · *GS3 (defence technology & indigenisation, border management, internal/external security) + GS2 (India–China relations, institutional mechanisms like the CDS)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines):** The author argues China treats missiles as instruments of both coercion and war-fighting (an estimated 200+ conventional launchers opposite India via its Western Theatre Command), while India's approach remains largely deterrence-only and institutionally fragmented. He calls for a unified, CDS-led Integrated Rocket Force (IRF) — first proposed by Gen. Bipin Rawat in 2021 — to close this gap before a border crisis, not during one.

**Key data points/facts:**
- China's PLA Rocket Force (PLARF) was elevated to a full service branch on 31 December 2015; its Western Theatre Command (est. 2016, HQ Chengdu) covers the ~3,488 km LAC and fields systems up to the DF-26 (claimed range 4,000–5,000+ km, dual nuclear/conventional capable).
- India's Chief of Defence Staff post (created Dec 2019, effective Jan 2020) already unifies nuclear delivery under the Strategic Forces Command (2003); the proposed IRF would be a separate, purely conventional tri-service command — still conceptual, with the short-range Pralay missile (150–500 km, approved 2023) its only inducted asset so far.
- India's longest-range operational missile, Agni-V (5,000+ km), became MIRV-capable via Mission Divyastra (March 2024), making India the sixth MIRV-capable nation.
- The author's core "deterrence logic": India doesn't need missile-for-missile parity with China, only credible, comparable-effect retaliatory capability.

**Main counter-argument / other side:** Critics would flag real trade-offs the author underplays: pre-delegating strike authority to a military command in a crisis's opening hours sits uneasily with India's tradition of strong civilian control over escalatory decisions; adopting a "counter-value" doctrine (targeting infrastructure, not just military assets) could lower the threshold for escalation rather than restrain it; and India's missile-industrial base — propulsion, semiconductors, advanced materials — faces real cost overruns and import dependence, making a credible rocket force a multi-decade industrial project, not a quick reorganisation. Some would also argue India's deliberate separation of nuclear (SFC) and conventional (proposed IRF) forces is itself a stabilising, restraint-signalling choice worth preserving.

**Mains value-addition points:**
- Use the China-vs-India institutional contrast (PLARF as an operational 4th service branch since 2015 vs India's still-conceptual IRF) as a crisp comparative point in any India–China security answer.
- The "strategic depth" argument — the Himalayas favour China's downward missile reach over India's upward one — is a good geography-meets-security value-add.
- Flag the civil–military control tension (pre-delegated strike authority vs democratic oversight) as a balancing counterpoint whenever discussing defence-reform proposals.

## 3. Sharing waters — the Tungabhadra dam

**Source:** The Hindu, Editorial (~29 Jun 2026, built around the 25 Jun 2026 inauguration) · *GS1 (rivers, dams) + GS2 (inter-State relations, cooperative federalism, Article 262) + GS3 (water resource management, dam safety)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines):** The Chief Ministers of Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh jointly inaugurated the Tungabhadra dam's 33 newly replaced crest gates — a rare instance of three-State consensus on river water. The editorial reads this as proof that institutional design (a tribunal-fixed sharing formula plus a standing joint board) can convert even a serious infrastructure failure into a cooperative-federalism success, while cautioning that deeper distributional disputes (Upper Bhadra, Telangana's RDS claims) remain unresolved beneath the ceremony.

**Key data points/facts:**
- Trigger: Crest Gate No. 19 was washed away on 10 August 2024, releasing an estimated 70,000–1,00,000 cusecs uncontrolled — the dam's first major structural failure in ~70 years. All 33 gates were replaced with high-grade steel at a cost of ₹51 crore in ~123 days, restoring full storage of 105 TMC.
- Water-sharing is fixed by the Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal (KWDT-I, "Bachawat Tribunal," 1969; final order gazetted 1976) at a 65:35 ratio between Karnataka and undivided Andhra Pradesh; the Tungabhadra Board (constituted 1953) manages day-to-day operations.
- Post-2014 bifurcation, Telangana holds a 15.9 TMC entitlement, accessed via the ageing Rajolibanda Diversion Scheme canal.
- Reservoir capacity has fallen from an original 133 TMC to ~106 TMC due to siltation — a problem the editorial flags as a wider, unresolved national issue.
- The Centre budgeted ₹5,300 crore for Karnataka's upstream Upper Bhadra project in 2023-24, then withdrew it from central schemes — a point the editorial cites as inconsistent Central facilitation.

**Main counter-argument / other side:** The cooperative optics are real but narrow: the joint inauguration resolved an infrastructure failure, not the harder distributional question. The Upper Bhadra lift-irrigation project upstream continues to worry Andhra Pradesh and Telangana about reduced inflows, Telangana continues to assert its RDS entitlement, and the Centre's inconsistent funding (budgeted, then withdrawn) raises doubts about whether the proposed High-Level Committee will have durable institutional backing rather than being another one-off gesture.

**Mains value-addition points:**
- Contrast the Tungabhadra's comparatively dispute-free history (tribunal formula + standing joint board) with the long-running Cauvery dispute — a strong illustrative pair for any "institutional mechanisms for inter-State water disputes" answer.
- Cite Article 262 and the ISRWD Act, 1956 as the constitutional/statutory basis for river-water tribunals — reusable across polity/federalism answers.
- Use "133 TMC → 106 TMC due to siltation" as a live data point for the broader, under-discussed issue of ageing dam infrastructure and desilting neglect across India.

## 4. India's "Israel habit" meets West Asian realities

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed — Vinay Kaura, Sardar Patel University of Police, Security and Criminal Justice; Non-Resident Fellow, ISAS-NUS (~1 Jul 2026) · *GS2 (India and West Asia, effect of regional developments on India's interests, foreign policy)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines):** The author argues India's deepening tilt toward Israel has become a matter of habit rather than fresh strategic deliberation, risking India being caught on the wrong side of a rapidly reconfiguring West Asia. He calls for an "architectonic" foreign policy — one that actively shapes India's strategic environment — rather than an adaptive one that merely follows alignments set by Washington or Tel Aviv, moving beyond a binary Israel-or-Iran framing.

**Key data points/facts:**
- Context: a joint US–Israeli strike killed Ayatollah Ali Khamenei on 28 Feb 2026; his son Mojtaba Khamenei was elected Supreme Leader by Iran's Assembly of Experts on 8 March 2026; the state funeral (delayed multiple times) is scheduled for 3–9 July 2026.
- PM Modi's second state visit to Israel (25–26 Feb 2026) — addressing the Knesset and signing a Critical and Emerging Technologies Partnership — came just two days before the US-Israel strikes on Iran.
- For the Khamenei funeral, India sent Minister of State Pabitra Margherita paired with Bihar Governor Lt Gen (retd) Syed Ata Hasnain, rather than the PM — read by the author as calibrated reassurance to Tehran.
- Millions of Indian workers in the Gulf send home remittances; most of India's oil imports transit routes vulnerable to Gulf conflict, including the Strait of Hormuz.

**Main counter-argument / other side:** The relationship with Israel delivers real, non-symbolic gains — substantial defence technology, know-how and counter-insurgency expertise, plus genuine economic complementarity via the new tech partnership and progress toward a free trade agreement — built on a historically reliable, crisis-tested partnership. From this view, continuity with Israel is a considered strategic asset, not mere inertia, even if it carries opportunity costs with Iran and reputational costs with the Global South.

**Mains value-addition points:**
- Use the "architectonic vs adaptive" foreign policy distinction — a crisp conceptual hook for any answer on India's strategic autonomy or multi-alignment.
- Cite India's calibrated funeral-delegation signalling as a concrete example of hedging diplomacy in practice, even without an explicit stated doctrine.
- Flag the Global South credibility cost (visible Israel alignment vs Global South sympathy for Palestinians) as a value-add point whenever discussing India's Vishwabandhu / Global South leadership claims.

## GS Mains practice question (this week's dominant theme: GS3 — Defence & Security)

> **"India's approach to missile warfare has traditionally been deterrence-oriented and service-fragmented, while its principal adversary treats missiles as instruments of both coercion and war-fighting."** Examine the security challenges this asymmetry poses for India along the Line of Actual Control, and discuss the institutional reforms needed to address it. **(15 marks, ~250 words)**

**Model-answer skeleton:**
1. **Intro:** Frame the changing character of missile warfare as fast, "political," and infrastructure-targeting rather than purely military — and note the asymmetry between China's operational PLA Rocket Force (a full service branch since 2015) and India's still-conceptual Integrated Rocket Force.
2. **The strategic problem:** Himalayan terrain now favours China's downward missile reach over India's upward one; China's Western Theatre Command fields systems (DF-21C to DF-26) capable of holding Indian infrastructure at risk without a declared war.
3. **India's institutional gaps:** fragmented command of long-range assets (Agni, BrahMos, LR-LACM) across services; the IRF remains a proposal (first floated 2021) with only the short-range Pralay inducted so far; no unified cross-service target list.
4. **The case for reform, with guardrails:** a CDS-led, phased Integrated Rocket Force building on the existing Strategic Forces Command precedent — but explicitly preserving civilian control over escalatory/strike decisions, given the risk that pre-delegated authority and counter-value targeting could lower the threshold for escalation rather than restrain it.
5. **Conclusion:** Deterrence credibility (comparable effects, not missile-for-missile parity) should be pursued through institutional unification and sustained indigenous investment — not at the cost of India's traditionally restrained, civilian-controlled approach to nuclear and conventional escalation.
