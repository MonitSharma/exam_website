## 1. Can biogas aid India's energy security?
**Source:** The Hindu, *Text & Context / explainer-analysis* (~22 July 2026) · *GS3 (environment — renewable energy, circular economy; economy — energy security, infrastructure)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** India's heavy dependence on imported crude leaves it exposed to price and supply shocks, sharpened by the recent West Asia disruption. Compressed biogas (CBG) — chemically near-identical to CNG but made from crop residue, cattle dung, press-mud and municipal waste — is pitched as a domestic, carbon-neutral fuel that also solves a waste-management problem, i.e. an energy answer and an environmental answer in one.

**Key data / facts.**
- India imports roughly **85% of its crude oil**, much of it routed through West Asia; energy-import bills are a standing macro vulnerability.
- **SATAT** (Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation, launched 2018) targets **5,000 CBG plants**; by 2024 only a few dozen were commissioned with several hundred in the pipeline — a large **execution gap** against target.
- CBG is renewable and carbon-neutral, supports a **circular economy** (waste → fuel + bio-slurry fertiliser), and cuts stubble-burning (a Delhi-NCR air-pollution driver). Linked schemes: **GOBARdhan**, **CBG–CGD blending**, National Bioenergy Programme.

**The other side / counter-argument.** CBG's contribution to the total energy basket is still marginal; feedstock aggregation and logistics are hard, plant economics are thin without assured offtake and price support, and mandatory blending can raise CNG costs. Critics argue biogas is a useful *complement* — good for rural/agri-waste and air quality — not a genuine substitute for the scale that oil, gas or nuclear provide.

**Mains value-addition points.**
- Frame CBG as a **triple-win**: energy security + circular economy + farm income (residue monetised, not burned) — a ready GS3 example bridging energy, environment and agriculture.
- Use the **SATAT target-vs-achievement gap** as a concrete illustration that scheme announcement ≠ ground delivery (reusable across GS3 "why targets slip" answers).
- Pair with air-quality answers: **CBG as a structural fix for stubble burning**, unlike seasonal bans.

## 2. The right path for India's nuclear power development
**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed (~22 July 2026), read alongside the *"India–Australia uranium supplies agreement"* explainer · *GS3 (science & technology, energy, infrastructure) + GS2 (IR — nuclear diplomacy, effect of external agreements on India)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** With a net-zero-by-2070 pledge and a **Viksit Bharat** energy demand curve, India is betting big on nuclear — targeting **100 GW by 2047** from a current base of only ~8 GW. The op-ed argues the leap needs three things at once: legislative reform to admit private and foreign capital, a credible **Small Modular Reactor (SMR)** programme, and secure fuel (uranium) supply — and cautions against treating headline targets as self-executing.

**Key data / facts.**
- Current installed nuclear capacity ≈ **8 GW** (~3% of the mix); the **100 GW-by-2047** target implies a step-change in build rate.
- **Nuclear Energy Mission** (Union Budget 2025-26): **₹20,000 crore** outlay, at least **five indigenous SMRs by 2033**; BARC designs include the **BSMR-200** and **SMR-55**.
- The long-standing barrier — **Section 17(b) of the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010** (supplier liability beyond global norms) — kept foreign vendors out; 2025 reform efforts (amendments to the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and CLNDA; the SHANTI Act, 2025 as reported) aim to open the sector to private capital.
- **Australia** is a major uranium source; the India–Australia civil-nuclear framework (agreement 2014, supplies from 2017) matters for fuel security, alongside domestic thorium/three-stage plans.

**The other side / counter-argument.** Nuclear is capital-intensive, slow to build and carries waste-disposal, land-acquisition and public-acceptance risks (post-Kudankulam/Jaitapur protests). Diluting supplier liability shifts risk from vendors toward victims/the state, raising accountability concerns. Sceptics say the same money in solar+storage delivers capacity faster and cheaper, and that 100 GW by 2047 is optimistic on current execution.

**Mains value-addition points.**
- **Energy trilemma** framing — security, affordability, sustainability: nuclear scores on security + low-carbon but is challenged on cost + acceptance; use it to structure balanced energy answers.
- The **liability-vs-accountability trade-off** (attracting suppliers by capping their liability) is a crisp GS2/GS3 governance tension worth naming explicitly.
- **SMRs as the "co-location" idea** — modular reactors on retiring coal sites reusing grid/land — a forward-looking point for just-transition answers.

## 3. The anti-defection law back in the dock
**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion (~21 July 2026, prompted by Monsoon-Session floor-crossings) · *GS2 (polity — Parliament & State legislatures, separation of powers, salient features of the Constitution)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** A fresh round of party-switching has revived the debate that the **Tenth Schedule** has failed its purpose: instead of curbing defection it now enables *bulk* defections dressed up as "mergers," while the Speaker's dual role as partisan presiding officer and neutral adjudicator invites delay and bias.

**Key data / facts.**
- The anti-defection law was inserted by the **52nd Amendment (1985)** as the **Tenth Schedule**; the **91st Amendment (2003)** deleted the one-third "split" exemption and now requires a **two-thirds merger**.
- **Kihoto Hollohan (1992):** the Speaker's disqualification decision is a quasi-judicial act **subject to judicial review**, but courts cannot intervene until a decision is made — enabling indefinite deferral.
- Recurrent problem: Speakers sitting on petitions for months/years so the term effectively expires without disqualification.

**The other side / counter-argument.** Defenders say the law has broadly stabilised governments and reduced the "Aaya Ram Gaya Ram" horse-trading of the 1960s–70s; the real fix is procedural (a fixed timeline, or shifting adjudication to an independent tribunal / the ECI) rather than scrapping the law, which could return legislators to unrestrained defection.

**Mains value-addition points.**
- Cite the **Speaker-as-adjudicator conflict** and the reform menu — **time-bound decisions, an independent tribunal, or ECI adjudication** (echoing Supreme Court observations) — as the standard value-addition on this topic.
- Tie to the wider theme of **intra-party democracy and voter mandate** — defection defeats the mandate the voter gave to a party symbol.

## 4. Declining fertility reflects social progress, not crisis
**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion (~22 July 2026) · *GS1 (Indian society — population & associated issues, role of women, social empowerment) + GS2 (governance — social protection)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Falling fertility should be read as an outcome of **expanding choice** — more education, delayed marriage, women's aspirations — not a demographic emergency to be "corrected" by pronatalist nudges. The right response is enabling conditions (economic security, gender equality, childcare) that let young people choose education *and* parenthood, rather than incentives to raise births.

**Key data / facts.**
- India's **Total Fertility Rate has fallen to ~2.0** (NFHS-5), just below the **replacement level of 2.1** — the demographic transition is largely complete nationally, with wide state variation (south/west below, some north/east above).
- Fertility decline correlates with **rising female education, later age at marriage, and urbanisation**.
- The "solution set" the piece favours: **childcare infrastructure, parental leave, and social security** rather than cash-for-babies schemes.

**The other side / counter-argument.** Some states and commentators worry about below-replacement fertility for **ageing, workforce shrinkage, fiscal/pension pressure**, and the **delimitation-and-federal-share** debate (states that curbed population fear losing seats/funds). They argue for at least mild pronatal and old-age-security planning now, before the demographic dividend closes.

**Mains value-addition points.**
- **Reframe the "population problem"**: the challenge is shifting from *quantity* to *ageing + quality of the workforce* — a sharper GS1 society framing than the old Malthusian line.
- **Federalism angle:** fertility divergence + delimitation is a live Centre–State equity issue — cross-links GS1 society to GS2 polity.
- **Childcare as infrastructure** (carried over from the 25 June & 9 July packs) fits here too — enabling women's workforce participation.

## GS Mains practice question (this week's dominant theme: GS3 — Energy security & sustainable development)

> **Q. India's quest for energy security increasingly rests on low-carbon options such as nuclear power and compressed biogas. Evaluate the potential of these two options to reduce India's import dependence, and examine the principal constraints in scaling them. (15 marks, 250 words)**

> *Directive — **"evaluate"** (with a supporting **"examine"**): weigh how far each option can actually move the needle, i.e. reach a judgement on worth/effectiveness rather than merely describe. Deliberately varied from recent weeks' "examine" (25 Jun), "critically examine" (9 Jul) and "discuss" (16 Jul).*
>
> *Maps to: GS3 — infrastructure/energy, environment (sustainable development), science & technology; with a GS2 (IR — uranium supply) edge.*

**Model-answer skeleton (4–5 points).**
1. **Intro / stakes:** anchor on the ~85% crude-import dependence and the net-zero-2070 + Viksit Bharat demand curve — energy security now means *decarbonised* security.
2. **Nuclear — potential:** 100 GW-by-2047 target, Nuclear Energy Mission (₹20,000 cr, 5 SMRs by 2033), legislative opening to private/foreign capital, uranium supply via Australia — high, firm, low-carbon base-load.
3. **Biogas — potential:** SATAT's 5,000-plant target, circular economy, stubble-burning fix, farm income, CGD blending — decentralised, quick-to-deploy, waste-to-energy.
4. **Constraints (the "examine" half):** nuclear — cost, build-time, liability-vs-accountability, land/public acceptance; biogas — thin plant economics, feedstock logistics, marginal share, SATAT target-vs-achievement gap.
5. **Balanced conclusion:** treat them as **complementary within an energy mix** (nuclear for firm base-load, biogas for decentralised/rural + air quality), delivery and financing — not targets — being the binding constraint; add solar+storage for balance.
