## 1. In India, voting cannot remain merely a statutory right

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed — **S.Y. Quraishi**, former Chief Election Commissioner of India (author, *An Undocumented Wonder: The Making of the Great Indian Election*) · ~7 July 2026 · *GS2 (Indian Constitution, fundamental rights, judiciary, electoral reforms)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Quraishi argues that seven decades of Supreme Court doctrine treating the right to vote as *merely statutory* now sits awkwardly with the Court's own case law, which has progressively given constitutional protection to facets *around* the vote — the right to know, informed choice, ballot secrecy, the right to reject (NOTA) — while withholding that status from the core act of voting itself. Since democracy and free and fair elections are part of the **basic structure**, he contends the citizen's entitlement to vote should not remain outside the constitutional core — even if procedural mechanics stay with Parliament.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- **The "statutory right" line:** *N.P. Ponnuswami* v. Returning Officer (1952) held the right to vote is a creature of statute, not a common-law or fundamental right; reaffirmed in *Jyoti Basu* v. Debi Ghosal (1982, "purely a statutory right") and *Kuldip Nayar* v. Union of India (2006, vote flows from the RP Act, 1951).
- **The constitutionalising trend around the vote:** *ADR* (2002) — right to know candidates' antecedents (Art. 19(1)(a)); *PUCL* (2003) — "freedom of voting"/informed choice held fundamental; **NOTA** judgment (2013) — rejecting all candidates is protected political expression.
- ***Anoop Baranwal*** (2023): the Constitution Bench majority (Justice K.M. Joseph) called voting a **constitutional right** flowing from Article 326; Justice Rastogi's **separate opinion** went further and called it a **fundamental right** — signalling movement, but not a formal overruling of the *Ponnuswami* line.
- **Textual anchors:** Article 326 (universal adult suffrage, 18+); Article 327 (Parliament's plenary power over elections — the source of the RP Acts); Article 329(b) (bar on judicial interference during elections); basic structure from *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) and free/fair elections as basic structure from *Indira Nehru Gandhi* v. *Raj Narain* (1975).

**The other side of the debate.** Seven decades of settled precedent treat the vote as statutory; reversing this needs a larger Bench, and courts are traditionally reluctant to read a *new* fundamental right into the Constitution without clear Part III text. Making the vote an absolute fundamental right could also invite litigation against routine electoral regulation, straining Parliament's plenary competence under Article 327. And *Anoop Baranwal*'s "fundamental right" formulation remains only a **single judge's separate opinion** — the doctrinal shift Quraishi describes is a *trend*, not authoritative law.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- Use the **"constitutional orphan" paradox** — if rejecting all candidates (NOTA) is constitutionally protected expression, why is *choosing* one candidate merely statutory? A crisp, examiner-friendly hook for any answer on the franchise or electoral democracy.
- Deploy Quraishi's **limiting principle**: constitutionalise the "core right to participate," but leave *mechanics* (age, residency, disqualification for corrupt practices) to statute under Article 327 — shows balance, not one-sidedness.
- Link to **SIR / electoral-roll accuracy** (covered 11 June): if the entitlement to be an elector is already constitutional in character (Art. 326), *wrongful exclusion* from the rolls strikes at a constitutional guarantee — a reusable line on ECI autonomy vs. the right to vote.

## 2. The right to belong beyond official documentation

**Source:** The Hindu, Editorial · ~6 July 2026 · *GS2 (citizenship provisions, judiciary, ECI) + GS1 (migration & demographic change)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** Triggered by the MEA's clarification (24 June 2026, 14th Passport Seva Divas) that an Indian passport is a **"travel document," not a "citizenship document,"** the editorial argues that across recent processes — SIR of electoral rolls, the Section 6A verdict, the operationalised CAA — the **burden of proving citizenship has shifted from the state to the individual**, with each identity document (Aadhaar, voter ID, now the passport) in turn described as insufficient. Its thesis: citizenship and the rights flowing from it should rest on **personhood and constitutional commitments** (equality, secularism, non-discrimination), not on documentary sufficiency alone.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- **Constitutional/statutory frame:** Part II (Articles 5–11); **Article 11** gives Parliament plenary power over acquisition/termination of citizenship. The Citizenship Act, 1955 originally reflected *jus soli*; a 2003 amendment moved away from it (denying citizenship where a parent was an "illegal migrant").
- **Section 6A** (inserted 1985 via the Assam Accord): entry before 1966 → full citizenship; 1966–25 March 1971 → citizenship with a 10-year voting bar; after 25 March 1971 → illegal migrant. **Upheld 4:1** by a Constitution Bench (17 October 2024; Justice Pardiwala dissenting).
- **CAA, 2019:** assented 12 Dec 2019, in force 10 Jan 2020, but **rules notified only 11 March 2024**; first certificates 15 May 2024; accelerated pathway for six non-Muslim communities from Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan (cut-off extended to 31 Dec 2024 by a Sept 2025 notification).
- ***ADR* v. ECI (27 May 2026):** upheld the Bihar SIR; ECI may run a **"limited" citizenship inquiry** for roll accuracy (distinct from final adjudication), with excluded persons referred to the "competent authority" for time-bound resolution.
- **Passports Act, 1967, Section 20:** a passport may be issued to a *non-citizen* only where "necessary...in the public interest" — an exception, which arguably understates the passport's evidentiary weight for ordinary applicants.

**The other side of the debate.** The MEA's distinction may be **technically accurate** — the Passports Act is a travel-and-identity statute, while the Citizenship Act is the substantive law for citizenship; the clarification can be read as legal, not rights-restricting. Periodic **roll verification** is an established state function, and the Supreme Court framed the ECI's role as a "principled" distinction between administration and adjudication. Article 11's text supports a **wide** parliamentary reading (as the 2024 Section 6A ruling shows), and documented illegal-migration concerns in specific States (*Sonowal*, 2005) give a non-arbitrary basis for verification.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- **Personhood vs. citizenship rights:** Articles 14 and 21 extend to "any person," while Article 19's freedoms and the franchise are citizenship-contingent — so a citizenship determination is consequential *well beyond* voting. Pair with Hannah Arendt's "**right to have rights**" (cited in the editorial) for an essay-grade framing.
- **Constituent Assembly precedent:** the rejection of P.S. Deshmukh's religion-based citizenship amendment and adoption of Ambedkar's neutral clause (now Article 5) is documented founding-era consensus **against religion as a citizenship criterion** — a strong value-add on secularism.
- Flag the **"doubtful"/limbo category** risk (from Assam's foreigners'-tribunal experience): an intermediate administrative status — neither confirmed citizen nor adjudicated foreigner — with rights held in abeyance. Ties neatly to pick #1 (wrongful exclusion from rolls).

*(Together, picks #1 and #2 form this week's dominant GS2 theme — the **vote and citizenship as the two faces of democratic membership**, and how the evidentiary burden on the individual is rising in both.)*

## 3. India needs a second home for the Asiatic lion

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-Ed — **Richa Singh**, Senior Consultant, EY India · ~6 July 2026 · *GS3 (biodiversity, protected areas, species reintroduction) + GS2 (Centre–State cooperation on wildlife)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** The Asiatic lion's recovery — from a few dozen animals to **891 in the 2025 census** — is a celebrated success, but the entire wild population sits in **one landscape (Gir, Gujarat)**, leaving it exposed to a single catastrophic event (epidemic, fire, disaster). The piece argues India must move from *conservation success* to **conservation security** via a **metapopulation** approach, and that the Supreme Court's **2013 direction** to establish a second, geographically distinct population at Kuno (Madhya Pradesh) remains unimplemented over a decade later.

**Key data / facts cited.**

- **Population trend:** ~20 (1930s) → 411 (2010) → 523 (2015) → 674 (2020) → **891 (2025)** — a 32.2% rise in five years; range up from ~30,000 sq km (2020) to ~35,000 sq km (2025). Of the 891, **394** are in Gir's core and **507** across nine satellite populations. IUCN status upgraded from Critically Endangered to **Endangered (2008)**.
- **The 2013 judgment (15 April 2013):** directed lion translocation to Kuno within six months *and* quashed the plan to bring African cheetahs to Kuno *ahead* of lions — prioritising lions.
- **The Kuno complication:** Kuno was upgraded to National Park (2018) and ~24 villages relocated (1996–2003) to free ~345 sq km — yet no lion has been moved. **Project Cheetah** cheetahs arrived (Namibia, Sept 2022; South Africa, Feb 2023); in March 2023 the Centre told the SC it was re-examining translocation over inter-species competition.
- **The risk, empirically:** the **2018 Canine Distemper Virus (CDV)** outbreak killed **28 lions in ~2 weeks** in Gir — documented single-site vulnerability, compounded by a genetic bottleneck. **Project Lion** (announced 2020; ₹2,900 crore, 25-year roadmap approved March 2025) currently emphasises "assisted natural dispersal" *within* Saurashtra, not inter-State translocation.

**The other side of the debate.** Gujarat's **in-situ model has demonstrably worked** — an expanding, range-diversifying population across nine satellite pockets. Kuno's **changed ecological context** (cheetahs now establishing there) is a genuine technical objection, not merely political. Translocation carries real capture/transport/habituation stress and cost. And with wildlife on the **Concurrent List**, federal cooperation **cannot be judicially compelled indefinitely** without straining Centre–State conservation partnerships.

**Mains value-addition points.**

- The **"success vs. security" / metapopulation** distinction is a ready-made thesis: *population growth ≠ risk diversification* — satellite populations near Gir (e.g., Barda, 17 lions, first presence since 1879) don't substitute for a genuinely **distant** second home.
- Use the **2018 CDV outbreak** as concrete, non-hypothetical evidence for the single-site-catastrophe argument — reusable across any biodiversity/protected-areas answer.
- Frame the **federalism angle**: the SC called the lion "national heritage," yet implementation hinges on State cooperation — a live example of the **gap between judicial direction and cooperative-federalism delivery** (pair with the Tungabhadra federalism point from 2 July).

## 4. India's economic prospects for 2026-27 — a contingent optimism

**Source:** The Hindu, Editorial/Analysis · ~8 July 2026 · *GS3 (growth, inflation, fiscal policy, external sector, agriculture) + GS2 (energy diplomacy)*

**Core argument (2–3 lines).** With a preliminary US–Iran understanding expected to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and soften crude, the piece assesses how India's 2026-27 outlook should be reframed. Its stance is one of **contingent optimism**: growth, inflation, fiscal arithmetic and the external balance all look manageable **if** West Asian peace holds — but the bigger *domestic* swing factor is an **El Niño-linked monsoon shortfall** threatening agriculture and food inflation. *(Note: the West Asia trigger overlaps recent packs — the value here is the domestic macro-and-agriculture reading, which is fresh.)*

**Key data / facts cited.**

- **Growth:** GDP grew **7.7% (2025-26, NSO provisional)**; RBI projects **6.6% for 2026-27**, lower — reflecting a Q1 crude disruption and monsoon risk.
- **Crude/energy:** Indian crude basket eased from **$114.5/bbl (April 2026)** to **$86.3/bbl (24 June)**; crude **import dependence >90%** (up from 54.9% in 1998-99), domestic output down to 26 MMT (from a 35.9 MMT peak in 2011-12) — a structural vulnerability the Hormuz episode exposed.
- **Agriculture risk:** IMD projected a ~10% rainfall shortfall vs. Long Period Average, but the **actual shortfall touched ~43% up to 24 June 2026** — a much sharper downside to kharif/rabi output, rural incomes and food inflation than headline figures suggest.
- **Fiscal cushion:** the **RBI dividend of ₹2.69 lakh crore (2024-25)** helps the budgeted **4.3%-of-GDP fiscal deficit** target hold even if fuel-excise cuts or higher fertilizer/food subsidies bite; CAD was 0.6% of GDP (2025-26).

**The other side of the debate.** The whole outlook rests on a **fragile assumption** — that the West Asian peace holds — and the piece offers no contingency roadmap for conflict resumption. Import dependence >90% reflects a long-standing failure to expand domestic exploration that refining capacity doesn't fix. And the near-**43% rainfall shortfall** signals the editorial may *understate* the agricultural downside. (Also: the piece's nominal-growth and deficit projections are the **author's own blended estimates**, not official Finance Ministry/RBI forecasts.)

**Mains value-addition points.**

- Map the **transmission channels** cleanly for any external-shock economy answer: crude → inflation (CPI/WPI/IPD) → fiscal (subsidies, tax buoyancy, deficit) → current account → rupee.
- **Low-regret hedging** as the policy takeaway: build **strategic reserves of crude *and* fertilizer**, diversify crude sourcing away from the Hormuz route, and recalibrate crop-specific trade policy *ahead* of El Niño stress — worth doing regardless of whether peace holds.
- Use **falling energy intensity of GDP** as a positive structural point — India absorbs price shocks better than in the past — to avoid a one-sidedly pessimistic answer.

## GS Mains practice question (this week's dominant theme: GS2 — Polity & Constitution)

> **Q.** "Indian courts have long treated the right to vote as a *statutory* right, even as they have progressively extended *constitutional* protection to the facets surrounding it." **Critically examine** this position in light of the basic-structure doctrine and recent judicial trends, and consider whether the core act of voting merits constitutional recognition. **(15 marks, ~250 words)**
>
> *Directive — "critically examine": set out the doctrine, weigh both sides, and reach a reasoned, balanced position (don't argue only one way).*
> *Maps to: GS2 (Indian Constitution — fundamental rights, judiciary, electoral reforms). Directive deliberately varied from recent weeks' "examine"/"examine and discuss."*

**Model-answer skeleton (5 points).**

1. **Intro / frame the paradox.** State the *Ponnuswami–Jyoti Basu–Kuldip Nayar* line (vote = statutory) and the paradox that adjacent rights — right to know (*ADR*, 2002), freedom of voting (*PUCL*, 2003), the right to reject/NOTA (2013) — have been constitutionalised while the core act has not. Thesis: the doctrine is increasingly incongruent.
2. **The case for constitutional recognition.** Basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) and free/fair elections as its essential feature (*Indira Gandhi* v. *Raj Narain*, 1975); Article 326's command of universal adult suffrage as a textual anchor; the *Anoop Baranwal* (2023) shift to "constitutional right" language (Rastogi J. going to "fundamental"); the "constitutional orphan" logic (NOTA protected, but not the vote itself).
3. **The case for caution.** Seven decades of settled precedent; absence of Part III enumeration; Parliament's plenary power under Article 327; the risk that treating the vote as an absolute fundamental right invites litigation against routine electoral regulation; *Anoop Baranwal*'s "fundamental right" view is only a *separate* opinion.
4. **A calibrated middle path.** Constitutionalise the **"core right to participate"** while leaving *mechanics* — age, residency, disqualification for corrupt practices — to statute; connect to the live stakes of **electoral-roll exclusion (SIR)**, where the vote's constitutional status directly affects protection against arbitrary disenfranchisement.
5. **Conclusion.** A larger/Constitution Bench could reconcile the "statutory right" doctrine with the constitutional status that Article 326 and basic-structure jurisprudence already imply — securing the citizen's stake in "We, the People" without disrupting orderly election administration.

*(Optional extension for a 20-marker: bring in pick #2 — citizenship as the *precondition* for the franchise, and the rising evidentiary burden on the individual — to argue that the vote and citizenship are two faces of the same question of democratic membership.)*
