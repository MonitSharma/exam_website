## 1. A timely reset for the Food Security Act

**Source:** The Hindu, Lead article, 13 August 2026 · *GS3 (economy — PDS: objectives, functioning, limitations, revamping; food security; issues of buffer stocks) + GS2 (welfare schemes; issues relating to poverty and hunger)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The draft **National Food Security (Amendment) Bill, 2026** proposes replacing the flat 35 kg monthly entitlement for Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) households with a per-capita norm of 7 kg, still capped at 35 kg. The editorial's argument is that this looks like an equity correction but functions as a quiet cut: it removes support from small households without adding anything for large ones. Its second, larger claim is that India's food-security frame is now obsolete — the binding constraint has shifted from **calories to diet quality**, and a cereal-only PDS cannot address a country carrying undernutrition and diabetes simultaneously.

### Key data & facts

- Share of households unable to afford the **ICMR-NIN recommended diet fell from ~52% (2011-12) to 25% (2023-24)** — real progress, but a quarter of households still cannot afford a healthy diet.
- Current design: **Priority Households 5 kg per person/month; AAY a flat 35 kg per household** regardless of size. A seven-member AAY household therefore gets 5 kg per head; an eight-member household **4.4 kg** — *less* than a Priority Household member.
- Draft Bill: **7 kg per person, capped at 35 kg**. Effect: households of **1–4 members lose 20–80%** of current support; households of **five or more see no change**; **no AAY household gains grain**.
- **Tamil Nadu illustration: 84.5% of its AAY households have fewer than five members**; the State estimates its monthly AAY allocation could fall by **~35.6%**.
- Coverage ceiling is frozen in time: NFSA permits up to **75% rural / 50% urban** coverage, but the **81.35-crore ceiling rests on Census 2011**. Against an estimated **2025 population of 146.4 crore**, that is only **55.6%** of Indians.
- Nutrition outcomes are uneven: **NFHS-6** shows stunting under-five down **35.5% → 29.3%**, but **wasting and underweight barely moved**; only **~15% of children aged 6–23 months** get a minimally adequate diet.
- The other half of the double burden: **ICMR-INDIAB (2021) — 101 million Indians with diabetes, 136 million with prediabetes**. A 2025 ICMR-INDIAB paper found **carbohydrates supply 62.3% of daily energy**, and the highest-carbohydrate group had **30% higher odds of new type-2 diabetes**. Notably, swapping refined cereals for whole wheat or millet flour did **not** reduce risk while the carbohydrate *share* stayed high — substituting protein calories did. **ICMR-NIN 2024 guidelines cap cereals and millets at 45% of energy.**
- Delivery infrastructure is no longer the bottleneck: by end-2025, nearly all **5.51 lakh fair price shops** used **ePoS** devices and **One Nation One Ration Card** covered almost all beneficiaries.

### The other side / counter-argument

The per-capita principle is not indefensible. A flat household entitlement is genuinely regressive at the top of the size distribution, and the government can argue that horizontal equity across beneficiaries — equal grain per person — is the cleaner principle, with the 35 kg cap preventing runaway fiscal exposure. A second defence: with affordability of a healthy diet having roughly doubled since 2011-12, the marginal rupee arguably does more good in nutrition-diversification and NCD-prevention than in additional cereal, so rationalising the cereal bill is a legitimate reallocation, not austerity. Third, on coverage, the counter is that Census 2011 anchoring is a **data** problem, not a policy choice — the ceiling cannot be honestly recalculated until Census 2027 lands. The editorial's own answer is a design one: sequence the reform (no-loss guarantee first, per-capita top-ups reviewed separately on consumption evidence) and **budget diversification separately** rather than financing it out of cereal entitlements.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Cereal security is not food security."** A crisp framing line for any PDS, malnutrition or NCD answer — and it now has hard backing: the same population shows falling stunting and rising prediabetes.
- The **Census-2011-anchored ceiling** is a transferable example of *administrative data lag as a form of exclusion error* — it generalises to SECC-based targeting, MGNREGA job cards, and DBT coverage debates.
- **Sequencing as a policy principle**: the editorial's "no-loss safeguard" is really an argument that a reform which is Pareto-neutral in aggregate can still be regressive in distribution. Useful whenever a question asks you to evaluate a "rationalisation" measure.

## 2. VB-G RAM G — has the new rural employment law weakened the guarantee?

**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion column, 13 August 2026 · *GS3 (inclusive growth; employment; government budgeting) + GS2 (welfare schemes for vulnerable sections; issues arising out of their design and implementation; cooperative federalism)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The **Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission Gramin (VB-G RAM G)**, which replaced MGNREGA, promises *more* — 125 days instead of 100, universal rural coverage, a ₹300 floor wage — but the first implementation data show employment generation collapsing. The column's argument is that the guarantee has been **re-engineered from a demand-driven Central legal obligation into a supply-constrained, State-financed programme**, and that the digital gatekeeping layered on top is excluding workers rather than plugging leakages.

### Key data & facts

- Headline promises: **125 days** guaranteed employment (up from 100), universal rural coverage, **₹95,000 crore** Central allocation plus a mandated **40% State contribution** — a notional outlay of about **₹1.55 lakh crore**.
- Ground reality: **~50% year-on-year fall** in rural employment generated in the first month of implementation (**July 2026**). January–June 2026 person-days fell from **335.4 million (2025) to 215.2 million — a 35.8% decline**. Households *demanding* work fell by nearly **50%**, despite drought and rural distress.
- Wage: the **₹300/day floor** is below statutory minimum wages in several States and well below the **Satpathy Committee's ₹375/day** (at 2018 prices) benchmark for a national minimum wage.
- Digital architecture: **e-KYC, facial recognition, NMMS app-based and geo-tagged attendance**. Large-scale job-card deletions followed the e-KYC drive.
- Federal design: States were not adequately consulted on the 40% cost-share; critics invoke **Article 258** (Union entrusting functions to States, with the Union bearing the extra administrative cost) against uncompensated obligations.

### The other side / counter-argument

The column itself concedes the balance. A programme one month old cannot be judged on one month's data: transition to a new registration and e-KYC regime mechanically depresses recorded person-days, and a fall in *demand* registered is not the same as a fall in *need* — some of it is deletion of duplicate and ghost job cards, which is the stated point of the reform. The government's case is that 125 days on a cleaner beneficiary base beats 100 days on an inflated one, that biometric attendance attacks the muster-roll fraud MGNREGA was persistently criticised for, and that State co-financing gives States skin in the game and should improve asset quality. The honest examiner-facing position: **the design concerns are structural and can be assessed now; the outcome claims need at least a full agricultural cycle.**

### Mains value-addition points

- **"Digital compliance as a new form of exclusion error."** This is the sharpest transferable idea of the week — e-KYC and facial recognition raise Type-I errors (excluding the genuinely eligible) even as they reduce Type-II errors (leakage). Deploy it in DBT, Aadhaar, PDS, and welfare-delivery answers.
- A **rights-based guarantee is defined by who bears the residual obligation.** When the legal duty to provide work sits with States but the money is capped centrally, the "guarantee" becomes an aspiration. Good line for any question on justiciable socio-economic rights.
- **Article 258 + uncompensated mandates** is a precise constitutional hook for the fiscal-federalism dimension — note this pairs with, but does not repeat, the FC-16 material in the 6 August pack.
- Gendered incidence: women are a majority of employment-guarantee beneficiaries in most States, so a contraction in works is also a contraction in **women's paid work participation** — a ready GS1/GS3 crossover point.

## 3. Ease of doing business needs ease of justice — the Tribunal Reforms Bill, 2026

**Source:** The Indian Express, Opinion column, 12 August 2026 (with The Hindu's editorial taking the sceptical view the same week) · *GS2 (statutory, regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies; separation of powers; judiciary)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The **Tribunal Reforms Bill, 2026** creates a **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** to govern India's 16 tribunals under one framework — the reform the Supreme Court has been demanding since *Rojer Mathew* and the *Madras Bar Association* line of cases. The column's argument is institutional-economic: specialised adjudication in tax, company law, securities and environment is infrastructure for investment, so **ease of justice and ease of doing business are the same problem**. The Hindu's counter-editorial the same week argues the Bill retains enough executive influence over the NTC to leave the independence question unresolved.

### Key data & facts

- Constitutional basis: **Articles 323A** (administrative/service tribunals) and **323B** (taxation, industrial disputes, land reforms, elections), inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**.
- Rationalisation trajectory: **Finance Act, 2017** merged tribunals **26 → 19**; **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** took it **19 → 16**. Several 2021 provisions on appointments and tenure were **struck down by the Supreme Court** for infringing judicial independence.
- NTC composition as proposed: a **former Supreme Court judge or former High Court Chief Justice as chairperson**, plus **two judicial and two technical members**, with a permanent secretariat.
- The Bill **does not alter substantive jurisdiction** — each tribunal keeps the powers given by its parent statute; only administrative governance is unified.
- Passage: taken through Parliament on **10–11 August 2026**, reportedly **without debate** — the procedural point The Hindu presses hardest.

### The other side / counter-argument

Two counters run in opposite directions, which is what makes this a good answer topic. The *pro* case: fragmented, ministry-wise tribunal administration is exactly what produced inconsistent appointments, chronic vacancies and executive capture; a single commission chaired by a former senior judge is a structural improvement over the status quo, and the Court itself asked for an NTC. The *sceptical* case (The Hindu): a commission whose members are appointed with executive involvement, and which lacks ring-fenced financial autonomy, reproduces the dependency it was meant to cure — and passing it without discussion undercuts the deliberative legitimacy that institutional reform depends on. There is also the deeper doctrinal worry, unaddressed by the Bill: tribunalisation itself has diluted judicial review while tribunals remain under-resourced.

### Mains value-addition points

- **Vacancy is the real independence problem.** Formal independence provisions matter less than whether appointments happen on time; make this the analytical pivot rather than listing NTC features.
- Link tribunals to **Article 323A/B and the 42nd Amendment** and then to the **basic-structure limits on ousting judicial review** (*L. Chandra Kumar*) — this converts a current-affairs answer into a doctrinal one.
- The **"ease of justice ⇒ ease of doing business"** formulation is a reusable bridge sentence between GS2 (institutions) and GS3 (investment, growth) answers.
- Cross-reference: pairs neatly with the 16 July pack's court-pendency item — but the angle here is *institutional design*, not delay statistics.

## 4. AI's next test — reaching India's informal woman worker

**Source:** The Hindu, Op-ed, 12 August 2026 · *GS3 (science and technology — developments and their applications; awareness in IT) + GS1 (role of women) + GS2 (governance, e-governance)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

AI's distributional effect in India will be decided by who designs the systems, whose data trains them and which languages they support. Since the overwhelming majority of India's working women are in informal work, the op-ed argues that AI will either **compound existing gender gaps or become the fastest route to closing them** — and that the deciding variable is governance, specifically gender impact assessment, gender-responsive budgeting and AI literacy treated as public infrastructure.

### Key data & facts

- **~82% of working women in India are in informal employment** — agriculture, domestic services, home-based production, micro-enterprise.
- **76.9% of rural women are engaged in agriculture**, making agricultural AI a disproportionately female-facing technology.
- **Farmer.Chat**, deployed across **12 States in 2024**, reported improved quality of life among **61% of women users**, with **engagement two to three times higher among women than men** — evidence that adoption follows design for language, literacy and mobility constraints, not the reverse.
- Existing scaffolding to hang answers on: **India AI Governance Guidelines** (fairness and equity as core principles), **IndiaAI Mission**, **BHASHINI**, the **India AI Impact Summit 2026**, and delivery channels — **DAY-NRLM, DDU-GKY, Skill India, Mission Shakti's Sakhi network**.
- Safety side: **IT (Amendment) Rules, 2021** grievance mechanisms; proposed **MeitY** measures on labelling AI-generated synthetic content; **NCW** review of cyber laws.

### The other side / counter-argument

The op-ed is programmatic rather than adversarial, so the counter has to be supplied. First, a capability objection: gender impact assessments on every AI system touching employment, credit, welfare and safety is an enormous compliance load on a state that already struggles to audit far simpler schemes — and premature proceduralisation could slow deployment that would have helped women fastest in absolute terms. Second, a sequencing objection: the binding constraint on a woman agricultural worker may be land title, credit access or unpaid care burden rather than model design — AI literacy without those is a thin intervention. Third, on the evidence: a single pilot's engagement numbers are a weak base for a national governance architecture. The strongest version of the op-ed's reply is that inclusion is far cheaper to design in at procurement stage than to retrofit — which is a real argument about *when*, not *whether*.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"From AI access to AI empowerment"** — the consumer/producer distinction (women as users of AI services vs. designers, entrepreneurs and decision-makers within the ecosystem) is a clean two-part structure for any tech-and-inclusion answer.
- **Digital safety as an economic policy, not a cybersecurity policy**: deepfakes and doxxing raise the *cost of participation* in digital economic space. This is a genuinely fresh framing and pairs with this week's gender-equality op-ed (below).
- **Gender-responsive budgeting applied to technology spending** — ask of any AI allocation: which women benefit, which barrier is removed, how is the outcome measured. Reusable as an evaluation template.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS3 — Food security, PDS and inclusive growth**)

> **"India's food security architecture was built to deliver calories; its present challenge is to deliver nutrition."** In the light of the proposed shift from a flat household entitlement to a per-capita norm under the National Food Security (Amendment) Bill, 2026, **evaluate** whether the reform advances equity, and suggest how the Public Distribution System should be redesigned for the double burden of malnutrition. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

*Maps to: GS3 — issues of buffer stocks and food security; Public Distribution System: objectives, functioning, limitations, revamping; inclusive growth. Directive **"evaluate"** is fresh — recent weeks used examine / critically examine / discuss / elucidate / critically analyse. What "evaluate" demands: apply an explicit standard, judge the reform against it, and deliver a verdict. Unlike "critically analyse", you are expected to **land clearly on one side** — a balanced non-answer loses marks here. State your criterion (equity + nutritional adequacy) in the introduction so the examiner can see the yardstick before the judgment.*

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Open by fixing the evaluative standard, not by describing the Bill.** One line on the NFSA 2013 framework, then state the two tests you will apply: does the amendment improve **distributional equity** among the poorest (AAY) households, and does it move the system toward **nutritional adequacy** as defined by ICMR-NIN? Note in the same breath that affordability of a healthy diet has improved sharply — **52% of households unable to afford it in 2011-12 down to 25% in 2023-24** — which reframes the problem from access-to-grain to access-to-diet. This paragraph is what converts a description into an evaluation.

2. **Verdict on equity: the reform fails its own stated test — show it arithmetically.** The flat 35 kg AAY entitlement is genuinely inequitable at the top of the size distribution (an eight-member household gets **4.4 kg per head**, below the Priority Household's 5 kg). But the proposed 7 kg-per-head-capped-at-35 kg fixes this by **subtraction**: households of **1–4 members lose 20–80%**, households of five-plus are unchanged, and **no household gains**. **Tamil Nadu — 84.5% of AAY households below five members, an estimated 35.6% fall in allocation** — is the decisive illustration. Add the equity-within-equity point: small AAY households are disproportionately the elderly living alone, widows and persons with disability, i.e. the reform is regressive precisely where vulnerability is highest.

3. **Concede the fiscal and horizontal-equity case before rejecting it.** Per-capita entitlement is the cleaner principle in the abstract, and the 35 kg cap contains fiscal exposure. The failure is in **design and sequencing**, not intent: a no-loss guarantee preserving the existing 35 kg floor, with per-capita top-ups for larger households examined separately on consumption evidence and transparent costing, would deliver the equity gain without the cut. Recommend this explicitly — an evaluation that only criticises is half an answer.

4. **Widen to the second test — nutrition — and marshal the double-burden evidence.** **NFHS-6**: stunting down **35.5% → 29.3%** but wasting and underweight nearly static; only **~15% of children 6–23 months** get a minimally adequate diet. Simultaneously **ICMR-INDIAB (2021): 101 million with diabetes, 136 million with prediabetes**, with **carbohydrates at 62.3% of daily energy** and the highest-carbohydrate group showing **30% higher odds of new type-2 diabetes** — and, critically, **substituting millets or whole wheat for refined cereals did not help while the carbohydrate share stayed high**. Against **ICMR-NIN's 45% cereal-energy ceiling**, a cereal-centric PDS is now working against one arm of the burden while addressing the other. This is where most answers will stop at "add millets" — the evidence says that is insufficient.

5. **Close with a redesign and a coverage correction — concrete, costed, sequenced.** (i) Diversify the PDS basket toward pulses, healthy edible oils and locally appropriate grains, **separately budgeted**, backed by MSP procurement for pulses and oilseeds, and phased through State pilots before national rollout; (ii) route the protein and micronutrient gap through **Anganwadi services and PM POSHAN** (eggs, milk or suitable alternatives) rather than through the ration shop alone; (iii) fix coverage — the **81.35-crore ceiling is anchored to Census 2011 and now covers only 55.6% of an estimated 146.4-crore population**, so recalculate on **Census 2027** with updated deprivation criteria; (iv) use the delivery infrastructure that already exists — **ePoS at ~5.51 lakh fair price shops, near-universal ONORC** — while guaranteeing offline fallback and doorstep access so **authentication failure never becomes denial of entitlement**. Final verdict sentence: the amendment as drafted trades a real equity gain for a certain welfare loss, and reform should be sequenced — protect first, diversify second, re-target on new Census data third.

## Next step

Write this in 250 words, hand-timed, then run the **`mains-answer-feedback`** task on it. Three things to watch on this question: (i) **"evaluate" requires a verdict** — if your conclusion is "the reform has both merits and demerits", you have written a "discuss" answer to an "evaluate" question; (ii) the arithmetic in point 2 is the load-bearing evidence, so give it a full sentence with numbers rather than a vague "smaller households may lose out"; and (iii) don't let the nutrition half become an afterthought — the question has two clauses and examiners split marks across both, so budget roughly 120 words to equity and 100 to redesign.
