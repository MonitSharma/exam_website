## 1. Urban fires and the governance vacuum — the unfinished 74th Amendment

**Source:** The Hindu, Opinion (~29 July 2026) · *GS2 (governance — devolution of powers to local levels, transparency & accountability, statutory bodies) + GS4 (probity in governance) + GS3 (disaster management)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The recurring urban fire tragedies of 2026 are not a failure of law but of governance. India already has the National Building Code and Model Building Bye-Laws; what collapses is enforcement, because urban functions are fragmented across six or more State-controlled agencies and building inspectors are State cadre officers accountable upward, not to the elected municipality. The editorial traces this back to the incomplete devolution promised by the 74th Constitutional Amendment.

### Key data & facts

- **74th CAA, 1992** inserted Part IX-A (Arts. 243P–243ZG). **Art. 243Q** — three types of municipalities; **Art. 243W + 12th Schedule** — 18 functions that States *may* devolve (the permissive "may" is the whole problem; most States have devolved fewer than half); **Art. 243X** — States *may* authorise ULBs to tax.
- **Fire services are a State subject** (7th Schedule). The **National Building Code (BIS, 2016)** and **Model Building Bye-Laws (MoHUA, 2016)** are templates — State adoption is discretionary and uneven.
- **~13,000–15,000 fire deaths a year** in India; Delhi alone recorded 39 fire deaths in Q1 2026, before the summer peak.
- The 2026 cluster: **Lucknow, Aliganj (22 June)** — 15 deaths, commercial unit in a residential zone with no fire clearance; a 2016 demolition notice had been withdrawn within two months. **Delhi, Malviya Nagar (3 June)** — B&B, illegal construction, no emergency exit. **Bhiwadi, Rajasthan (Feb 2026)** — garment plant concealing a firecracker unit ("change of use" violation). **Virudhunagar, TN (2026)** — illegal cracker unit operating on a Sunday.
- **Accountability loop problem:** post-disaster SITs are typically IAS/IPS officers reporting to senior IAS officers — the same service hierarchy that runs the Development Authorities implicated.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Fragmentation is not purely a devolution failure.** Metropolitan services (water, trunk roads, land use) have genuine scale economies that sit above ward or even municipal level; full devolution of all 18 functions to weak, small ULBs could worsen delivery, not improve it. The real fix may be *metropolitan* governance (Art. 243ZE MPCs, which almost no State has operationalised) rather than more municipal autonomy.
- **Capacity precedes autonomy.** Many ULBs cannot collect property tax, prepare a spatial plan, or retain a qualified town planner. Devolving fire NOC powers to such bodies risks localising rather than removing patronage.
- **Enforcement failure is also a demand-side problem.** A large share of urban India lives and works in informal or non-conforming structures; strict enforcement means mass sealing and livelihood loss, which is why political actors intervene. Any reform proposal that ignores the regularisation question is incomplete.

### Mains value-addition points

- Name the pathology precisely: **"functions without funds and functionaries"** — the 74th CAA devolved visibility to Mayors and power to Chief Ministers. Use *Art. 243W's "may"* as the one-word constitutional design flaw.
- **AMRUT and Smart Cities Mission (2015–)** channelled money *around* ULBs rather than through them — a transferable line for any GS2 answer on why scheme-led urbanism doesn't build institutions.
- The **withdrawn demolition notice** (Lucknow, 2016) is a compact, citable illustration that selective non-enforcement signals patronage protection and manufactures future violations — usable in GS2 (accountability), GS3 (disaster risk reduction) and GS4 (probity) alike.
- Reform package worth memorising: mandatory devolution of all 18 functions → a **dedicated municipal cadre** accountable to the ULB → ring-fenced own-revenue (property tax reform) → an independent urban ombudsman able to prosecute officials who withdraw lawful notices.

## 2. The RBI's exchange-rate dilemma — managed float meets the Impossible Trinity

**Source:** The Hindu, Opinion (~29 July 2026) · *GS3 (economy — monetary policy, external sector, capital flows) + GS2 (effect of developed-country policies on India)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

The rupee is under simultaneous pressure from a trade shock, reserve depletion and capital outflows towards a high-yield US. The op-ed argues the RBI's toolkit — spot intervention, forwards, FCNR(B), NDF restrictions — is increasingly reactive and insufficient, and that the underlying question is architectural: whether India should deepen capital account convertibility or entrench the managed float.

### Key data & facts

- India runs a **managed float since 1993**: pre-1991 fixed/overvalued rupee → **LERMS (1992)** dual rate (40% official, 60% market) → unification 1993. The 1991 crisis (reserves ≈ three weeks of import cover) forced an ~18–19% devaluation.
- Legal frame: **FEMA, 1999** (replaced FERA, 1973; forex violations civil, not criminal) + **RBI Act, 1934 (s. 45W)**.
- **Full current account convertibility; only partial capital account convertibility** — the Tarapore Committees (1997, 2006) recommended full CAC; both were deferred.
- **Trade ≈ 45.8% of GDP** — high openness means external shocks transmit fast through both trade and financial channels.
- **RBI has been a net dollar seller since October 2023**; reserves were drawn down in May–June 2026. **FPIs bought ₹21,652 crore under the Fully Accessible Route (FAR) in June 2026.**
- Precedent: **Taper Tantrum (2013)** — rupee ~₹54 → ~₹68/USD; RBI used FCNR(B) swaps raising ~$4 bn; the 2016 maturities then created a second outflow wave. Any 2026-vintage FCNR(B) carries the same deferred risk into **2028–29**.
- The binding constraint is the **Mundell–Fleming Impossible Trinity**: fixed rate + free capital mobility + monetary autonomy — pick two. India picks monetary autonomy + partial stability, paying with capital controls.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Depreciation is not automatically a failure.** With a structural CAD, a weaker rupee is the adjustment mechanism — it restores export competitiveness. Defending a level burns reserves to subsidise importers and encourages unhedged foreign borrowing.
- **The peer comparison cuts both ways.** The op-ed notes the rupiah, yen and won fell more while the rupee held — but that relative stability is bought by intervention, and an over-managed rate can leave the REER overvalued, hurting exactly the exports India needs.
- **The NDF restriction trade-off is contested:** curbing offshore speculation reduces market depth and reads to FPIs as regulatory unpredictability — a credibility cost that may exceed the speculative gain.
- **Korea's counter-example:** deep capital integration *with* exchange-rate stability, achieved via current account surpluses, deep bond markets and institutional quality — i.e., the fix is structural, not monetary, and lies outside the RBI's remit.

### Mains value-addition points

- Deploy the **Impossible Trinity as the organising frame** for any external-sector answer — then place India explicitly on it ("monetary autonomy + managed stability, financed by partial CAC").
- **"Reserves are a stock, the CAD is a flow"** — intervention buys time, not solvency; a one-line critique that works in GS3 macro answers.
- The **FCNR(B) maturity trap (2013 → 2016; 2026 → 2028–29)** is a crisp example of *policy that defers rather than solves* — reusable across GS3 fiscal/monetary answers.
- Oil (~85% import dependence) makes depreciation **directly inflationary and fiscally costly** — the link that ties external sector to inflation-targeting to the fiscal deficit in one sentence.

## 3. Strategic autonomy — neither a China reset nor US dependence

**Source:** Opinion by **Shashi Tharoor, MP** (~27 July 2026) · *GS2 (IR — India's foreign policy, bilateral relations with China and the US, effect of developed-country policies) + GS3 (economy — supply-chain security)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

Against a business-lobby push to reset China policy in response to Washington's transactionalism, the author argues India's post-Galwan restrictions are diplomatic leverage, not mere reactions, and should not be traded away without reciprocal Chinese movement on the border. The prescription is a third path: strategic patience plus internal economic fortification — supply-chain resilience and diversification rather than bloc dependence.

### Key data & facts

- **LAC ≈ 3,488 km**, undemarcated, three sectors (Western/Middle/Eastern); India recognises the McMahon Line in the east, China does not.
- Friction record cited: **Depsang (2013)** ~19 km intrusion, 21-day standoff, PP 10–13 still contested; **Chumar (2014)** during Xi's state visit; **Doklam (2017)** 73-day standoff near the Siliguri Corridor; **Galwan (2020)** 20 Indian soldiers killed — deadliest since 1967.
- Economic asymmetry: **China's trade surplus with India exceeds $100 bn**; India imports **~65–70% of its APIs** from China.
- Post-Galwan instruments: **Press Note 3 (2020)** — government approval for FDI from land-border-sharing countries; **300+ Chinese apps banned** under s. 69A, IT Act; Huawei/ZTE excluded from telecom.
- Other listed irritants: renaming of features in Arunachal Pradesh ("Zangnan"), stapled visas for J&K and Arunachal residents, UNSC vetoes shielding Pakistan-based terror listings, blocking India's NSG entry, **CPEC (~$62 bn)** through PoK.
- The piece alleges Chinese real-time satellite intelligence support to Pakistan during **Operation Sindoor (May 2025)** — flag this as an *allegation made in the op-ed*, not established fact.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Estrangement has a bill.** Restricting Chinese capital goods and inputs has raised costs and slowed electronics and solar manufacturing — the very sectors self-reliance depends on. India cannot substitute its way out of Chinese inputs while simultaneously scaling manufacturing on PLI timelines.
- **PLI's record is mixed.** API and solar localisation has been slower and costlier than projected; "build domestic capacity" is a prescription, not a policy.
- **Leverage is asymmetric.** App bans and FDI screening cost China far less than the dependency costs India — so calling them "leverage" may overstate their bargaining value.
- **De-risking ≠ decoupling.** The EU/Japan approach — targeted controls on critical sectors plus normal trade elsewhere — is a live alternative to blanket restriction.

### Mains value-addition points

- Frame the doctrine as **"multi-alignment, not equidistance"** — an active posture requiring capability, not a passive refusal to choose. Strong opening line for any GS2 IR answer.
- **"Salami-slicing"** — incremental, individually deniable territorial changes — is the precise term for the Depsang→Galwan pattern; use it once, define it in six words.
- The **"economic kill-switch"** framing (APIs, rare earths, electronics) links GS2 IR to GS3 supply-chain security in one move.
- Balance requirement: any answer must concede the **input-cost trade-off**, otherwise it reads as advocacy rather than analysis.

## 4. For antibiotics, the right dose is restraint — India's AMR crisis

**Source:** The Hindu, Editorial (~27 July 2026), built on a *Lancet Public Health* study · *GS3 (science & technology, health) + GS2 (governance — health policy, regulatory enforcement)*

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India consumes far more antibiotics than the optimal range, and consumes the *wrong* ones — heavy on broad-spectrum "Watch" drugs, light on first-line "Access" drugs. The editorial argues the fix is not another action plan but enforcement and diagnostics: prescription-only rules that actually bind, elimination of irrational fixed-dose combinations, and lab capacity so prescribers stop guessing.

### Key data & facts

- **Lancet Public Health study:** India at **18.3 defined daily doses (DDD) per 1,000 people/day** against an optimal **9.9–14.7**. **Watch** antibiotics at **9.3 DDD** vs a recommended **6** (~55% excess); **Access** at only **4.5 DDD**.
- **WHO AWaRe classification (2017, updated 2021):** Access / Watch / Reserve — **Access should be ≥60%** of total consumption. India's pattern is structurally inverted.
- Global burden: the **Lancet (2022)** attributed **1.27 million deaths in 2019** directly to AMR.
- Regulation: **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**; **Schedule H1 (2013)** made fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems prescription-only with record-keeping — yet OTC sale persists (an enforcement, not a drafting, failure). The **Kokate Committee (2014)** flagged 340+ irrational FDCs; *Pfizer Ltd v. Union of India (2017)* dealt with the legality of FDC bans.
- **One Health:** **colistin** — a Reserve antibiotic — was used as a livestock growth promoter until a **2019 ban**; pharma effluent (e.g. the Hyderabad bulk-drug cluster) seeds environmental AMR.
- Policy: **NAP-AMR 2017–21 → NAP-AMR 2.0 (2025–2029)**; Antimicrobial Stewardship Programmes now mandatory in NABH-accredited hospitals; **Red Line Campaign** against self-medication.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Under-treatment kills too.** In a country with a large infectious-disease burden and thin diagnostic access, restricting empirical antibiotic use without first building labs converts an AMR problem into a mortality problem — the "access–excess" paradox. Globally, more people still die of *lack* of antibiotics than of resistant infections.
- **Prescriber blame is misplaced.** A physician with a three-minute consultation and no culture-and-sensitivity facility is behaving rationally in prescribing broad-spectrum. Enforcement against pharmacists and doctors without fixing diagnostics shifts cost to the weakest link.
- **DDD comparisons have limits.** Consumption metrics don't adjust for disease burden; a high-infection-burden country will legitimately consume more than a low-burden one, so 18.3 vs 14.7 is a flag, not a verdict.

### Mains value-addition points

- **"India over-uses and under-provides simultaneously"** — the inverted AWaRe pyramid is the single sharpest line for any AMR answer; it reframes AMR as a *health-systems* failure, not a behaviour failure.
- Use **One Health** as the structural frame (human–animal–environment), then instantiate it with **colistin in livestock** and **pharma effluent** — two concrete, citable examples.
- **Schedule H1 as an enforcement-gap case study** pairs with the urban-fires pick above: same pathology, different sector — good law, discretionary enforcement. This cross-link is exactly the kind of synthesis that lifts a GS2 governance answer.
- Diagnostics-first sequencing (**rapid tests and C&S at PHC/CHC level before prescription crackdowns**) is the balanced conclusion an examiner rewards.

## GS Mains practice question (rotated paper: **GS4 — Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude**)

> **Q.** A municipal building inspector documents clear fire-safety and land-use violations in a commercial establishment and issues a lawful demolition-cum-sealing notice. Within weeks, senior officials — acting on political instruction — direct that the notice be withdrawn. The inspector complies. Two years later, a fire in that building kills fifteen people.
>
> In such situations, non-enforcement is rarely a single dishonest act; it is a routine administrative decision whose consequences surface much later. **Elucidate** the ethical obligations of a public servant confronted with pressure to withdraw a lawful enforcement action, and examine what institutional design would make the ethical choice the survivable one. **(10 marks · 150 words)**

*Maps to: GS4 — probity in governance, accountability and ethical governance, work culture, moral and political attitudes; with a GS2 (governance, local bodies) foundation. Directive **"elucidate"** is deliberately fresh — recent weeks used examine / critically examine / discuss. Note the two-part demand: **individual obligation** AND **institutional design** — a single-part answer will lose half the marks. Anchored to the Lucknow Aliganj facts reported in the 29 July editorial.*

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Frame the dilemma correctly, in one line.** This is not a bribe case. It is the harder category: a *lawful-looking instruction* to exercise discretion in a particular direction. The ethical question is whether obedience to hierarchy can discharge a duty owed to the public. Name the conflict: **legal-rational authority vs. constitutional duty**; **loyalty to superiors vs. accountability to citizens**.

2. **State the obligations, in order of priority.** (i) *Fidelity to law over instruction* — Art. 14 forbids arbitrary selective non-enforcement; a withdrawal without recorded reasons is legally void, not merely unethical. (ii) *Written record* — insist that the direction be issued in writing with reasons (the single most powerful ethical instrument available to a subordinate officer, and the one that shifts accountability upward). (iii) *Non-maleficence and foreseeability* — safety violations create a **latent risk to identifiable third parties**, which distinguishes this from ordinary procedural discretion. (iv) *Escalation before resignation* — vigilance/ombudsman channels, then dissent on record; theatrical resignation abandons the beneficiaries.

3. **Concede the constraint honestly** — this is what separates a Good answer from an average one. The inspector faces transfer, adverse ACRs, and isolation; **moral courage is not costless and cannot be the primary safeguard of public safety**. An ethics answer that only exhorts individual virtue is analytically weak: it makes system failure a character failure. *(Cite the "closed-loop inquiry" problem — SITs of IAS officers examining IAS-run authorities — as evidence that individual courage is unsupported by the accountability architecture.)*

4. **Institutional design that makes integrity survivable.** (i) **Mandatory written directions** with reasons for any reversal of an enforcement notice, plus automatic logging in a public register. (ii) **Municipal cadre with ULB accountability**, ending the upward-only career dependence of inspectors on State political executives (links straight to the 74th CAA devolution gap). (iii) **Independent urban ombudsman** empowered to prosecute reversal of lawful notices. (iv) **Whistleblower protection with an insulated transfer/posting board.** (v) **Technology as a de-discretioning tool** — GIS/satellite anomaly detection makes violations visible by default, so *not* acting becomes the conspicuous act. Note the honest caveat: the constraint on such technology is political cost, not capability.

5. **Conclude with the general principle.** The test of an ethical administration is not whether it produces heroes but whether it needs them. Institutions should be designed so that the honest choice is the low-cost default and the dishonest one leaves a trace — *foreseeable, preventable harm to the public is the point at which deference to hierarchy stops being a virtue.*

**Useful thinkers/anchors (use one, not four):** Weber on the tension between the *ethic of responsibility* and the *ethic of ultimate ends*; the **2nd ARC (4th Report, "Ethics in Governance")** on institutionalising integrity rather than moralising about it; Kant on treating persons as ends when a foreseeable risk is knowingly tolerated.
