## 1. India's China+1 chance will be decided inside its States, not in Delhi

**Source:** The Hindu, op-ed, ~15 October 2026 — **P.S. Srinivas and Anisha Sircar**, East Asian Institute, National University of Singapore. The op-ed draws on the authors' EAI Special Report (September 2026), *India, China, and the China+1 Strategies of Global Firms*, built on roughly **50 interviews** with executives and policymakers.

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Indian Economy** (primary: changes in industrial policy and their effects on industrial growth, investment models, effects of liberalisation, employment); **GS2 — Polity & Governance** (federal structure, competitive and cooperative federalism, State capacity); GS2 — IR (effect of policies of developed countries on India's interests) as a secondary hook.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

A global firm relocating out of China does not choose "India" — it chooses a **State**, and with it that State's land, power, approvals, workforce and supplier base. On the authors' evidence, **Tamil Nadu** won investment through a manufacturing ecosystem built over decades, while **Karnataka** built outward from a talent and R&D base; but both still show **thin backward linkages and low domestic value addition**, because what has arrived so far is largely final assembly.

### Key data & facts

- **FDI equity share, October 2019 – March 2026:** Maharashtra **31%**, Karnataka **21%**, Gujarat **15%**, Delhi **13%**, Tamil Nadu about **6%**.
- **Crucial caveat the authors themselves flag:** FDI is recorded at the **head-office location, not the factory location**. Maharashtra's and Delhi's shares are therefore partly a registration artefact, and Tamil Nadu's 6% badly understates what is physically built there. *This caveat is itself the examinable point — it is a lesson in reading administrative data.*
- **Karnataka:** Foxconn's **Devanahalli** campus, about **300 acres**, targeting roughly **50,000 jobs**.
- **Tamil Nadu:** Tata Electronics plants at **Hosur**; Karnataka's at **Narsapura**.
- **The localisation gap, concretely:** one firm reached about **$600 million** of a **$1 billion** local-sourcing target after **2–3 years** — progress, but a shortfall that shows how slowly supplier depth accumulates.
- **Central policy frame:** the third tranche of the **Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (ECMS)**, approved in **January 2026**, cleared **22 proposals** worth **₹41,863 crore** with about **33,791 direct jobs** across **eight States**. The **PLI for Large-Scale Electronics Manufacturing ended on 31 March 2026** — so component-stage support (ECMS) is now carrying the load that assembly-stage support (PLI) used to.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **The national frame did the heavy lifting.** Assembly-scale PLI, tariff restructuring and trade policy created the entry point; States then competed *inside* that frame. On this reading State capacity is a second-order variable, not the decisive one.
- **Low domestic value addition at entry is normal, not a verdict.** Vietnam and Mexico also began with assembly and deepened over 10–15 years. Judging India's ecosystem by DVA two or three years in may be premature.
- **The FDI-share numbers partly refute the States-matter claim** as presented: if location data is this noisy, State-wise comparison of "success" needs physical-output and employment measures, not FDI shares.
- **Competitive federalism has a cost side** — a subsidy race between States can transfer rents to firms without raising aggregate investment.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Firms do not locate in countries; they locate in districts."** The single most portable line from the week. It converts an industrial-policy answer from a list of central schemes into an argument about State capacity — land assembly, single-window approvals, assured power, industrial training and supplier parks.
2. **Distinguish the two State pathways explicitly.** Tamil Nadu = *ecosystem-first* (decades of auto and electronics clustering, supplier depth, industrial labour); Karnataka = *talent-first* (R&D, engineering base, later pulling in hardware). Naming two distinct routes to the same outcome is worth more than naming ten schemes.
3. **Assembly is not an ecosystem.** The examinable distinction is between **gross output/exports** and **domestic value addition**. ECMS exists precisely because the component layer is the missing one; the ending of large-scale-electronics PLI on 31 March 2026 is the policy pivot from assembly to components.
4. **The data-literacy point.** "FDI is booked at the head office" is a ready example of why administrative statistics need to be read against their collection method — reusable in GS3 answers on statistical governance and in essay introductions.

## 2. The cost of being called "unskilled"

**Source:** The Hindu, op-ed, ~15 October 2026. *(The author's name was not available in the summaries accessible this week — attribute the argument to "a recent Hindu op-ed on skill classification" rather than to a named writer.)*

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Indian Economy** (primary: growth and employment, inclusive growth, human resource development, labour reforms); **GS2 — Social Justice** (welfare and protection of vulnerable sections, mechanisms and laws for the labour force); **GS1 — Indian Society** (internal migration, urbanisation); GS4 overlap on dignity of labour.

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

"Unskilled" is treated as a neutral description of a worker's ability, but it functions as an **administrative classification that the system actively sustains**. Once applied, it legitimises a lower statutory wage, justifies withholding training and benefits, and correlates with worse safety outcomes — so the label becomes a cause of low productivity rather than a report of it.

### Key data & facts

- **Safe in India Foundation (auto-sector injuries, 2024):** workers earning **under ₹8,000 a month** lost **2.27 fingers per accident**, against **1.54** for those earning **over ₹20,000**. The severity of an industrial injury tracks the wage band.
- **IIMAD (Thiruvananthapuram) estimate:** roughly **40% more wage theft** reported among "unskilled"-classified migrant workers in Kerala's **construction and hospitality** sectors.
- **The wage wedge is statutory, not market-set:** Uttar Pradesh's interim monthly minimum rates run **₹13,690 (unskilled)** against **₹16,868 (skilled)** — a gap created by the classification itself.
- **The four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025**, consolidating **29 central labour laws**.
- **Constitutional anchors:** **Article 39(d)** (equal pay for equal work), **Article 42** (just and humane conditions of work), **Article 43** (living wage).
- **The existing remedy already exists on paper:** the **NSQF**'s provision for **Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL)** — certifying skills a worker already has but was never credentialed for.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **Classification is how minimum-wage floors are administered at all.** Without skill categories there is no enforceable statutory floor, and the realistic alternative to a low floor is often no floor.
- **Part of the differential is genuine productivity.** Certified welders, electricians and machine operators are measurably more productive; collapsing the categories would compress the wage incentive to acquire skills.
- **Causation runs the other way, at least partly.** Low-wage workplaces are disproportionately small, informal and under-capitalised; the injury correlation may reflect **firm size, machine age and safety capital** rather than the label attached to the worker. *This is the strongest objection and a good answer should concede it before resolving it.*
- **Employers' side:** mandatory reclassification raises wage bills in precisely the labour-intensive sectors India wants to expand, with employment effects at the margin.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"A classification that produces the condition it claims to describe."** This is the analytically sharp framing: a label that denies training and then cites the absence of training as proof of unskilledness. Reusable well beyond labour — in GS1 on social stratification and in GS2 on how categorisation in welfare administration creates entitlement hierarchies.
2. **Pair this directly with piece 1.** Domestic value addition cannot deepen on a workforce the system classifies as unskilled and therefore does not train. *The China+1 question and the labour-classification question are the same question asked from two ends* — that sentence alone will lift an industrial-policy answer.
3. **Lead with RPL as the implementable reform.** It costs little, needs no new statute, and converts existing capability into a wage claim — a far better answer than "more skilling" in the abstract.
4. **Safety is the measurable consequence, so use it.** The 2.27-vs-1.54 fingers figure makes an abstract classification argument concrete. One such number beats a paragraph of adjectives.

## 3. A decade of mandatory CSR — from rupees spent to outcomes achieved

**Source:** The Indian Express, op-ed, **14 October 2026** — **Purvi Mehta**.

**GS mapping:** **GS3 — Indian Economy** (primary: mobilisation of resources, inclusive growth and issues arising from it, government budgeting); **GS4 — Ethics** (corporate governance, probity, corporate social responsibility); GS2 — Governance (accountability, outcome measurement).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India's mandatory CSR regime has successfully mobilised money, but it **audits inputs**: the 2% rule asks whether a company spent, not whether anything changed. After a decade, the author argues the regime should be re-engineered around **measured outcomes**, **pooled funding** and firms' **core competencies** rather than their cheque books.

### Key data & facts

- **Cumulative CSR mobilisation since 2014:** about **₹2.6 lakh crore**. **2024-25 spending: ₹40,794 crore.**
- **Fragmentation, quantified:** about **29,500 companies** ran roughly **72,000 projects** — an average of about **₹57 lakh per project**.
- **Geographic concentration:** **Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka and Delhi** together received about **47%** of spending — i.e. CSR flows toward where companies are headquartered, not where need is greatest.
- **Statutory basis:** **Section 135 and Schedule VII, Companies Act 2013**; obligation is **2% of average net profits of the preceding three financial years** for companies crossing the net-worth/turnover/profit thresholds.
- **Impact assessment is already mandatory** for companies with an average CSR obligation of **₹10 crore or more**, for projects of **₹1 crore or more** — so the outcome machinery partly exists and is under-used.
- **Ready-made frameworks the author points to:** the **Aspirational Districts and Aspirational Blocks** programmes (for geography and indicators) and the **Social Stock Exchange** (for outcome reporting).

### The other side / counter-argument

- **A mandated, non-tax-deductible 2% is functionally an earmarked levy on profits** — if the State wants outcome-directed social spending, the honest instrument is taxation and the Budget, with parliamentary accountability.
- **Steering CSR to chosen districts erodes the "responsibility" in corporate social responsibility** and converts a voluntary ethic into quasi-fiscal direction.
- **Rigorous impact evaluation is expensive.** On a ₹57-lakh average project, a credible evaluation could consume a large share of the project budget — the measurement would crowd out the intervention.
- **Scale check:** at roughly **₹41,000 crore a year**, CSR is a rounding error against Union health and education outlays. It is a supplement, not a substitute — so loading it with national-outcome expectations is a category error.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Input audit versus outcome audit"** is the transferable idea of the week. The same critique applies to scheme evaluation generally — utilisation certificates, physical-target reporting, expenditure-based performance review. Carry it into GS2 answers on governance and GS3 on budgeting.
2. **The ₹57-lakh average is the killer statistic** — it shows fragmentation without any adjectives. Pair it with the 47% four-State concentration to make the twin critique (too scattered to measure, too clustered to be equitable) in two sentences.
3. **The GS4 framing matters:** mandatory CSR is a live case in **whether a legally compelled ethical act remains an ethical act**. Use it in GS4 on corporate governance — a compelled 2% can crowd out the voluntary ethic it was meant to institutionalise (the classic crowding-out-of-intrinsic-motivation argument), which is precisely why outcome accountability, not a larger mandate, is the author's prescription.
4. **Concede the scale point before you conclude.** An answer that treats CSR as a development instrument without noting it is under ₹41,000 crore a year reads as innumerate.

## 4. Overtreatment and overmedication — India's "dual pathology"

**Source:** The Hindu, op-ed, **14 October 2026** — **Dr Chandrakant Lahariya**, physician and public-health specialist.

**GS mapping:** **GS2 — Governance / Social Justice** (primary: issues relating to the development and management of the health sector, regulation of professions, statutory bodies); **GS4 — Ethics** (professional ethics, conflict of interest, informed consent, probity in service delivery); GS3 — Economy (out-of-pocket expenditure, human capital).

### Core argument (2–3 lines)

India's health system carries a **dual pathology**: those who can pay receive **too much** care, while those who cannot receive **too little** — and both failures stem from the same incentive structure. Medical progress, the author argues, now depends as much on knowing **when to hold back** as on expanding what is available.

### Key data & facts

- **Polypharmacy:** about **49%** of older adults affected (2021 meta-analysis); about **one in three** older adults take **five or more** medicines (2025 study); **28.8%** of cases involved **potentially inappropriate medicines**.
- **Caesarean sections:** about **47.4%** in **private** facilities against **14.3%** in **public** ones (**NFHS-5**), against a **WHO** population reference of about **10%**. The public–private gap is the evidence that the driver is incentive, not clinical need.
- **Demography:** the elderly share of India's population is projected to exceed **20% by 2050** (**UNFPA, 2023**) — which turns polypharmacy from a niche concern into a system-level cost.
- **Policy and legal anchors:** **Article 47** (State duty to raise nutrition and public health), the **Clinical Establishments Act, 2010**, the **National Health Policy 2017**.
- **Five drivers the author identifies:** indiscriminate testing; fragmented specialist-led care; marketisation and **fee-for-service** incentives; patient expectations; and **defensive medicine**.
- **Remedies proposed:** restore the **family physician as gatekeeper**; add **review and stop dates** to prescriptions; adopt **low-value-care lists**; equip patients with a short set of questions for shared decision-making; and shift payment toward **outcomes or capitation**.

### The other side / counter-argument

- **India's primary-care workforce is thin**, especially in cities — the gatekeeper model presumes a cadre of family physicians that largely does not exist in urban India.
- **Most care is delivered by a loosely regulated private sector**, so a gatekeeping or payment reform has no obvious lever; the Clinical Establishments Act is not adopted uniformly across States.
- **The real and serious risk:** a campaign against low-value care can be captured by payers and used to **ration legitimate care**, worsening undertreatment among the poor — the opposite of the author's intent.
- **Defensive medicine is a rational response to liability**, so the behaviour will not change until the medico-legal environment does.

### Mains value-addition points

1. **"Too much care for those who can pay, too little for those who cannot"** — one sentence that captures Indian health inequity better than any expenditure statistic, and is usable in GS2 on health governance, GS3 on out-of-pocket expenditure, and the essay paper.
2. **The 47.4% vs 14.3% C-section gap is the week's single best health statistic**, because it isolates the *incentive* variable: the same population, the same clinical indications, radically different rates by ownership of the facility. Use it whenever an answer needs to show that provider payment design drives clinical behaviour.
3. **Payment design is the policy lever, not exhortation.** Fee-for-service rewards volume; capitation and outcome-based payment reward restraint. Naming the mechanism distinguishes a serious answer from one that asks for "better ethics".
4. **GS4 reading:** this is a textbook **conflict of interest** case — the provider both advises on and profits from the volume of the intervention, with the patient structurally unable to assess necessity. Pair it with **informed consent**: consent is meaningless where the information asymmetry is this large. Note that mental health (covered in the 3 September pack) was an *access* problem; this is its mirror image, an *excess* problem in the same system.

## GS Mains practice question — rotated paper: **GS3 (Indian Economy — industrial policy & employment)**

> *Paper rotation check: the last four packs ran GS4 (ethics, 3 Sep), GS2 (IR, 10 Sep), GS3 (internal security, 17 Sep) and GS1 (society, 5 Oct). GS3 returns, but to the **economy** limb — industrial policy, manufacturing and employment have not carried the weekly question in this run. Directive rotation: recent weeks used* examine, critically examine, assess, evaluate, discuss, elucidate, critically analyse, analyse, comment *— so this week uses* **critically evaluate**.

**Question (15 marks, 250 words):**

> India's recent manufacturing investment has clustered in a few States and largely at the final-assembly stage, even as a substantial part of its industrial workforce continues to be classified as "unskilled". **Critically evaluate** the proposition that the success of India's "China+1" opportunity will depend less on central incentives than on State-level industrial ecosystems and the quality of industrial employment.

### Model-answer skeleton (5 points)

1. **Set the unit of decision in the introduction.** A relocating firm does not choose a country; it chooses a site — land, assured power, approvals, a trained workforce and nearby suppliers are all State-level goods. State one sentence of context: the ending of large-scale-electronics PLI on **31 March 2026** and the shift to component-stage support via **ECMS** (third tranche, January 2026: **22 proposals, ₹41,863 crore, ~33,791 direct jobs, eight States**) mean the next phase of investment is decided by exactly the capabilities States supply.

2. **Argue the "States matter" limb with the two pathways, not a list.** Tamil Nadu's **ecosystem-first** route (decades of auto–electronics clustering and supplier depth) versus Karnataka's **talent-first** route (engineering and R&D base pulling hardware in afterwards) — e.g. Foxconn's **300-acre Devanahalli** campus targeting about **50,000** jobs. Then deploy the data-literacy point as evidence of your own care: the FDI-share table (Maharashtra **31%**, Karnataka **21%**, Tamil Nadu about **6%**) records FDI at the **head office, not the factory**, so FDI share is the wrong measure of where manufacturing actually lands.

3. **Establish the depth problem — assembly is not an ecosystem.** Thin backward linkages and low **domestic value addition** are the binding constraint; one firm reaching about **$600 million** of a **$1 billion** local-sourcing target after 2–3 years shows how slowly supplier depth accumulates. Distinguish **gross exports** from **value added**: India can post rising electronics export numbers while the margin, the components and the IP sit abroad.

4. **Bring in the employment-quality limb — this is what lifts the answer.** Value addition cannot deepen on a workforce the system classifies as unskilled and therefore does not train: statutory wedges such as UP's **₹13,690 (unskilled)** against **₹16,868 (skilled)**, severity of injury tracking the wage band (**2.27** fingers lost per accident below ₹8,000 a month against **1.54** above ₹20,000), and roughly **40% more wage theft** reported among "unskilled"-classified migrant workers. Name the cheap, available lever: **Recognition of Prior Learning** under the **NSQF**, plus the **four Labour Codes** in force since **21 November 2025** as the new compliance frame. Anchor normatively in **Articles 39(d), 42 and 43**.

5. **Critically evaluate — then resolve, don't straddle.** Concede the strongest objections: central policy (tariffs, PLI, trade agreements) created the entry point and States compete *inside* that frame; low DVA at entry is normal and Vietnam/Mexico deepened over 10–15 years; a State subsidy race can transfer rents without raising aggregate investment; and injury severity may be driven by **firm size and safety capital** rather than the classification itself. Resolve on **sequencing**: central incentives were necessary to win the assembly stage and cannot be dispensed with, but they are **not sufficient** for the component and value-addition stage, which runs on State-supplied ecosystem goods and on a workforce that is certified and trained. The proposition is therefore **sound as a statement about the next phase, overstated as a general claim** — the two are complements, not substitutes.

*Trap to avoid: this is a GS3 industrial-policy-and-employment question, not a GS2 federalism essay. Competitive federalism is **evidence** here, not the subject. An answer that becomes a Centre–State powers discussion, or that lists central schemes without ever reaching domestic value addition and workforce quality, will cap around 7–8/15 however accurate it is. Also resist writing two disconnected halves — the examiner is looking for the sentence that joins them: the China+1 question and the skill-classification question are the same question asked from two ends.*

- All four main pieces were cross-checked across more than one summary source where available; the **ECMS third tranche** figures (22 applications, ₹41,863 crore, approved early January 2026) were verified independently of the op-ed, and the **China+1** op-ed's provenance was confirmed against the authors' NUS East Asian Institute Special Report of September 2026.

- The author of the "unskilled" op-ed could not be established from the sources accessible this week. Attribute the argument to the piece, not to a named writer.
- No quotation in this pack is presented as a verbatim quote from an editorial. Phrases in quotation marks — *"a classification that produces the condition it claims to describe"*, *"firms do not locate in countries; they locate in districts"*, *"input audit versus outcome audit"* — are compressions written for answer-writing use, not the authors' words.
