# Model Essay — The Demographic Dividend Is a Deadline: Jobs, Skills and Women's Work in India's Window of Opportunity

*Topic type: Issue-based / contemporary (economy, society, governance, gender, federalism, international). Week 10 of the alternation cycle — last week was philosophical ("Courage is not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it"). Themes covered in recent weeks (courage, climate, freedom to err, federalism, AI, cities, doubt, measurement) are deliberately not repeated; this essay is about people as an economic asset.*

*Current hook: Census 2027, India's first digital Census, whose houselisting phase began on 1 April 2026.*

---

In April 2026, enumerators began knocking on doors for the first phase of India's Census, the first in sixteen years. When the count is complete it will describe, house by house, the most consequential asset the country owns: its people. The headline is already well known. India is the most populous nation on earth, and most of its people are of working age. Yet a population is only a number until it is employed, educated and healthy. This essay argues that the "demographic dividend" is not a gift of arithmetic but a conditional opportunity with an expiry date: it pays out only to the society that builds jobs, skills and women's participation in time, and it turns into a liability for the one that merely waits.

The arithmetic is favourable but finite. The UNFPA's *State of World Population 2025* put India's population at about 1.46 billion, with roughly 68 per cent aged 15–64 and a total fertility rate of 1.9, already below the replacement level of 2.1. The same report expects the population to peak at around 1.7 billion before it begins to fall. A falling fertility rate means the share of children shrinks and the share of workers swells, but only for a few decades; after that the elderly dependency ratio rises. The window is therefore a deadline, and the strategic question is not whether India is young but whether it will become prosperous before it becomes old.

History shows that the dividend is earned, not inherited. Bloom, Canning and Sevilla, in their RAND study *The Demographic Dividend* (2003), argued that a changing age structure creates only a potential for growth, realised when policy supplies health, education, flexible labour markets and sound macroeconomic management. East Asia converted its working-age bulge into an economic miracle through mass schooling and labour-intensive exports; other regions with comparable age structures did not. The lesson for India is that demography proposes while policy disposes.

The economic test is jobs, and here the picture is more troubling than the headline suggests. The Economic Survey 2023–24 estimated that India must create about 78.5 lakh non-farm jobs every year until 2030 to absorb its growing workforce. The latest Periodic Labour Force Survey annual report for 2025 shows an unemployment rate of only 3.1 per cent, but youth (15–29) unemployment stands at 9.9 per cent, 43 per cent of workers are still in agriculture, 56.2 per cent are self-employed and merely 23.6 per cent hold regular wage or salaried jobs. Low unemployment in a poor country often reflects not abundance of work but the absence of any alternative to scraping by; people cannot afford to be unemployed. The challenge is to move workers from low-productivity farms and household enterprises into factories, construction, logistics and modern services, which requires labour-intensive manufacturing, a more supportive environment for small firms to grow, and exports that compete with those of Vietnam and Bangladesh.

Skills are the second link. A job is a match between an opening and a capability, and India's difficulty is often the capability. The ASER surveys have for years found that many children in the upper primary grades struggle with reading and arithmetic expected of lower classes, and a graduate degree does not always carry employable skills. The National Education Policy 2020 promises foundational literacy, vocational exposure and multidisciplinary learning, and apprenticeship schemes seek to bridge classroom and workplace. But a dividend cannot be built on credentials alone; Amartya Sen's insistence that people are the ends and also the means of development reminds us that health, nutrition and quality schooling in the first years of life decide the productivity of the thirty-year-old.

The most underused part of the workforce is women. The PLFS shows female labour force participation rising from 23.3 per cent in 2017–18 to 41.7 per cent in 2023–24, and 40.0 per cent in the 2025 annual report, a striking improvement. Yet the nature of that rise matters more than its size. Analysts have pointed out that much of it is self-employment and unpaid helping in household enterprises, that women's share of regular wage work has barely moved (about 15.9 per cent), and that part of the increase may be distress-driven, with women entering work when family incomes are squeezed. Claudia Goldin's 2023 Nobel-winning work on women's labour-market history shows how social norms, childcare and the structure of jobs hold back participation even when education improves. For India, safe transport, affordable childcare, flexible hours and equal pay are therefore not welfare extras but dividend policy. Equity and efficiency point in the same direction.

The dividend is also unevenly distributed across the federation. In the NFHS-5 first-phase data, Bihar's fertility rate was about 3, while Kerala's was 1.8. Southern and western states are ageing earlier and richer; northern and eastern states are younger but poorer, with weaker schools and fewer jobs. This suggests that the workforce of tomorrow is being raised where capacity is thinnest. The answer lies in fiscal transfers that follow need, investment in human capital in the high-fertility states, and labour mobility with portable entitlements such as ration, health insurance and social security for migrants, so that a worker from Bihar can add to the growth of Tamil Nadu without losing his rights.

The dividend has an international dimension. As Europe, East Asia and, increasingly, China age, they will compete for skilled labour. India, already the world's largest recipient of remittances, can negotiate mobility and recognition agreements for nurses, technicians and engineers, but only if its training meets global standards. Technology complicates the picture: automation and artificial intelligence threaten the routine services and back-office jobs through which India expected to absorb its graduates, so the skills agenda must emphasise adaptability rather than a one-time qualification.

Finally, there is a human and political dimension. A young person with an education and no job is not a statistic; he is a postponed marriage, a deferred family, a mounting sense of grievance. Aspirations rise faster than opportunities, and the resulting frustration can sour social trust and feed demagoguery. Keynes's remark that "in the long run we are all dead" was a warning against waiting for markets to correct themselves; for a young country the corollary is that the unemployed twenty-year-old cannot wait for the long run.

Some sceptics argue that the very language of "dividend" is misleading: that capital-intensive growth may deliver output without jobs, that India's ageing may come sooner than its prosperity, and that population is a burden on land, water and the environment. These cautions are fair, and they strengthen rather than weaken the case for urgency. A dividend is a probability, not a promise, and the cost of failure, an old country that never got rich, would be borne by exactly the youth who were meant to be its strength.

The Census will soon tell us how many we are. What it cannot tell us is what we shall have made of ourselves by the time the count is repeated. India's young are an asset only if they are healthy, taught, employed and, in the case of half of them, free to work. The window is open, but it is not open for long, and the clock began running long before the enumerators arrived.
