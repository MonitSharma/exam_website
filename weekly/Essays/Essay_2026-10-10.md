# Model Essay — "Courage is not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it"

*Topic type: Philosophical / abstract (courage, fear, freedom, moral choice). Week 9 of the alternation cycle — last week was issue-based (India's development–climate dilemma). Themes covered in recent weeks (doubt, measurement, freedom to err, federalism, climate, AI, cities) are deliberately not repeated.*

*Source of the line: Nelson Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom (1995).*

---

In August 1908, an eighteen-year-old from Midnapore walked to the gallows in Muzaffarpur, convicted for a bomb thrown at a carriage in the course of the revolutionary campaign against the Raj. Khudiram Bose is remembered because he went to the rope composed. Yet the lesson that has grown around him is incomplete if it suggests he felt nothing. A boy of eighteen who feels nothing at the gallows is not brave; he is numb. Courage was in the walking, not in the absence of trembling.

Nelson Mandela made this point plainly in his autobiography: "I learned that courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it." The sentence overturns a popular picture of the brave person as one born without anxiety. This essay argues that courage is a *relationship* to fear, not a deficiency of it: fear is the signal, courage the response, and a society that wishes to remain free must learn to produce the second in the presence of the first, in its citizens, its institutions and its statecraft.

Philosophy reached this view early. Aristotle treats courage in the *Nicomachean Ethics* as a mean between rashness and cowardice, which means that the fearless fool and the frozen coward fail alike; the brave person fears the right things, to the right degree, and acts anyway. The Bhagavad Gita opens its catalogue of divine qualities with *abhayam*, fearlessness, but it arrives there through Arjuna's collapse on the battlefield, not around it. Kierkegaard went deeper, calling anxiety "the dizziness of freedom": fear is not a malfunction but the felt weight of having choices that matter. If so, abolishing fear would abolish seriousness itself.

Psychology supports the philosophers. Fear is an ancient, protective alarm; the amygdala fires before reflection does. Nobody chooses the first tremor. What can be trained is what follows: the soldier's drills, the surgeon's checklists, the pilot's emergency procedures all exist because courage under stress is better built as habit than hoped for as temperament. Neerja Bhanot, a 22-year-old flight purser on the hijacked Pan Am Flight 73 at Karachi in 1986, did not need to be fearless; she needed to remain responsible. She helped passengers escape and died shielding children, and was posthumously awarded the Ashoka Chakra. Her bravery lay in what she did *with* terror, not in its absence.

The political dimension is where fear becomes most consequential. Aung San Suu Kyi, in her essay "Freedom from Fear", wrote that "it is not power that corrupts but fear", adding that the fear of losing power corrupts those who wield it, and the fear of the scourge of power corrupts those subject to it. Authoritarian systems run on this twin fear: the ruler's insecurity breeds surveillance; the subject's breeds silence. Democracies, by contrast, are institutionalised courage: the right to dissent, an independent judiciary and a free press are mechanisms that spread the cost of bravery so that no single citizen must bear all of it. Roosevelt's 1933 line that "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself" was less a platitude than a diagnosis: panic had turned a banking crisis into a collapse.

India's freedom struggle was a long experiment in the same idea. Gandhi's contribution was not to make Indians fearless overnight but to give fear a disciplined outlet. Satyagraha, the salt march and the refusal to flee lathis converted dread into method, and he insisted that non-violence was the weapon of the strong, not a refuge for the timid; he preferred violence to cowardice, as he said in 1920, while hoping for something higher than both. Ambedkar's courage was of another kind: the Mahad Satyagraha of 1927 asked those socially forbidden to draw water from a public tank to do so in defiance of centuries of enforced fear. Tagore's prayer in *Gitanjali*, for a place "where the mind is without fear", set the national aspiration in a single image.

The same test applies in modern, quieter forms. Civil courage today is often bureaucratic: the engineer who flags a tender irregularity, the auditor who refuses to sign, the citizen who files the right-to-information application. Satyendra Dubey, an engineer with the National Highways Authority of India, was murdered in 2003 after exposing corruption in the Golden Quadrilateral project; Manjunath Shanmugam of Indian Oil was killed in 2005 for sealing a petrol pump that sold adulterated fuel. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act was enacted in 2014, but its effective operation has long been questioned. The moral is institutional: private courage is finite, and a state that depends on heroes has already failed. A mature polity lowers the price of doing right, so that courage need not be exceptional.

Economic life depends on this too. Enterprise is, at bottom, organised risk-taking: the founder who leaves a salary, the farmer who adopts a new crop, the woman who takes her first loan. A culture that stigmatises failure raises the cost of trying and starves innovation, while one that treats failure as information releases it. Fear is rational when downside is ruinous; the policy answer is to cap the downside through bankruptcy reform, insurance, credit access and social security, so that people can take reasonable risks. Courage thrives where safety nets exist, which is why the welfare state and the entrepreneurial state are allies rather than rivals.

Nations, like individuals, can mistake fear for prudence. Thucydides named fear, honour and interest as the drivers of war, and the arms races of the Cold War were driven as much by mutual dread as by ambition. Real international courage is the harder act of reassurance: the leader who signs a treaty, withdraws a threat or extends a hand risks being called weak by his own side. The Indus Waters arrangements that survived several wars, and the confidence-building habits that have kept some borders quieter than their politics, show that restraint taken in the presence of fear can be a form of strength.

There remain fair objections. Courage can be misdirected: the suicide bomber and the fanatic are also unafraid of death, which shows that fearlessness without moral purpose is not a virtue. Nor is every fear to be overcome; caution before a flood, a pandemic or a financial bubble is wisdom, which is exactly why Aristotle insists on the mean. And the privileged may call for courage cheaply from those who bear its true cost. Irom Sharmila fasted for nearly sixteen years, from 2000 to 2016, against the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act in Manipur, and her endurance reminds us that we owe such people gratitude and institutional protection, not just admiration. The test of courage, then, is threefold: a worthy end, proportionate means and the acceptance of cost.

Return to the boy at the gallows. What we honour in Khudiram Bose is not the absence of a heartbeat but the choice he made while it raced. The same choice, in smaller forms, is asked of ordinary citizens every day: to speak when silence is safer, to refuse when compliance is easier, to try when failure is likely. Societies cannot abolish fear, and should not wish to; they can only make the triumph over it more likely and less costly. A free country is one where courage is common because it has been made affordable, and where Mandela's sentence is no longer a miracle of the few but a habit of the many.

## Structure map

1. **Hook:** Khudiram Bose at the gallows, then the twist that composure is not numbness.
2. **Thesis:** courage is a *relationship to* fear, not its absence, via Mandela's line.
3. **Philosophical foundation:** Aristotle's mean, the Gita's *abhayam*, Kierkegaard's "dizziness of freedom".
4. **Psychological/individual dimension:** fear as alarm, courage as trained response; the Neerja Bhanot case.
5. **Political dimension:** Suu Kyi on fear and power; democracy as institutionalised courage; Roosevelt.
6. **Historical/Indian dimension:** Gandhi's satyagraha, Ambedkar at Mahad, Tagore's ideal.
7. **Contemporary, economic and international dimensions:** whistleblowers, risk-taking and safety nets, fear in geopolitics and reassurance as courage.
8. **Counter-view and conclusion:** misdirected courage, rational caution, the cost to the marginalised (Irom Sharmila); return to the hook with a forward-looking close.

## Reusable fodder

- **Quote (Mandela, *Long Walk to Freedom*):** "I learned that courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it." Fits essays on leadership, resilience, freedom, youth and ethics.
- **Quote (Aung San Suu Kyi, "Freedom from Fear"):** "It is not power that corrupts but fear." Useful for governance, democracy, authoritarianism, civil liberties and media freedom.
- **Case study:** Satyendra Dubey (NHAI, 2003) and Manjunath Shanmugam (IOC, 2005); both killed for exposing wrongdoing, showing why the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014 needs real operationalisation. Fits integrity, probity and governance answers (GS-4, GS-2).
- **Example:** Irom Sharmila's near-sixteen-year fast (2000–2016) against AFSPA in Manipur; also usable for non-violence, civil resistance, human rights and security-versus-liberty debates.
