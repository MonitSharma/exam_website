# Model Essay — The Plate Is Full, the Farmer Is Not: Reimagining Indian Agriculture for Income, Resilience and Dignity

## The Essay

In 1965, with the country dependent on imported wheat and threatened by war, Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri gave India a slogan that fused its two anxieties: *Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan*. Six decades later, the soldier guards a border and the farmer guards the plate, yet only one of them has been fully paid in dignity. India's farms have just produced a record 376.56 million tonnes of foodgrain (Third Advance Estimates, 2025–26), about 5 per cent above the previous year, and nearly 80 crore people receive subsidised grain under the National Food Security Act. Meanwhile, the average agricultural household earned ₹10,218 a month in 2018–19, the last national survey estimate (NSS 77th round), up from ₹6,426 in 2012–13 in nominal terms. The paradox is plain: India has solved the problem of *food* but not the problem of the *farmer*. The task of the next decade is to shift the goal from tonnes produced to livelihoods secured, and to do so within the limits of land, water and climate.

**Historically,** Indian agriculture is a story of hard-won success. The Bengal famine of 1943 killed millions under colonial neglect; the "ship-to-mouth" years of the 1960s forced PL-480 imports. The Green Revolution, driven by high-yielding wheat and rice varieties, Swaminathan's scientific leadership, public procurement and the MSP regime, converted a begging bowl into a granary. Amartya Sen's observation that no functioning democracy with a free press has suffered a famine finds its Indian proof here. But the very success locked the country into a template: wheat and rice, procured at assured prices, grown intensively in a few states. The strategy that ended hunger now distorts the cropping pattern.

**Economically,** the central fact is the mismatch between people and output. Agriculture employs about 46 per cent of India's workforce but contributes well under a fifth of gross value added, which means that income per worker is structurally low. Landholdings compound the problem: according to the Agriculture Census, a large majority of holdings are marginal (under one hectare), and the average holding is only about a hectare, too small for mechanisation or bargaining power. Price support, though politically potent, reaches unevenly; a high-level committee (Shanta Kumar, 2015) estimated that only about 6 per cent of farmers actually sold to government at MSP, concentrated in wheat and paddy in a few states. Yet the MSP regime has real strengths: it reduces price risk and underpins the public distribution system. The debate is therefore not "MSP or markets" but how to widen security without fiscal collapse or distortion, through price-deficiency payments, diversified procurement of pulses, oilseeds and millets, and better storage.

**Ecologically,** the template is exhausted in places. Free power and assured paddy procurement have drawn down aquifers across Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh; stubble burning blankets north India each winter; excess fertiliser, especially urea, damages soil health. Climate change raises the stakes: the heat wave of March 2022 reduced wheat yields in parts of north India and led to a wheat export ban, showing how quickly weather shocks become macroeconomic and diplomatic events. Agriculture is both a victim and a contributor, since paddy fields and livestock are major sources of methane. Here the philosophy of "more crop per drop," micro-irrigation, direct-seeded rice, crop diversification to millets and pulses, and natural and organic practices (Sikkim's full organic conversion in 2016 offers a case study, with its limits for yield and scale) are not luxuries but conditions of survival.

**Technologically,** a quiet transformation is under way. Digital public infrastructure such as direct benefit transfer has allowed PM-KISAN to send ₹6,000 a year to over 9 crore farm families without intermediaries, and the Cabinet has extended it to 2030–31. Satellite data, drones, soil health cards, e-NAM and AI-based advisory services promise to reduce information asymmetry. But technology without access is another form of exclusion: a smallholder with poor connectivity, tenancy without legal recognition and no institutional credit cannot benefit from a precision-farming app. The Green Revolution's lesson, that technology amplifies existing inequalities unless institutions carry it to the weak, applies doubly in the digital age.

**Socially and politically,** the farmer is both revered and neglected. The agitation against the three farm laws of 2020, repealed in November 2021, revealed deep distrust: reforms imposed without consultation, in a subject largely on the State List, were read as an abandonment of the safety net. The episode carries two lessons together. Markets, private investment and contract farming can raise incomes, and an economist like Theodore Schultz argued long ago that peasants are rational and respond to opportunity; but farmers will accept change only when trust, consultation and cooperative federalism precede legislation. There is also a gender dimension: women do a large share of farm work but own little land, and fewer than one in four PM-KISAN beneficiaries is a woman. Agrarian distress, expressed in farmer suicides and rural indebtedness, is not only an economic but a human and moral question.

**Internationally,** agriculture is now an instrument of both prosperity and diplomacy. Agricultural exports reached about US$54.7 billion in 2025–26, and India supplies a large share of the world's rice and many commodities, so export bans, however well-intended for domestic prices, shake food-importing nations and invite criticism. At the WTO, India defends public stockholding for food security against subsidy ceilings it considers unfair, a stance rooted in the interests of its low-income farmers and consumers. Balancing domestic stability with its role as a reliable global supplier is a delicate act of statecraft.

**Counter-views deserve their place.** Some argue that India's real task is to move people *out* of agriculture into manufacturing and services, and that sentimentalising the farm only prolongs low productivity. Others insist that, in a country of this scale, jobs elsewhere are insufficient and the farm must absorb labour and be made remunerative, since it is a shock absorber, as the pandemic years showed when reverse migration swelled farm employment. Both are partly right. The sensible position is a double movement: make farming more productive and profitable for those who stay, while building non-farm rural employment, agro-processing, food parks, cold chains, and rural manufacturing, for those who leave.

**The way forward** is therefore a package rather than a panacea. Consolidate small farms functionally rather than physically, through Farmer Producer Organisations and cooperatives that give smallholders scale in buying, selling and processing. Reorient incentives from water-guzzling cereals to the crops that nutrition and ecology demand. Recognise tenant farmers, expand crop insurance with timely settlement, and strengthen market infrastructure and warehouse receipts. Treat the farmer as an entrepreneur and steward of ecosystems, paying for ecosystem services such as carbon storage and groundwater recharge. And protect the consumer, because food inflation is the most regressive of taxes.

Shastri's slogan once asked the nation to honour two kinds of sentinel. A country that celebrates record harvests while its cultivators remain among its poorest has honoured the crop but not the one who grew it. The real measure of Indian agriculture in 2047 will not be how many million tonnes it harvests, but whether the person who sows the seed can sleep without fear of the sky, the market or the moneylender. When the plate is full *and* the farmer is secure, the slogan will at last have been fulfilled.

4. **Economic** — workforce vs GVA mismatch, small holdings, MSP's reach and its real strengths.

6. **Technological** — DBT/PM-KISAN, digital tools, and the access/inclusion caveat.

8. **International** — exports, export bans, WTO public stockholding.
9. **Counter-view** — "move people out of farming" vs "farm as shock absorber"; reconcile via a double movement.
10. **Way forward + conclusion** — FPOs, incentives, tenants, insurance, ecosystem payments; callback to Shastri and a closing image of the farmer's security.
