## The Essay

In August 2025 the United States doubled its tariffs on Indian goods to 50 per cent, a quarter of it a declared penalty for India's purchases of Russian oil. Within six months, India had concluded a free trade agreement with the European Union (announced in late January 2026) covering a market of roughly two billion people, and a trade framework with Washington that brought the US tariff down to 18 per cent. The same country had been punished by one partner, courted by another, and left standing at the centre of a negotiation it did not start. This is what a fractured world looks like from New Delhi: not a contest between two blocs, as in the Cold War, but a bazaar of overlapping rivalries in which every partner is also a competitor and every supply chain is also a pressure point. The question for India is no longer whether to choose a side, but how to remain a free agent while being plugged into everyone. Strategic autonomy, understood as the capacity to take decisions in one's own interest without being coerced by anyone, is the organising principle of that answer. The argument of this essay is that autonomy is not distance from the world but capability within it, and that India will keep it only if it is built at home as carefully as it is defended abroad.

The idea has deep roots. In the late 1940s Nehru declared that independent India would keep away from the power politics of rival groups that had twice led the world to war; the Non-Aligned Movement, founded at Belgrade in 1961, institutionalised that instinct. But the history is more instructive than the slogan. Non-alignment never meant passivity: the 1962 war with China exposed how idealism without capability invites humiliation, and the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation of August 1971, signed on the eve of the Bangladesh crisis, showed that a state that prizes independence may still need a partner when its survival is at stake. The 1991 balance-of-payments crisis, which forced India to pledge gold and open its economy, taught the economic corollary: autonomy in the world of finance is purchased with reserves, exports and credibility, not rhetoric. The lesson from seven decades is that autonomy is a product of strength; the doctrine changes its name (non-alignment, strategic autonomy, multi-alignment) but its test remains the same: can India say no when it must, and yes when it should?

The strategic dimension makes the test sharpest. India sits beside two nuclear-armed neighbours, one of them its largest trading partner and a standing adversary on the Himalayan border. A multipolar order gives India room to manoeuvre, since it can belong to the Quad and to BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation at once, but also removes the comfort of predictable alliances. Defence is where dependence is most visible. According to SIPRI, India was the world's second-largest arms importer in 2020–24, taking 8.3 per cent of global arms imports, and while Russia's share of its imports has fallen from 72 per cent in 2010–14 to 55 per cent in 2015–19 and 36 per cent in 2020–24, diversification towards France, the United States and Israel has merely spread rather than ended reliance. A country that imports its fighter engines, submarine technology and spares cannot be fully autonomous, however skilful its diplomacy. This is why the real foundation of autonomy is domestic defence production and indigenous technology, from the engine programmes to the iDEX start-up ecosystem, and why the Atmanirbhar defence agenda deserves to be read as a foreign-policy instrument.

The economic dimension is where the world has changed most. Interdependence, once the promise of globalisation, has become a weapon: tariffs, sanctions, export controls on semiconductors, and rare-earth restrictions are used to coerce. India's answer has been to hedge through trade diplomacy. Free trade agreements with the United Kingdom, the EU, and the European Free Trade Association, a framework with the United States, and engagement with the Gulf and ASEAN reduce the leverage any single market holds over India. Yet the hedge has costs. The EU deal brings with it carbon border adjustment pressures and regulatory standards that may squeeze small exporters, while any American arrangement raises questions about agriculture and data. A mature view recognises that autonomy is never absolute; what matters is the diversity of one's dependencies. A nation dependent on ten suppliers for a critical input is freer than one dependent on one, even if neither is wholly independent.

Energy exposes the ethical tension within the doctrine. India imports the bulk of its crude oil, and its purchases from Russia, which rose to about a third of its crude imports during the war in Ukraine before easing under pressure, were defended on the ground that a developing country with hundreds of millions of poor consumers cannot be asked to bear the price of another continent's war. External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar's retort to European criticism in 2022, that Europe had to grow out of the mindset that its problems are the world's problems but the world's problems are not Europe's problems, captured a real grievance about double standards. But critics reply, with some force, that neutrality between an aggressor and its victim is not a moral position, and that a state that aspires to a permanent seat at the Security Council cannot treat principles as commodities. The honest answer lies between: India has been consistent in calling for dialogue and respect for sovereignty, but the credibility of that call depends on applying it equally to every conflict, from Ukraine to Gaza.

The technological dimension is the frontier on which autonomy will be won or lost in the next decade. Data, semiconductors, cloud computing and artificial intelligence are the new oil. India has shown that a digital public infrastructure model, Aadhaar, UPI and the Open Network for Digital Commerce, can be built in the public interest and exported as an alternative to both the American platform model and the Chinese state model, and countries from Singapore to the UAE have linked their payment systems with UPI. That is soft power with substance. But dependence on foreign chips, foreign cloud providers and foreign foundation models is the new vulnerability, which is why the India Semiconductor Mission and the push for sovereign compute are not industrial policy alone but security policy.

Autonomy also has a social and psychological dimension that diplomats seldom discuss. A confident nation negotiates differently from an anxious one. The diaspora of over thirty million, whose remittances make India the world's largest recipient, is both an asset and a hostage: it gives India influence in Washington, London and the Gulf, but also exposes it to visa policies and immigration politics it cannot control. And a polity that is internally divided, whose federal states quarrel over water, whose minorities feel insecure, whose youth are unemployed, is easier to pressure externally. Chanakya's maxim, in the Arthashastra's mandala theory, that the neighbour is a natural rival and the neighbour's neighbour a natural friend, is a reminder that foreign policy begins at the border and is sustained by domestic cohesion. Thucydides' Melian dialogue, in which the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must, is what awaits a nation that confuses rhetoric with power.

Two criticisms deserve acknowledgement. Realists argue that strategic autonomy is a luxury of the strong: a state of India's size, with per capita income still well below that of China, cannot afford to keep all doors open and will eventually have to choose. Idealists argue the opposite, that multi-alignment reduces India to a transactional power with partners but no friends. Both have a point, and both are answered by the same thing: the more India's economy, technology and military capacity grow, the more its choices become its own. Autonomy is therefore less a foreign-policy doctrine than a development outcome.

Return to the tariff wall of August 2025 and the trade tables of 2026. The lesson is not that India won by being clever, but that it was able to negotiate because it was large, growing and useful to everyone. In a fractured world no country can be self-sufficient, and none should wish to be. The goal is to be so woven into many networks that no single thread can strangle, and so strong at home that no partner can dictate. India's task, then, is to be everyone's partner and no one's dependent, and the surest way to that end is the unglamorous work of building capability at home: in factories, laboratories, classrooms and institutions. A nation that does this need not choose a camp, because the world will come to its tent.
