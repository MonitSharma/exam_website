# Model Essay — Unity Without Uniformity: The Federal Question in India's Next Decade

*Topic type: Issue-based / contemporary (polity & governance, economy, society, culture, institutions). Week 6 of the alternation cycle — last week was philosophical ("Not everything that can be counted counts").*

---

On 17 April 2026, the Lok Sabha declined to amend the Constitution. The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 — which would have raised the House's ceiling from 550 seats to 850, anchored the coming delimitation to the 2011 census, and thereby brought forward the one-third reservation for women promised in 2023 — secured 298 votes in a House of 528 present, fifty-four short of the two-thirds required. A government with a comfortable simple majority had been stopped by the one arithmetic it could not command. The defeat was not about arithmetic but about a question India has deferred for half a century: on what terms the parts of the Union are represented in the whole. Federalism is routinely treated here as a concession the Centre makes to the states. It is better understood as the method by which a country this size holds together at all — unity purchased not by uniformity, but by negotiation.

The founders were candid that they were not building a textbook federation. Moving the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, B.R. Ambedkar said it "is a Federal Constitution inasmuch as it establishes what may be called a Dual Polity" — the Union at the centre, the States at the periphery, each sovereign in its assigned field — yet added that it "can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances." Article 1 calls India a "Union of States", avoiding the vocabulary of compact: the parts may not secede. K.C. Wheare judged the arrangement quasi-federal; Granville Austin, more sympathetically, called it cooperative federalism. Partition and princely integration made centralisation rational in 1950. Whether a settlement designed for that India can carry the India of 2050 unrevised is the open question.

Nowhere is the strain clearer than in representation. Seats were frozen to the 1971 census by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, and the freeze extended by the 84th to the first census after 2026, to reassure states pursuing population stabilisation. That reprieve is now expiring. On 2011 figures, with the House unchanged, PRS Legislative Research projects Tamil Nadu falling from 39 seats to 32 and Kerala from 20 to 15, while Uttar Pradesh rises from 80 to 89 and Bihar from 40 to 46 — shares that move the same way even if the House is enlarged. The southern grievance is intuitive: states that did what national policy asked would be governed less. Yet the opposing principle is not frivolous. "One person, one vote, one value" is democratic doctrine too, and a voter in Bihar cannot indefinitely be asked to count for less. Two goods collide here; there is no villain.

Mature federations resolve such collisions structurally, by giving territory a chamber of its own: the American compromise of 1787 paired a population-based House with a Senate in which Wyoming equals California, and Germany's Bundesrat weights small Länder far above their numbers. India has no such counterweight. The Rajya Sabha is itself apportioned broadly by population, and since the domicile requirement went in 2003 it has functioned less as a council of states than as a second chamber of parties. The 131st Bill would have widened the gap, shifting the ratio between the Houses from roughly 2.2:1 to 3.3:1. Expanding the lower House without repairing the upper one treats the symptom.

The fiscal argument runs along the same grain. The Sixteenth Finance Commission (Chair: Arvind Panagariya), reporting for 2026–31, held the states' share of the divisible pool at 41 per cent and rebuilt the horizontal formula: income distance 42.5 per cent, 2011 population 17.5, and 10 per cent each for demographic performance, area, forest and ecology, and a new "contribution to GDP". The last rewards producer states, the others protect poorer ones — a hedge between efficiency and equity. But it declined to cap cesses and surcharges, which keep roughly a fifth of the Centre's gross tax revenue outside the pool. States that spend about 60 per cent of combined government expenditure while raising little more than a third of revenue negotiate from structural dependence. Their own conduct complicates the claim: off-budget borrowing, unreformed power distribution companies and competitive subsidy politics have damaged the case for autonomy as much as Delhi has.

Friction has migrated to the courts as well. In *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025) the Supreme Court found the Governor's withholding of ten Bills illegal, imposed timelines, and used Article 142 to deem those Bills assented. A Presidential Reference followed, and on 20 November 2025 a five-judge Bench unanimously held the opposite: no judicial timelines, no deemed assent, and the functions under Articles 200 and 201 not ordinarily justiciable. The opinion was no licence for obstruction — it reaffirmed federalism as a basic feature, confined the Governor to three options, and preserved limited review where prolonged, unexplained inaction frustrates a legislature. The lesson is sobering: courts can police the edges of federal conduct, but cannot manufacture the convention that a Governor is a constitutional head rather than a party instrument.

Culture supplies the oldest fault line. Language, not money, made the states: the reorganisation of 1956 followed sustained agitation, and the Official Languages Act was amended in 1967 to guarantee English's indefinite continuance after the anti-Hindi protests in Madras. That memory shapes the present quarrel over the three-language formula in the National Education Policy, under which Samagra Shiksha funds to Tamil Nadu were withheld; the state budgeted ₹2,152 crore from its own exchequer in 2025-26 rather than comply. Whatever one concludes about the pedagogy, the mechanism deserves scrutiny. When transfers become contingent on a contested cultural policy, cooperative federalism quietly becomes conditional federalism, and compliance bought is not consent won.

Not all of this is conflict. The GST Council remains the most ambitious experiment in pooled sovereignty attempted by any large federation, and in September 2025 it collapsed four rate slabs into two — 5 and 18 per cent, with 40 per cent on sin and luxury goods — by consensus rather than by vote. The machinery works when the will exists, even if states surrendered much of their tax autonomy to build it. Harder to defend is the third tier. India's urban local bodies raise revenue worth about 0.6 per cent of GDP, against 9.2 per cent for the Centre and 14.6 per cent for the states; the Sixteenth Finance Commission's ₹7,91,493 crore for local bodies helps, but states that demand subsidiarity from Delhi rarely extend it to their own municipalities. A federalism that stops at the state secretariat is half-built.

Read in this light, the vote of 17 April 2026 is evidence of health, not paralysis. A constitutional order in which the terms of the Union cannot be rewritten without persuading those who stand to lose is behaving as designed; the two-thirds threshold exists so that representation is renegotiated rather than imposed. What follows matters more than what failed. A settlement that lasts must expand the House so that fast-growing states gain without slow-growing states losing seats absolutely; give the Rajya Sabha a purpose commensurate with its name; restore the Inter-State Council as a forum of first resort; and push resources past the states to the cities and panchayats where citizens actually meet the government. Daniel Elazar defined federalism as self-rule plus shared rule. India has spent three-quarters of a century perfecting the second half of that formula. The next decade will test whether it can be equally serious about the first.

## Structure map

1. **Hook — a dated, concrete event.** The 131st Amendment Bill's defeat (17 April 2026; 298 of 528, needed 352). A specific vote is sharper than a general lament.
2. **Thesis in the last two lines of the intro.** Reframe the subject: federalism is not a *concession* to states but the *method* of Indian unity — "unity without uniformity."
3. **Historical–constitutional foundation.** Ambedkar's dual polity, Article 1's "Union of States", Wheare's "quasi-federal", Austin's "cooperative federalism"; why 1950 chose centralisation. Establishes that the tension is designed, not accidental.
4. **Dimension 1 — political/representational.** Delimitation: the 1976 freeze, the 84th Amendment, PRS seat projections. Deliberately argue *both* sides (demographic penalty vs one-person-one-vote) — examiners reward this.
5. **Dimension 2 — comparative/institutional.** US Senate, German Bundesrat, India's missing counterweight chamber. Comparative evidence lifts an essay out of the purely domestic.
6. **Dimension 3 — economic/fiscal.** 16th FC formula, cesses and surcharges, 60 per cent expenditure share — then a self-correcting paragraph on states' own fiscal indiscipline.
7. **Dimensions 4 and 5 — constitutional-executive and cultural.** Governor/assent litigation; language and conditional transfers. Both end on a principle, not an anecdote.
8. **Counter-current, then conclusion.** GST Council as proof of what works, the neglected third tier as the unfinished task; close by returning to the April vote and to Elazar's "self-rule plus shared rule."

## Reusable fodder

- **Ambedkar, 4 November 1948:** the Constitution "is a Federal Constitution inasmuch as it establishes what may be called a Dual Polity", and "can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances." Works for federalism, Constitution-as-living-document, institutional design, and "flexibility vs rigidity" essays.
- **The 0.6 / 9.2 / 14.6 triad:** urban local bodies raise revenue worth about 0.6 per cent of GDP, against 9.2 per cent for the Centre and 14.6 per cent for the states (RBI, 2023-24). A single line that proves decentralisation stalled at the second tier — usable in urbanisation, local governance, service delivery and even climate-adaptation answers.
- **Delimitation seat projections (PRS, 2011 census, House unchanged):** Tamil Nadu 39→32, Kerala 20→15, Uttar Pradesh 80→89, Bihar 40→46. Concrete numbers for representation, demographic dividend, north–south divergence and population-policy essays.
- **Daniel Elazar's definition:** federalism as "self-rule plus shared rule." A compact conclusion-line for any essay on decentralisation, autonomy, diversity or institutional balance.
