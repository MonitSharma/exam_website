# Model Essay — India in the Age of Artificial Intelligence: Adoption Without Agency Is Not Progress

*Topic type: Issue-based / contemporary (technology, governance, economy, ethics, environment, India & the world). Week 4 of the alternation cycle — last week was philosophical ("Doubt is the beginning, not the end, of wisdom").*

---

In February 2026, at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi, delegations from 118 countries gathered for the India AI Impact Summit; on 19 February, eighty-nine countries and international organisations adopted the New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact, built around three sutras — People, Planet, Progress — and framed by the old Sanskrit formula *Sarvajan Hitaya, Sarvajan Sukhaya*. There was a quiet historical irony in the scene. A country that sat out the first industrial revolution as a colony, whose handloom weavers were undone by Lancashire's mills, was now convening the world on the revolution in machine cognition. But hosting a summit is not the same as shaping a technology. India's real question is not whether it will adopt artificial intelligence — the market has settled that — but whether adoption will translate into **agency**: over its livelihoods, its data and compute, its public reasoning, and its energy. Adoption without agency is not progress; it is a more sophisticated form of dependence.

Begin with livelihoods, because that is where anxiety is loudest. The IMF's 2024 analysis found that roughly 40 per cent of global employment is exposed to AI — around 60 per cent in advanced economies, 40 per cent in emerging markets, and 26 per cent in low-income countries. India, with a large agricultural and informal workforce, sits at the lower end of that exposure. It is tempting to read this as a shield. It is better read as a symptom: we are insulated because we are underdeveloped. And the insulation is thinnest precisely where India's aspiration is thickest. Generative systems are most capable at routine cognitive work — drafting, coding, first-level analysis, voice support — the entry-level white-collar rung that has carried a generation of first-graduates out of small-town poverty. The ILO–IHD *India Employment Report 2024* found that youth constitute nearly 83 per cent of India's unemployed, and that the share of the unemployed with secondary or higher education nearly doubled, from 35.2 per cent in 2000 to 65.7 per cent in 2022. Education already fails to guarantee work. A technology that automates the beginner's job, while leaving the expert's judgement intact, will widen that gap before it narrows it.

Yet the pessimistic reading is incomplete, because in India technology has been a leveller whenever it was built as public infrastructure rather than private product. UPI democratised payments not by being clever but by being a public rail anyone could ride. The same logic animates Bhashini, the national language mission offering open speech-recognition, translation and text-to-speech APIs across India's 22 scheduled languages. For a farmer in Bundelkhand or a litigant in Kerala, the barrier to the digital state has never been literacy alone — it has been English. An AI that speaks Bhojpuri and Kannada dissolves a gatekeeping no scheme could. Similar gains appear in AI-assisted tuberculosis screening in districts with no radiologist, and in voice-delivered crop advisories. The technology is not intrinsically concentrating; it concentrates when owned narrowly and diffuses when built as a commons.

Which is why the sovereignty question matters. Under the IndiaAI Mission, approved in March 2024 with an outlay of ₹10,371.92 crore, India has onboarded over 38,000 GPUs at subsidised rates for startups and researchers — serious, and also modest against a global capital race measured in hundreds of billions of dollars. The deeper vulnerability is structural: India supplies data and talent, and imports the models, chips and cloud that turn them into intelligence. That is the classic colonial trade pattern — raw material out, finished goods in — dressed in silicon. Strategic autonomy once meant not choosing between blocs; today it means not being reduced to a market for someone else's cognition. India's advocacy for Global South access is therefore not diplomatic generosity but enlightened self-interest.

The ethical and constitutional dimension is starker still, because the Indian state now runs on databases. Here the cautionary tale is Dutch. In the *toeslagenaffaire*, the Netherlands' tax authority used a self-learning fraud-detection system that effectively flagged low-income families with dual or foreign nationality; some 26,000 families were wrongly accused, driven into debt and, in thousands of cases, separated from their children. In January 2021 the entire Dutch government resigned. The lesson is not that algorithms are malevolent but that they are confident, fast and unaccountable at scale — and that a welfare state which cannot explain a denial has failed at something more fundamental than efficiency. Articles 14 and 21 demand reasoned, non-arbitrary state action; a model that cannot give reasons cannot discharge that duty. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is a beginning, but consent law does not by itself deliver contestability. The right to be told *why* must survive automation.

Democracy's other exposure is to synthetic truth. On 10 February 2026, MeitY notified amendments to the IT Rules bringing "synthetically generated information" — deepfakes and AI-generated media — within the due-diligence framework, effective 20 February, with mandatory labelling, provenance metadata and a three-hour takedown window for flagged unlawful content. In a country where an audio clip can move a constituency overnight, such rules answer a genuine danger. But speed cuts both ways: a three-hour clock invites platforms to remove first and adjudicate never, and over-removal is itself a harm to a republic that depends on argument. Good regulation here is a balance between the citizen's right not to be deceived and the citizen's right to speak.

There is, finally, an ecological bill. The IEA estimates data centres consumed about 415 TWh in 2024 — roughly 1.5 per cent of global electricity — and projects this will more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, close to Japan's entire consumption today. For a power- and water-stressed country courting data-centre investment, "intelligence" is not weightless; it is turbines, transmission lines and cooling water. AI can help too — optimising grids, forecasting renewables — but only if the ledger is kept honestly on both sides.

Beneath all this lies a quieter, individual question. Gandhi, in *Young India* of 17 June 1926, wrote: "What I object to, is the craze for machinery as such… Men go on 'saving labour', till thousands are without work and thrown on the open streets." His objection was never to the tool but to its unexamined worship. Norbert Wiener, the father of cybernetics, put the same warning in colder terms in *God & Golem, Inc.* (1964): the future "will be an ever more demanding struggle against the limitations of our intelligence, not a comfortable hammock in which we can lie down to be waited upon by our robot slaves." A machine that answers every question can quietly erode the habit of asking them.

India's median age is about 29, and roughly 68 per cent of its people are of working age — a dividend that is really a deadline. The window in which a young, ambitious workforce can be converted into prosperity will not stay open while we deliberate. What Bharat Mandapam offered was a stage; what it did not confer was capability. Agency will be earned in less photogenic places: in a school that teaches judgement rather than recall, in a fab and a sovereign compute stack, in a grievance officer who can explain an algorithm's refusal, in an electricity grid honest about its costs. The machines will compute regardless. Only a republic can decide what the computation is for.

## Structure map

1. **Hook (event-based)** — Bharat Mandapam, Feb 2026: 118 countries, New Delhi Declaration; historical irony of a nation that missed the first industrial revolution now convening the cognitive one.
2. **Thesis** — the question is not adoption but *agency*; adoption without agency is sophisticated dependence. Four arenas flagged: livelihoods, data/compute, public reasoning, energy.
3. **Economic dimension (the threat)** — IMF exposure data; India's low exposure is a symptom, not a shield; the entry-level rung is the vulnerable one; ILO youth-unemployment data.
4. **Counterpoint (the opportunity)** — technology as public infrastructure levels; UPI logic; Bhashini and 22 languages; health and agriculture uses. *Balance is introduced here deliberately, immediately after the pessimistic paragraph.*
5. **Strategic / international dimension** — IndiaAI Mission and GPUs vs global capital; "raw material out, finished goods in" colonial-pattern analogy; strategic autonomy updated for the algorithmic age.
6. **Ethical / constitutional dimension** — Dutch *toeslagenaffaire* as case study; Articles 14 and 21; DPDP Act as beginning, not answer; the right to reasons.
7. **Democratic / informational dimension** — IT Rules amendment 2026 on synthetic content; genuine danger *and* over-removal risk; two rights in tension.
8. **Environmental + individual/psychological dimensions** — IEA data-centre electricity projections; then Gandhi and Wiener on tools, work and the atrophy of thought.
9. **Conclusion** — demographic dividend as deadline; return to Bharat Mandapam ("a stage, not a capability"); close on the machine/republic distinction, echoing the thesis.

**Reusable skeleton:** Event hook → thesis naming 3–4 arenas → threat → counterpoint → strategic → ethical/constitutional → democratic → environmental/personal → deadline + callback.
