# Model Essay — Growing Without Burning: India's Development–Climate Dilemma Is a Design Problem, Not a Choice

*Topic type: Issue-based / contemporary (environment, energy, economy, international relations, ethics, health, technology). Week 8 of the alternation cycle — last week was philosophical ("Freedom is not worth having if it does not include the freedom to err").*

*Current hook: COP31 (Antalya, Türkiye, November 2026) and India's NDC 3.0, approved by the Union Cabinet on 25 March 2026.*

---

In 1730, soldiers of the Maharaja of Marwar arrived at Khejarli, a village near Jodhpur, to fell khejri trees to fuel lime kilns for a new palace. Amrita Devi, a Bishnoi woman, wrapped her arms around a trunk and refused to let go; tradition remembers her judging that a tree saved, even at the cost of a life, was a good bargain. She was killed, then her daughters, then, by the community's reckoning, 363 Bishnois in all. On learning of the killings, the king halted the felling and issued a copper-plate decree forbidding the cutting of trees and the hunting of animals in Bishnoi villages. As negotiators gather in Antalya this November for COP31, India faces Khejarli's question on a continental scale: the palace or the grove, the kiln or the tree. The question is usually framed as a choice. It is a false one. India cannot grow without energy, but neither can it grow on a degrading atmosphere; the real choices lie in how growth is built, who pays for the transition, and whether the world's rules treat latecomers fairly.

India has answered this question before, in a distinctive voice. At Stockholm in 1972, Indira Gandhi asked, "Are not poverty and need the greatest polluters?", insisting that no environment can be improved on the backs of the hungry. In 1991, Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain's *Global Warming in an Unequal World* separated the "survival emissions" of the poor from the "luxury emissions" of the rich and condemned the contrary position as environmental colonialism. The Brundtland Commission's 1987 formula for sustainable development, meeting present needs "without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs", supplied the grammar. The ethical core is therefore double: we owe the unborn a liveable planet and the poor a decent life, and any policy that sacrifices one to the other is incomplete.

The numbers give India's claim substance. Its per-capita emissions are about two tonnes, less than half the global average (Global Carbon Project, 2022), and the government estimates that it has contributed only about 4 per cent of cumulative emissions since 1850 while housing roughly one-sixth of humanity. Yet arithmetic cuts both ways. India is now the third-largest emitter, around 8 per cent of the global total, and the Global Carbon Budget 2025 projected its fossil emissions to rise 1.4 per cent that year, while the remaining 1.5°C budget, about 170 gigatonnes, would be exhausted in roughly four years at current rates. "Small per head" is a moral argument, not a physical shield; the atmosphere does not read per-capita tables.

India's practice has been better than its critics allow, and worse than its boosters claim. By 31 July 2026, non-fossil capacity stood at about 300 GW, more than 54 per cent of installed capacity, with solar alone near 165 GW. When demand touched a record 256 GW on 25 April 2026, it was met without shortage. But capacity is not generation: coal still anchors the baseload, and the government has told Parliament that coal-based capacity is projected to reach 315 GW by 2035–36. NDC 3.0 promises a 47 per cent cut in the emissions intensity of GDP by 2035 from 2005, 60 per cent non-fossil capacity, a carbon sink of 3.5–4.0 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent, and net zero by 2070. Critics may fairly note that the 60 per cent goal asks for only six more points from a system already above 54; defenders answer that a developing economy's first duty is to keep the lights on while it decarbonises. Both are right, which is precisely why the dilemma is one of design.

Internationally, the dilemma becomes a bargaining problem. At Baku in 2024 the world agreed a finance goal of $300 billion a year by 2035 for developing countries, against an aspirational $1.3 trillion that they say they need. Meanwhile trade has become a climate instrument: the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026, pricing the carbon embedded in imports of steel, aluminium, cement and fertilisers, sectors in which Indian exporters are exposed. There is an irony in rich countries that industrialised on cheap carbon now taxing the carbon of those who follow. India's posture at COP31, where Türkiye hosts and Australia's Chris Bowen presides over negotiations, must be two-edged: ambitious at home, so that its demands for finance, technology and fair trade rules carry weight abroad. Its invocation of common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR-RC) in NDC 3.0 is credible only to the extent that its domestic delivery is.

At home, the social dimension is where abstractions meet lives. Coal-belt districts such as Dhanbad and Korba run on the mine and the plant; a transition that shuts them without alternative livelihoods merely moves the burden from the atmosphere to the poor. Retraining, district-level diversification and social protection are therefore conditions of political sustainability, not afterthoughts. And the cost of delay is already being paid. A *Lancet Planetary Health* study of ten Indian cities attributed about 33,000 deaths a year, 7.2 per cent of all deaths, to PM2.5 above WHO guidelines, with Delhi at 11.5 per cent. The Lancet Countdown 2025 reported that in 2024 the average Indian endured 19.8 heatwave days, and that heat cost 247 billion potential labour hours and about $194 billion in potential income, roughly two-thirds of the losses in agriculture. Clean air and cooler workplaces are not luxuries to be purchased after development; they are development.

Technology and institutions supply grounds for hope. Renewables allow a latecomer to leapfrog rather than repeat the West's coal-fired path, though storage, grid investment and financing costs remain the hard frontier. Adaptation shows what institutions can do. The 1999 super cyclone killed nearly 10,000 people in Odisha; the state responded with a dedicated disaster authority, hundreds of cyclone shelters and village-level warning systems. In 2013, Cyclone Phailin killed 44 after nearly a million people were moved to safety, and in 2024 the evacuation of some 600,000 people ahead of Cyclone Dana produced zero casualties. Resilience was not luck; it was design.

Finally, climate policy cannot be left to ministries. Agarwal and Narain's distinction must be turned inward: India's affluent cannot shelter behind the poor's per-capita average. Gandhi's remark that "we are thieves in a way" when we keep what we do not need, from *Trusteeship*, points the same way. Some will urge India to grow first and clean up later, as the Environmental Kuznets Curve hypothesis hopefully suggests. But carbon-intensive infrastructure locks in emissions for decades, the poor meet climate shocks first and hardest, and retrofitting is dearer than designing right the first time.

The Khejarli decree did not abolish the kiln; it drew a line around the grove. India's task is the same on a larger canvas: to keep the kiln burning for 1.4 billion aspirations while protecting what sustains them, through clean capacity, fair finance, a just transition and resilient institutions. Growth and sustainability are not rival claimants but two halves of a single design brief. A country that treats them as a trade-off will end with neither prosperity nor planet; one that treats them as a design problem may be remembered, in 2047 and beyond, as the nation that grew, and whose growth the Earth could afford.

## Structure map

1. **Historical hook with an argument inside it.** Khejarli (1730): a community that valued trees over royal demand, ending in a protective decree. Link at once to COP31 and pose the question: palace or grove?
2. **Thesis that dissolves a false binary.** Not "growth vs environment" but *how* growth is built, *who pays*, and *whether global rules are fair*. Naming three variables gives the body its skeleton.
3. **Intellectual and ethical lineage.** Indira Gandhi (Stockholm 1972) → Agarwal & Narain (survival vs luxury emissions, 1991) → Brundtland (intergenerational equity, 1987). Frame the ethics as double: unborn and poor.
4. **Economic/scientific data with a two-edged reading.** Low per-capita and historical share (India's moral case) versus 8 per cent of global emissions and a shrinking 1.5°C budget (physical reality).
5. **Policy record with honest tension.** Over 300 GW non-fossil, record peak demand met, NDC 3.0 targets, but coal capacity still rising to 315 GW. Present critics and defenders, then conclude that both are right.
6. **International dimension.** CBDR-RC, the $300 billion vs $1.3 trillion finance gap, CBAM as climate-trade linkage, COP31 diplomacy; India must be ambitious at home to be persuasive abroad.
7. **Social, health and adaptation dimensions.** Just transition in coal belts; air pollution and heat as costs of delay; Odisha's cyclone record as proof that institutions can be designed.
8. **Individual responsibility, counter-view, and a synthesis that returns to the hook.** Turn the survival/luxury distinction inward; rebut "grow first, clean later"; close with the Khejarli decree as a model: the line around the grove, not the end of the kiln.
