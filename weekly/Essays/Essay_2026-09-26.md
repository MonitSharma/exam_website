# Model Essay — "Freedom is not worth having if it does not include the freedom to err"

*Topic type: Philosophical / abstract (freedom, autonomy, error, responsibility). Week 7 of the alternation cycle — last week was issue-based (federalism in India's next decade).*

*Adapted from Gandhi: "Freedom is not worth having if it does not connote freedom to err and even to sin." — Young India, 12 March 1931.*

---

For much of the colonial era, the argument against Indian self-rule was delivered in the soft voice of guardianship: Indians were not yet "ready". They would quarrel, mismanage, perhaps fail; better, then, that wiser hands govern until the pupil came of age. In March 1931, days after signing the Gandhi–Irwin Pact, Gandhi answered that argument at its root. "Freedom is not worth having," he wrote in *Young India*, "if it does not connote freedom to err and even to sin." If even the humblest creature has been given the freedom to err, he asked, how could any human being, however experienced, delight in depriving another of that right?

The claim sounds almost reckless — a defence of mistakes. It is in fact among the most serious statements about freedom ever made. Its thesis is that freedom that is granted only on condition of being used correctly is not freedom but supervision; that error is not the enemy of autonomy but its price and its teacher. Yet the freedom to err is not the freedom to harm, and a mature society must learn to tell the two apart.

Begin with the individual, for that is where the claim is most intimate. A person who is never permitted to choose wrongly never truly chooses at all; he merely complies. Learning in every serious sense — a child's first steps, a scientist's hypothesis, a young adult's career — proceeds through trial and correction. Viktor Frankl, writing out of the Nazi camps in *Man's Search for Meaning*, identified the last of the human freedoms as the freedom "to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances." That freedom is meaningful only because the choice could have gone otherwise. A life protected from all error is also a life emptied of authorship.

The political argument follows naturally. John Stuart Mill built *On Liberty* (1859) on two pillars: that "over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign", and that "all silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility." Mill's insight was that no authority — king, church, party or expert committee — is so certain of the truth that it may forbid others from being wrong in public. Democracy is institutionalised fallibility: it expects voters and governments to make mistakes and builds the machinery to correct them. Amartya Sen's finding that no famine has occurred in a functioning democracy is, at bottom, an argument about error. Democracies misjudge harvests too; but a free press and the fear of the ballot force the mistake into the open before it turns into catastrophe. Authoritarian systems do not err less; they err silently, and at scale.

Science and enterprise tell the same story in a different register. In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to find a mould contaminating one of his culture plates — a laboratory mistake that became penicillin. ISRO's Vikram lander crashed on the Moon in September 2019; the agency did not bury the failure but studied it, and Chandrayaan-3, which landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023, was built on what ISRO chairman S. Somanath called a "failure-based design" — asking not what must go right, but what can go wrong and how to survive it. Economies, too, need a dignified way to fail. India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 was, in essence, a legal recognition that a market where businesses cannot exit cleanly is a market where few will dare to enter. A culture that punishes every failure as disgrace ends up with safe bureaucrats and no pioneers.

Yet the essay must now turn against its own momentum, because the maxim can be abused. Isaiah Berlin, in his 1958 Oxford lecture *Two Concepts of Liberty*, quoted the grim truth that "freedom for the pike is death for the minnows." The freedom to err is defensible when the one who errs also bears the cost. It becomes indefensible when the error is paid for by others — the drunk driver, the polluting factory, the official whose "mistake" costs a village its water. Mill himself drew this line with his harm principle: society may restrain a person only to prevent harm to others. There is also the matter of scale. An individual's error teaches; a state's error, repeated across a billion lives, can devastate. This is why the Constitution guards some choices fiercely — the Supreme Court's nine-judge bench in *Puttaswamy* (2017) unanimously held privacy to be a fundamental right rooted in dignity and autonomy — while subjecting public power to audit, judicial review and the discipline of reasons.

The more insidious threat today does not come from open prohibition but from quiet design. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008) argued for "libertarian paternalism": arrange defaults so that people drift toward better choices while remaining free to opt out. Used wisely — pension auto-enrolment, organ-donation defaults — nudges respect freedom while reducing avoidable error. Used carelessly, and multiplied by recommendation algorithms that predict and pre-empt our desires, they risk producing citizens who are never forbidden anything and yet seldom decide anything. India's own framework now wrestles with this: the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, notified in November 2025 and phased in over eighteen months, place consent and its withdrawal at the centre of the digital relationship. Consent is merely the legal form of an older idea — that a person must remain the author of his choices, including the unwise ones.

Freedom to err, finally, is inseparable from responsibility. On the night of 14 August 1947, Nehru reminded the Constituent Assembly that "freedom and power bring responsibility." Two years later, Ambedkar warned that "liberty cannot be divorced from equality, equality cannot be divorced from liberty" — for liberty without fraternity and equality becomes the privilege of the few. The freedom to err is therefore not a licence for carelessness. It is a covenant: I may be wrong, but I must own my wrongness, learn from it, repair what it damages, and extend the same freedom to others. A society that tolerates error but not accountability becomes indulgent; one that demands accountability but tolerates no error becomes fearful. Maturity lies between.

Gandhi practised what he wrote. He publicly called his premature launch of civil disobedience in 1919 a "Himalayan miscalculation", suspended the Non-Cooperation Movement after Chauri Chaura in 1922, and turned his autobiography into a public record of *Experiments with Truth* — experiments, not proclamations. The lesson for India's next century is the same one he offered the Empire. Do not ask whether citizens, students, entrepreneurs or scientists are ready never to fail; no one ever is. Ask whether our institutions are strong enough to let them fail safely, honest enough to let them fail visibly, and generous enough to let them try again. A freedom that is withdrawn at the first mistake was only ever a loan. The freedom worth having is the one that trusts us to stumble — and holds us responsible for rising.

## Structure map

1. **Historical hook that frames the stakes.** The colonial "not ready" argument, answered by Gandhi's 1931 line. A hook that carries an *argument* is stronger than one that is merely decorative.
2. **Thesis with a built-in limit.** Freedom conditional on correct use is supervision — but the freedom to err is not the freedom to harm. Announcing the limit early signals balance.
3. **Individual/psychological dimension.** Learning through error; Frankl's "last of the human freedoms"; choice without the possibility of error is compliance.
4. **Political dimension.** Mill's sovereignty and infallibility arguments → democracy as institutionalised fallibility → Sen's famine argument recast as error-correction.
5. **Scientific and economic dimension.** Fleming, Chandrayaan-2 → Chandrayaan-3's "failure-based design", IBC 2016 — one global and two Indian cases in one paragraph.
6. **Counter-argument paragraph.** Berlin's pike and minnows, Mill's harm principle, the problem of scale, Puttaswamy. Attack your own thesis to earn the right to keep it.
7. **Contemporary frontier.** Nudges, algorithms, DPDP Rules — how freedom erodes by design rather than prohibition. This is where the essay ties to current affairs.
8. **Ethical synthesis + conclusion.** Nehru and Ambedkar on responsibility → "error + accountability" as a covenant → return to Gandhi (his own admitted errors) → forward-looking close.

## Reusable fodder

**Quotes (verified).**

- "Freedom is not worth having if it does not connote freedom to err and even to sin." — **Gandhi**, *Young India*, 12 March 1931. Use for liberty, democracy, education, youth, innovation essays.
- "All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility." — **J. S. Mill**, *On Liberty* (1859), ch. 2. Use for free speech, media, academic freedom, dissent.
- "Freedom for the pike is death for the minnows." — quoted by **Isaiah Berlin**, *Two Concepts of Liberty* (Oxford, 31 October 1958). The best one-line counter-argument to any "more freedom" thesis; use for regulation, inequality, markets.
- "Liberty cannot be divorced from equality, equality cannot be divorced from liberty. Nor can liberty and equality be divorced from fraternity." — **B. R. Ambedkar**, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949.
- Avoid: "Between stimulus and response there is a space…" is commonly attributed to Viktor Frankl, but Quote Investigator could not find it in his writings. Use Frankl's genuine "last of the human freedoms" line instead.

**Examples and data (bank these).**

- **Failure-based design:** Vikram lander lost in September 2019; Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023. S. Somanath: design around "what all can fail, and how to protect it"; landing zone widened from 500 m × 500 m to 4 km × 2.5 km. Use for science & tech, institutional learning, resilience, leadership.
- **Famines and democracy:** Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999) — no famine has taken place in a functioning democracy. Use for democracy, media, food security, governance.
- **Right to privacy:** *K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017), nine-judge bench, unanimous — privacy as a fundamental right under Article 21. Pair with the **DPDP Rules** (notified November 2025, phased over 18 months; consent managers and consent withdrawal). Use for technology, data, surveillance, AI governance.
- **Nudge:** Thaler & Sunstein (2008), "libertarian paternalism" — defaults steer choices without forbidding. Use for public policy, behavioural economics, Swachh Bharat–style campaigns, AI and choice.
