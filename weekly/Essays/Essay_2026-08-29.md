# Model Essay — India's Cities Will Decide India's Century

*Topic type: Issue-based / contemporary (governance, economy, society, environment). Week 2 of the alternation cycle — last week was philosophical.*

---

In September 1994, plague broke out in Surat. Rats, garbage and stagnant water had done what no invader could: emptied a city of a quarter of its people in a week. Surat became a global byword for Indian urban squalor. Three decades later, the same city stands second in the Swachh Survekshan 2024–25 rankings, a diamond-and-textile hub whose garbage is collected door to door and whose drains are mapped. Nothing changed in Surat's geography, climate or poverty; what changed was the quality of its administration and the seriousness of its citizens. That is the whole argument of this essay in miniature. India's cities are not destined to be either engines or slums. They are the place where the country's economic ambition, ecological survival and social promise will all be settled — and how they turn out is a matter of political choice, not fate.

Begin with the arithmetic, because it is startling. India's cities already generate roughly 63% of national GDP from about a third of its people, and the World Bank projects that urban areas will contribute close to 70% of GDP growth by 2036, when 600 million Indians — 40% of the population — will live in towns and cities. The urban population is expected to roughly double between 2020 and 2050, from about 480 million to 951 million. This is agglomeration economics at work: density lowers the cost of matching workers to firms, ideas to capital, and buyers to sellers. Bengaluru's software cluster, Tiruppur's knitwear, Surat's diamonds — none of these could exist dispersed across villages. If India is to escape the middle-income trap, the escape route runs through its cities.

But the same density that multiplies productivity also multiplies failure. Air pollution is the clearest case. A 2024 study in *The Lancet Planetary Health* estimated that long-term PM2.5 exposure above WHO guidelines is associated with about 1.5 million excess deaths a year in India. Heat is the newer emergency: the summer of 2024 saw 37 Indian cities cross 45°C, and independent researchers documented over 700 heatstroke deaths against an official count of 360 — a gap that itself indicts our data systems. Water swings between extremes, with Chennai's "Day Zero" of 2019 and Bengaluru's tanker crisis of 2024 bracketing years of floods. The World Bank estimates India needs some $2.4 trillion by 2050 to build climate-resilient urban infrastructure, against current urban infrastructure spending of roughly 0.7% of GDP. The cities that generate our growth are also where climate change will present its bill.

The deepest constraint, however, is not money but governance. The 74th Constitutional Amendment of 1992 promised elected, empowered urban local bodies with devolved functions, funds and functionaries. Three decades on, the promise remains half-kept. Municipal revenues and expenditures in India have stagnated at around 1% of GDP for over a decade, against 7.4% in Brazil and 6% in South Africa; the RBI's 2024 report on municipal finances found more than half of municipal corporations raising less than half their revenue themselves. Most Indian cities have mayors with ceremonial powers and commissioners answerable to the state, while parastatals build the metro and the water board. Delhi's air cannot be fixed by a body that controls neither its stubble, its vehicles nor its construction. A city that cannot tax, plan or be voted out cannot be held responsible — and irresponsibility, not poverty, is the root of most urban failure.

Socially, the Indian city is a place of extraordinary contradiction. It shelters 6.54 crore people in slums by the 2011 Census, and its economy runs overwhelmingly on informal labour that possesses neither tenure nor contract — a reality made brutally visible in the reverse migration of 2020, when millions walked home because the cities that employed them had never counted them. Yet the same city is also India's greatest engine of emancipation. Ambedkar was scathing about the romanticised village, calling it a sink of localism and a den of ignorance and communalism; he saw urban anonymity as liberation from the tyranny of the known. Cities give a Dalit entrepreneur a customer who does not ask his caste, a woman a wage of her own, a young person a life not scripted by birth. The migrant in a Mumbai chawl is not a symptom of failure; she is exercising the most rational choice available to her. Policy that treats migration as a problem to be reversed misreads what cities are for.

Which is why the housing and services question is a question of citizenship, not charity. PMAY-Urban has delivered over 90 lakh houses to slum-dwelling families, a real achievement, but the harder work is tenure security, rental housing for the single migrant worker, and services that follow people rather than property titles. Aristotle observed that the city comes into being for the sake of life and exists for the sake of the good life. An Indian city that offers work but not water, wages but not walkability, has stopped halfway.

The encouraging truth is that competence is contagious and demonstrably possible. Indore has topped Swachh Survekshan for eight consecutive years — not through some unique endowment, but through source segregation, route-monitored collection, scientific processing and sustained political will. Surat rebuilt itself after 1994. Kerala's decentralisation devolved real money to local bodies and produced measurably better local outcomes. These are Indian solutions in Indian conditions, which is precisely what makes them impossible to dismiss. The instruments exist too: land value capture and municipal bonds to fund infrastructure from the wealth that infrastructure itself creates; property tax reform; transit-oriented development; and credible city-level climate action plans of the kind Ahmedabad pioneered with India's first Heat Action Plan in 2013.

There is a global dimension as well. More than half the urban India of 2050 is yet to be built. That is an unusual kind of freedom: countries with mature stock must retrofit, whereas India can choose, once, whether its future cities are sprawling and car-dependent or compact and transit-served. Because India will be among the largest sources of new urban emissions and new urban demand this century, these choices are not merely domestic. What Ranchi and Rajkot decide about zoning will register in global carbon budgets — and, conversely, India's success would offer the most useful development model available to urbanising Africa.

None of this permits complacency in either direction. Urban boosterism that ignores the crushed lungs and the tanker queues is dishonest; so is the pastoral nostalgia that treats the city as India's disease. The honest position is that the city is a technology — enormously powerful, and dangerous when badly maintained. Surat's story is finally not about drains. It is about the discovery that a city's condition reflects the seriousness with which its people and its institutions take themselves. India's coming decades will not be decided in its villages or in its capital's committee rooms alone, but in the ward offices, water pipes and bus lanes of a few hundred cities. The task is to build them, deliberately and in time, for the 600 million Indians who will call them home.

## Structure map

1. **Hook (vignette):** Surat 1994 plague → today's second-cleanest city. Concrete, surprising, and carries the thesis.
2. **Thesis:** Cities are where India's economic, ecological and social future is settled — and the outcome is a matter of choice, not destiny.
3. **Economic dimension:** Scale and stakes — GDP share, 2036/2050 projections, agglomeration logic.
4. **Environmental dimension:** The same density magnifies harm — air, heat, water, and the price tag of resilience.
5. **Governance dimension (the pivot):** Diagnose the *root cause* — the unfinished 74th Amendment, municipal finance, accountability. Placing the causal paragraph at the centre gives the essay a spine.
6. **Social dimension + counterpoint:** Slums and informality, then the deliberate turn — the city as emancipator (Ambedkar). This is where balance is demonstrated.
7. **Solutions, evidenced:** Indore, Surat, Kerala, Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan, land value capture. Prove the thesis is achievable, not aspirational.
8. **International/forward dimension:** Half of urban India 2050 is unbuilt; India's choices as a global variable.
9. **Conclusion:** Reject both boosterism and nostalgia, return to the Surat hook, close on the 600 million.

## Reusable fodder

- **"Cities generate ~63% of India's GDP and will contribute nearly 70% of GDP growth by 2036; 600 million urban Indians (40%) by 2036" (World Bank).** Deployable in essays on growth, employment, infrastructure, federalism, or the demographic dividend.
- **Municipal revenues stuck at ~1% of GDP vs 7.4% in Brazil and 6% in South Africa (RBI Report on Municipal Finances, 2024).** The single sharpest data point for any answer on decentralisation, the 74th Amendment, or fiscal federalism.
- **~1.5 million excess deaths a year from PM2.5 above WHO guidelines (*The Lancet Planetary Health*, 2024); 2024 heatwave — 37 cities above 45°C, 700+ documented heatstroke deaths vs 360 official.** Use for climate, public health, or the recurring "data and governance" theme; the official-vs-actual gap doubles as an argument about state capacity.
- **Ambedkar on the village as "a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism" (Constituent Assembly, 4 November 1948).** A high-value counter-quote against romanticised ruralism — usable in essays on caste, modernity, migration, or Gandhi–Ambedkar debates.
- **Indore: cleanest city eight years running; Surat: from 1994 plague to 2nd rank.** The best Indian "capacity is buildable" case study — works in any essay needing proof that reform is possible without waiting for wealth.
