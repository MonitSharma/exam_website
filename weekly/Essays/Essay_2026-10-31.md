# Model Essay — "Patience and Time": Why a Hurried Age Still Needs the Slow Virtues

## The Essay

In *War and Peace*, as Napoleon's army marches on Russia, the aged General Kutuzov tells a restless young officer: "there is nothing stronger than those two: patience and time, they will do it all." Kutuzov refuses glory-seeking battles, lets the winter and the distance do their work, and wins. The novel is Tolstoy's quarrel with the cult of the decisive moment, and two centuries later it reads like a diagnosis of our own age. We live in a civilisation built for speed: India now processes about 24 billion UPI transactions in a single month (September 2026), a payment settles before a thought completes, and a verdict, a career or a reputation is expected in the time it takes to scroll. Yet the things that matter most, a constitution, a scientist, a forest, a trusted institution, a settled mind, still mature on slow clocks. This essay argues that patience is not passivity or delay; it is disciplined, purposeful endurance, and societies and individuals that lose it do not become faster, only more brittle.

**Philosophically,** the first task is to separate patience from its imitators. Laziness waits because it will not act; patience acts and then lets consequences ripen. The Bhagavad Gita's counsel of *nishkama karma* (Chapter 2, verse 47: the right is to action alone, never to its fruits) is perhaps the subcontinent's deepest statement of the idea: do the work wholeheartedly, release the clock. The Stoics made the same point differently, locating freedom in what is within our control, our effort and response, rather than in the timing of outcomes. Patience, so understood, is a form of strength that presupposes a goal worth waiting for, which is why it differs from resignation. Its opposite is not urgency but impatience, the inability to bear the gap between wish and result.

**Psychologically,** modern life is engineering that gap away. Gloria Mark's research at the University of California, Irvine found that the average time people spent on a single screen before switching fell from about 150 seconds in 2004 to about 47 seconds in studies from 2016 to 2020. A mind trained in 47-second units struggles with the sustained attention that long novels, hard mathematics or deep friendships demand. Walter Mischel's famous marshmallow experiments of the 1970s seemed to show that children who could wait for a larger reward later fared better. But the story is more humble than it first appeared: a 2018 replication by Watts, Duncan and Quan, using over 900 children, found the association small and largely vanishing once family background was controlled. The lesson is double-edged. Patience is learnable and valuable, but it is also a product of circumstance: a child who has reason to trust that adults keep promises can afford to wait. The ability to wait is therefore partly a privilege, and a just society must create the security that makes waiting rational.

**Historically and politically,** the great achievements of democracies have been slow ones. India's Constituent Assembly took two years, eleven months and eighteen days to frame the Constitution, sitting for 167 days across eleven sessions, in a country wounded by Partition and impatient for self-rule. The deliberation was criticised as delay; it gave India an instrument that has survived over seven decades of strain when many post-colonial charters did not. Gandhi's Dandi March of 1930, a 24-day walk to pick up a pinch of salt, was similarly a lesson in political time: movements that endure are built by accumulation, not eruption. The opposite temptation, quick fixes, ordinances, overnight announcements and governance by headline, often produces policy that must be reversed at higher cost. Patience in governance means consultation, evidence and sequencing.

**Economically,** the payoffs of patience compound. UPI, which seems to have appeared from nowhere, rests on years of unglamorous groundwork: Aadhaar from 2009, the Jan Dhan accounts from 2014, mobile penetration, and the interoperable design of NPCI, which launched UPI in 2016. Impatient capital looks for returns in quarters, whereas infrastructure, human capital and research yield in decades. Nations that invest in schools and laboratories while their rivals chase quarterly numbers collect the dividend later. The same logic applies to households: saving, skill-building and education are all deferred gratification, and financial products that exploit impatience, from instant loans to speculative trading apps, show how profitably this weakness can be mined.

**Scientifically and technologically,** discovery is the patience of institutions. When India's Chandrayaan-2 lander Vikram crashed near the lunar south pole in September 2019, the response of the scientific community was not to abandon the programme but to diagnose, redesign and try again; Chandrayaan-3 touched down on 23 August 2023, making India the first country to land near the lunar south pole, at a cost of roughly ₹615 crore by former ISRO chairman K. Sivan's estimate. A culture that treats failure as terminal cannot attempt hard things; a culture that treats failure as data can. Technology, meanwhile, pulls both ways. Algorithms engineered for engagement erode attention, while the same tools can bring slow knowledge, archives, lectures and research, to millions.

**Environmentally,** nature runs on timescales that exasperate human calendars. A forest takes decades to mature, an aquifer centuries to recharge, and soil organic matter generations to rebuild, but each can be spent in a season. Climate change is, in this sense, the ultimate failure of patience: a harvest of present comfort taken from future capital. The remedy is not austerity for its own sake but a longer horizon: planting, restoring and transitioning energy systems on the understanding that the beneficiary may be someone else's grandchild.

**Ethically and socially,** patience is the root of the virtues we most prize in public life: tolerance, which is patience with difference; compassion, which is patience with suffering; and forgiveness, which is patience with the wrongdoer. A society impatient with disagreement drifts toward majoritarian shortcuts, and a public that demands instant justice, through mobs, trial by media or "encounters," abandons due process, which is institutionalised patience. In the household, the slow work of raising children, caring for the old or sustaining a marriage resists optimisation.

**Yet patience has its limits, and a balanced view must say so.** Martin Luther King, in 1963, spoke of "the fierce urgency of Now" and warned against "the tranquilizing drug of gradualism." To tell the oppressed to wait is to turn patience into an instrument of injustice; centuries of caste discrimination, gender inequality and untouchability were defended in the name of slow reform, and Ambedkar's insistence on immediate constitutional guarantees reflected the same impatience with evil. Urgency is likewise right when the clock is real: a pandemic response, a flood rescue or a famine cannot be deliberated at leisure. The distinction is one of object: patience with *processes* that need time to ripen, impatience with *injustices* that need no more time to be recognised as wrong. Wisdom, as often, lies in telling the two apart.

**The way forward** is to build patience into the design of our lives and institutions rather than rely on exhortation: schools that reward depth over rote speed; public-service norms and tenure that allow officials to see projects through; budgets and infrastructure plans with long horizons; media and technology norms that protect attention; and social security that gives citizens the confidence to wait. The administrator, the very person who will one day read these lines, stands in exactly this tension, expected to act promptly when a flood or riot demands it and to resist the pressure to deliver quick, cosmetic results when the problem is structural.

Kutuzov won not because he did nothing but because he knew which moments demanded the sword and which demanded the calendar. That knowledge is the heart of the matter. A hurried age does not need less speed so much as better judgment about when speed serves us. India, as it approaches its centenary of independence, will be judged by whether it combines the urgency of a young nation with the patience of an old civilisation, acting at once where injustice is concerned and waiting, with purpose, where growth is concerned. In that union, patience and time may indeed "do it all."

7. **Science-tech and environment** — Chandrayaan-2 to Chandrayaan-3; forests, aquifers and climate as failure of patience.

10. **Way forward and conclusion** — Institutional design for patience; callback to Kutuzov and the "old civilisation, young nation" synthesis.
