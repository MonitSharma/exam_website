## Post-attempt analysis — 9 instructive questions from CSAT 2023

These are real items from the 2023 paper, verified against the official key and re-computed independently. I've paraphrased stems rather than reproducing the full copyrighted paper; go to the official PDF for exact wording. Each one teaches a method or flags a trap that UPSC reuses.

### Quant & number system

**1. Sum of all digits appearing in the integers 10 to 100. → 856**
The trap is brute force under time pressure. Don't add 100 numbers — count digit *occurrences*. For 10–99: each tens-digit 1–9 shows up 10 times → (1+…+9)×10 = **450**; each units-digit 0–9 shows up 9 times → 45×9 = **405**. Add 100 → **1**. Total = 450+405+1 = **856**. Lesson: positional counting beats addition every time UPSC says "sum of all digits."

**2. The 9-digit repunit 111,111,111 multiplied by itself — sum of digits of the product. → 81**
Tempting wrong move: try to actually multiply. Recognize the pattern instead: 11²=121, 111²=12321, 1111²=1234321… so 111,111,111² = **12,345,678,987,654,321**, whose digits run 1→9→1. Sum = 2×(1+…+8)+9 = 72+9 = **81**. Lesson: repunit / "all-same-digit" numbers are pattern questions, not multiplication questions.

**3. p, q, r, s are distinct single-digit positive numbers — greatest value of (p+q)(r+s). → 225**
The trap is grabbing 9 and 8 for one bracket. Two facts: (a) use the four largest digits 6,7,8,9 (fixed total 30); (b) for a fixed sum, a product of two parts is **maximized when the parts are equal**. So split 30 into 15 and 15: (6+9)(7+8) = 15×15 = **225**. (9+8)(7+6)=17×13=221 is the seductive wrong answer. Lesson: "fixed sum → make the factors equal" is one of the highest-yield CSAT shortcuts.

**4. N is the number formed by writing 9 ninety-nine times; remainder when N is divided by 13. → 11**
Don't chase the giant number — write N = 10⁹⁹ − 1 and work mod 13. Powers of 10 mod 13 cycle with length 6: 10, 9, 12, 3, 4, 1, then repeat. 99 = 16×6 + 3, so 10⁹⁹ ≡ 10³ ≡ **12** (mod 13). Then N ≡ 12 − 1 = **11**. Lesson: "a digit repeated k times" → convert to 10ᵏ−1 (or a geometric form) and use a remainder cycle.

**5. Rearrange the digits of 11223344 into 8-digit numbers so odd digits sit in odd positions and even digits in even positions — how many? → 36**
The trap is treating all 8 digits as one big permutation. They're independent: the four odd positions take {1,1,3,3} → 4!/(2!2!) = **6** ways; the four even positions take {2,2,4,4} → likewise **6** ways. Multiply: 6×6 = **36**. The wrong-option 12 comes from adding (6+6) instead of multiplying. Lesson: when a constraint splits a problem into independent blocks, count each block and **multiply**.

**6. 1186 leaves a remainder of 31 when divided by a natural number — how many such numbers exist? → 9**
Key step most people miss: if remainder is 31, the divisor must divide 1186 − 31 = **1155**, *and* must be **greater than 31** (a remainder can't exceed its divisor). 1155 = 3×5×7×11 → 16 divisors. Those above 31 are 33, 35, 55, 77, 105, 165, 231, 385, 1155 = **9**. Forgetting the "divisor > remainder" filter is the classic error here. Lesson: "leaves remainder r" → divisor divides (N−r) and divisor > r.

**7. For a 3-digit number, the ratio (number) : (sum of its digits) is least — find the difference between its hundreds and units digit. → 8**
To make N/(digit-sum) small you want a *small* number with a *large* digit sum, which pulls in opposite directions. Scanning the cheap candidates, 199 gives 199/19 ≈ 10.47, beating 189/18 = 10.5 and 99-style traps. The minimizer is **199**; hundreds digit 1, units digit 9, difference = **8**. Lesson: "minimize value-to-digit-sum" almost always lands on a number like 199 / 1999 — small lead digit, nines after.

### Reading comprehension (the section that broke most candidates in 2023)

**8. The waste-to-energy passage** (municipal solid waste: segregation at source is rare, recycling sits with the informal sector, three-quarters of the municipal budget goes to collection/transport, and waste-to-energy "ideally fits after segregation and recycling, before the landfill").
UPSC's trap here is the **over-reach inference**: an option that says waste-to-energy is "the best solution to India's waste problem" or that India "should stop using landfills." The passage only *places* waste-to-energy in a sequence — it never ranks it as best or calls for ending landfills. The correct option restates the author's modest claim: waste-to-energy is one link in a chain that **only works if segregation and recycling happen first**. Lesson: on "what does the author imply/assume," pick the option you can defend with a sentence already in the passage — reject anything stronger, more absolute, or more prescriptive than the text.

**9. The water-stress / inter-State rivers passage** (14 of 20 major river systems already water-stressed; 75% of people in water-stressed regions; water is a **State** subject under the Constitution except for regulation of inter-State rivers).
The tempting distractor is a "solution" option — e.g., "the Union should take over all water management" — which the passage does **not** support; it merely describes the constitutional split. The other classic trap is mis-reading the statistic (confusing "water-stressed" with the narrower "water-scarce" subset). The correct option sticks to what's stated: stress is widespread and worsening, and authority is constitutionally divided, which is *why* inter-State disputes arise. Lesson: in CSAT RC, statistics and legal/constitutional facts are quoted precisely to bait misreads — re-find the exact figure or clause before you commit.

## Recurring patterns UPSC repeats (bank these)

1. **"Repeated-digit / repunit" number theory.** Numbers like 111…1, 999…9, or "9 written 99 times" appear almost every year (2023 had at least two). They are *never* meant to be computed directly — convert to 10ᵏ±1 and use a remainder cycle, or recall the repunit-square pyramid (12321…). Pattern recognition, not arithmetic.

2. **Counting with an independence or a constraint filter.** Arrangement/divisor questions (Q5, Q6) hinge on one structural insight — split into independent blocks and multiply, or apply the "divisor > remainder" / "divides (N−r)" rule. The wrong options are deliberately built from the *adding-instead-of-multiplying* or *forgot-the-filter* mistake.

3. **RC "inference vs. over-reach."** Every CSAT RC set has 2–3 questions where the wrong answer is true-sounding but says something **stronger, more absolute, or more prescriptive** than the passage (words like *best, only, must, all, never*). The right answer is the cautious restatement you can point to in the text. This single skill is worth more marks than any quant topic.

## One strategy tweak for next time

**Run the paper in two passes with a hard time gate.** On a brutal paper like 2023, the failure mode isn't difficulty — it's spending 6 minutes on a number-theory problem and never reaching three easy RC questions. Next sitting: **Pass 1 (first 70 min)** — go front-to-back, solve only what clicks within ~75 seconds, and *circle-and-skip* anything that doesn't (especially long quant). **Pass 2 (last 50 min)** — return to skipped items, and finish with all decision-making questions, attempting every one since they carry **no negative marking**. Track how many questions you left unseen in Pass 1; on 2023, simply *reaching* every easy RC and DM question is usually the difference between 60 and 85.
