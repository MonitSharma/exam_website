## Problems (representative UPSC-style — closed-book attempt first)

**P1 (interference — division of amplitude).** In a **Newton's rings** arrangement, a plano-convex lens of radius of curvature $R$ rests on a flat glass plate; the film is illuminated normally with monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$ and viewed in reflection.
(a) Derive the radii of the **dark** and **bright** rings and show the dark rings satisfy $r_n = \sqrt{n\lambda R}$.
(b) Explain why the **centre is dark** in reflected light.
(c) If the air film is replaced by a liquid of refractive index $\mu$, how do the radii change? How can this be used to measure $\mu$?

**P2 (interference — Michelson interferometer).** Describe the Michelson interferometer and, for the case of the two mirrors *effectively parallel*, derive the condition for the **circular (equal-inclination) fringes** seen at infinity. Hence show how a known displacement $d$ of the movable mirror over which $N$ fringes collapse at the centre gives $\lambda = 2d/N$. State how the instrument measures the refractive index of a thin transparent sheet.

**P3 (diffraction — single slit).** For **Fraunhofer diffraction at a single slit** of width $a$ illuminated normally by a plane wave of wavelength $\lambda$:
(a) Derive the intensity distribution $I(\theta) = I_0\left(\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2$, $\beta = \dfrac{\pi a \sin\theta}{\lambda}$.
(b) Locate the minima and show the **central maximum is twice as wide** as the secondary maxima.
(c) Find the angular half-width of the central maximum and its linear width on a screen at focal distance $f$ of a lens.

**P4 (diffraction — grating & resolving power).** For a **plane transmission grating** of $N$ slits with grating element $(a+b)$:
(a) State and justify the grating equation $(a+b)\sin\theta = m\lambda$ and explain the roles of the "interference" and "diffraction" factors in the intensity.
(b) Define **resolving power** and, using the Rayleigh criterion, derive $\dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN$.
(c) A grating $2\ \text{cm}$ wide has $6000$ lines. In the second order, can it resolve the sodium D-lines ($589.0$ and $589.6\ \text{nm}$)?

**P5 (polarization — Malus & Brewster).**
(a) State and derive **Malus's law** $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ for light passing through an analyser.
(b) Derive **Brewster's law** $\tan\theta_B = \mu$ and prove that at the polarizing angle the reflected and refracted rays are mutually perpendicular.
(c) Unpolarized light of intensity $I_0$ passes through two Polaroids whose axes are at $60^\circ$. Find the emergent intensity.

**P6 (polarization — wave plates).**
(a) Explain how a **quarter-wave plate** and a **half-wave plate** work; give the thickness condition $t = \dfrac{(2k+1)\lambda}{4(\mu_o - \mu_e)}$ for a quarter-wave plate.
(b) Show how plane-polarized light incident at $45^\circ$ to the optic axis of a quarter-wave plate emerges **circularly polarized**.
(c) Outline how you would experimentally **distinguish** unpolarized, circularly polarized, and elliptically polarized light using a quarter-wave plate and an analyser.

**P7 (coherence — numerical).** A "white-light" source is filtered to a mean wavelength $\lambda = 600\ \text{nm}$ with a spectral width $\Delta\lambda = 3\ \text{nm}$.
(a) Estimate the **coherence length** $L_c$ and **coherence time** $\tau_c$.
(b) Roughly how many fringes can be observed in a Michelson interferometer before they wash out?
(c) State the relation between **fringe visibility** and the degree of coherence, and explain physically why increasing $\Delta\lambda$ reduces the number of visible fringes.

**P8 (Fresnel diffraction — zone plate).** Using the idea of **Fresnel half-period zones**:
(a) Show that for a plane wave the radius of the $n$-th zone on a plane at distance $b$ is $r_n = \sqrt{n\,b\,\lambda}$, and that the zone areas are (to first order) equal.
(b) Explain how a **zone plate** (alternate zones blocked) acts like a converging lens, and derive its principal focal length $f = \dfrac{r_1^2}{\lambda}$ (and the multiple foci $f/3, f/5, \dots$).
(c) Compare the zone plate with a convex lens (chromatic behaviour, multiple foci).

## Worked solutions

### Key formulas used (quote these cold)

$$
\text{Two-beam interference (near-normal thin film): } 2\mu t\cos r = \begin{cases} m\lambda & \text{destructive (reflected)} \\ (m+\tfrac12)\lambda & \text{constructive (reflected)} \end{cases}
$$
$$
\text{Newton's rings (reflection): } r_n^{\text{dark}} = \sqrt{n\lambda R},\qquad r_n^{\text{bright}} = \sqrt{\left(n-\tfrac12\right)\lambda R}
$$
$$
\text{Single slit: } I = I_0\!\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,\ \beta=\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda};\qquad \text{Grating: }(a+b)\sin\theta=m\lambda,\ \ \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}=mN
$$
$$
\text{Malus: } I=I_0\cos^2\theta;\qquad \text{Brewster: } \tan\theta_B=\mu;\qquad \text{QWP: } (\mu_o-\mu_e)t=\lambda/4
$$
$$
\text{Coherence: } L_c \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda},\quad \tau_c=\frac{L_c}{c}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta\nu};\qquad \text{Zones: } r_n=\sqrt{nb\lambda},\ \ f=\frac{r_1^2}{\lambda}
$$

### P1 — Newton's rings

**Geometry.** At radial distance $r$ from the point of contact, the air-film thickness $t$ satisfies (sagitta of the sphere)
$$
r^2 = R^2 - (R-t)^2 = 2Rt - t^2 \approx 2Rt \quad (t\ll R) \;\Rightarrow\; t = \frac{r^2}{2R}.
$$

**Path difference in reflection.** The two interfering beams are (i) reflected from the top (glass→air) and (ii) reflected from the bottom (air→glass) of the film. Reflection at the *air→glass* (lower, denser) surface introduces an extra phase of $\pi$, i.e. an extra path of $\lambda/2$. For near-normal incidence ($\cos r \approx 1$, film index $=1$ for air),
$$
\Delta = 2t + \frac{\lambda}{2}.
$$

**(a) Ring radii.**
*Dark rings (destructive):* $\;2t + \tfrac{\lambda}{2} = (2n+1)\tfrac{\lambda}{2} \Rightarrow 2t = n\lambda \Rightarrow \dfrac{r_n^2}{R}=n\lambda$,
$$
\boxed{\,r_n^{\text{dark}} = \sqrt{n\lambda R}\,},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots
$$
*Bright rings (constructive):* $\;2t + \tfrac{\lambda}{2} = n\lambda \Rightarrow 2t=(n-\tfrac12)\lambda$,
$$
\boxed{\,r_n^{\text{bright}} = \sqrt{\left(n-\tfrac12\right)\lambda R}\,}.
$$
The rings get **closer together** as $n$ grows (radius $\propto\sqrt{n}$), a signature to quote.

**(b) Central spot dark.** At the point of contact $t=0$, so the only path difference is the $\lambda/2$ from the phase change on internal reflection → destructive → **dark centre**. This is direct experimental evidence for the $\pi$ phase change on reflection at a denser medium.

**(c) Liquid film of index $\mu$.** The optical path in the film becomes $2\mu t$, so $2\mu t = n\lambda$ for dark rings and
$$
\boxed{\,r_n^{\text{dark}} = \sqrt{\frac{n\lambda R}{\mu}}\,}.
$$
Radii shrink by $1/\sqrt{\mu}$. Practically, measure $r_n$ for air and for the liquid; since
$$
\mu = \frac{r_n^2(\text{air})}{r_n^2(\text{liquid})} = \frac{D_n^2(\text{air})}{D_n^2(\text{liquid})}
$$
(with $D$ the diameter), the ratio of squared diameters gives $\mu$ without knowing $R$ or $\lambda$. Even cleaner, use two rings: $\mu = \dfrac{D_{n+p}^2(\text{air})-D_n^2(\text{air})}{D_{n+p}^2(\text{liq})-D_n^2(\text{liq})}$, which cancels systematic errors at the centre.

### P2 — Michelson interferometer

**Setup.** A beam splitter (half-silvered plate) at $45^\circ$ divides light into two arms terminating in mirrors $M_1$ (fixed) and $M_2$ (movable on a micrometer). A compensating plate equalizes glass paths. Recombined beams interfere. Optically, $M_1$ and the image $M_2'$ of $M_2$ in the splitter form an **equivalent air film** of thickness $d = $ (arm-length difference).

**Circular fringes (equal inclination).** With $M_1 \parallel M_2'$, the film is of uniform thickness $d$. A ray at inclination $\theta$ to the axis traverses the film with path difference
$$
\Delta = 2d\cos\theta.
$$
Including the $\lambda/2$ from one internal reflection at the splitter (a constant, absorbed into the order), **bright** fringes occur where
$$
\boxed{\,2d\cos\theta = m\lambda\,}.
$$
All rays of a given $\theta$ share the same $\Delta$ → the fringes are **concentric circles** (Haidinger fringes) localized at infinity, viewed with a telescope focused at $\infty$.

**Wavelength from fringe count.** The **centre** corresponds to $\theta=0$: $2d = m\lambda$. As $M_2$ moves by $\Delta d$, the order at the centre changes by $\Delta m$, with one fringe appearing/disappearing per half-wavelength of travel:
$$
2\,\Delta d = \Delta m\,\lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\,\lambda = \frac{2\,\Delta d}{N}\,},\qquad N=\Delta m .
$$
Count $N$ fringes crossing the centre for a measured mirror displacement $\Delta d$ → $\lambda$.

**Refractive index of a thin sheet.** Insert a transparent sheet (thickness $t$, index $\mu$) in one arm. The extra optical path (double pass) is $2(\mu-1)t$, shifting the fringe order by
$$
N = \frac{2(\mu-1)t}{\lambda} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\,\mu = 1 + \frac{N\lambda}{2t}\,}.
$$
Using white light (to locate the zero-order fringe) makes the count unambiguous.

### P3 — Single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction

**(a) Amplitude by integration.** Divide the slit ($0$ to $a$) into elements $dx$. The element at $x$ sends, to a point at angle $\theta$, a wavelet with phase $\phi(x) = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}x\sin\theta$. The resultant amplitude:
$$
E \propto \int_0^a e^{\,i k x\sin\theta}\,dx = \frac{e^{\,ika\sin\theta}-1}{ik\sin\theta},\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.
$$
Its magnitude, with $\beta \equiv \dfrac{ka\sin\theta}{2} = \dfrac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$:
$$
|E| \propto a\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\,I(\theta)=I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\,},\quad \beta=\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
($I_0$ = central intensity, using $\lim_{\beta\to0}\sin\beta/\beta = 1$.)

**(b) Minima and maxima.** $I=0$ when $\sin\beta=0,\ \beta\ne0$, i.e. $\beta = m\pi$:
$$
\boxed{\,a\sin\theta = m\lambda,\quad m=\pm1,\pm2,\dots\,}\quad(\text{minima}).
$$
Secondary maxima occur near $\beta = \tan\beta$, i.e. approximately $\beta \approx \tfrac{3\pi}{2}, \tfrac{5\pi}{2},\dots$, with intensities $\approx 4.5\%,\,1.6\%,\dots$ of $I_0$.
**Central max is twice as wide:** it spans between the first minima at $\beta=\pm\pi$ (i.e. $\sin\theta = \pm\lambda/a$), a full width in $\beta$ of $2\pi$; every secondary maximum lies between consecutive minima separated by only $\Delta\beta=\pi$. Hence the central maximum is **twice as broad** as any side maximum.

**(c) Widths.** Angular half-width of the central maximum (first minimum): $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/a$, so for small angles $\theta_1 \approx \lambda/a$. On a screen at the focal distance $f$ of a lens, the linear half-width is $y_1 = f\lambda/a$, and the **full central width**
$$
\boxed{\,W = \frac{2f\lambda}{a}\,}.
$$
Narrower slit → broader pattern (the diffraction/uncertainty trade-off).

### P4 — Diffraction grating and resolving power

**(a) Grating equation.** For $N$ identical slits (width $a$, opaque gap $b$, period $d=a+b$), the total amplitude is (single-slit diffraction factor) × (N-slit interference factor):
$$
I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)^2,\quad \beta=\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda},\ \ \gamma=\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
**Principal maxima** occur where $\sin\gamma\to0$ with the ratio $\to N$, i.e. $\gamma = m\pi$:
$$
\boxed{\,d\sin\theta = (a+b)\sin\theta = m\lambda\,},\qquad m=0,\pm1,\dots
$$
Physically: successive slits must differ in path by an integer number of wavelengths. Between principal maxima there are $N-1$ minima and $N-2$ weak secondary maxima; larger $N$ → sharper principal maxima.

**(b) Resolving power (Rayleigh).** Two wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda+\Delta\lambda$ are *just resolved* when the principal maximum of one falls on the first adjacent minimum of the other. Adjacent minima of order $m$ satisfy $Nd\sin\theta = (mN\pm1)\lambda$. Differentiating the grating equation at fixed $\theta$ and equating the angular shift of the maximum ($d\cos\theta\,d\theta = m\,\Delta\lambda / \dots$) to the maximum-to-first-minimum spacing gives
$$
\boxed{\,\frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN\,}.
$$
Resolving power = (order) × (total number of rulings). Note it is independent of $d$ — only the *illuminated* line count $N$ and the order $m$ matter.

**(c) Sodium D-lines.** Lines/cm $= 6000/2 = 3000\ \text{cm}^{-1}$; total illuminated lines $N = 6000$. Required resolving power:
$$
\frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{589.0}{0.6} \approx 982.
$$
Available in 2nd order: $mN = 2\times6000 = 12000 \gg 982$. **Yes**, comfortably resolved (even first order, $6000$, suffices). First check the lines are not beyond the grating's angular range: $\sin\theta = m\lambda/d = 2\times589\times10^{-7}/(1/3000)\ \text{cm} = 0.353 < 1$ ✓.

### P5 — Malus's law and Brewster's angle

**(a) Malus's law.** Light emerging from a polarizer is linearly polarized with amplitude $E_0$ along its transmission axis. An analyser at angle $\theta$ transmits only the component along its own axis, $E = E_0\cos\theta$. Since intensity $\propto$ amplitude²,
$$
\boxed{\,I = I_0\cos^2\theta\,}.
$$
(For *unpolarized* light incident on the first Polaroid, the average $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$, so the first Polaroid halves the intensity.)

**(b) Brewster's law.** At the polarizing angle $\theta_B$, the reflected light is completely plane-polarized (perpendicular to the plane of incidence). This happens when the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular: $\theta_B + r = 90^\circ \Rightarrow r = 90^\circ-\theta_B$. Snell's law:
$$
\mu = \frac{\sin\theta_B}{\sin r} = \frac{\sin\theta_B}{\sin(90^\circ-\theta_B)} = \frac{\sin\theta_B}{\cos\theta_B}\;\Rightarrow\;\boxed{\,\tan\theta_B=\mu\,}.
$$
Physical reason: at $\theta_B$ the reflected direction coincides with the dipole-oscillation axis of the refracted-wave charges for the parallel polarization, and dipoles do not radiate along their own axis → the parallel component vanishes in reflection.

**(c) Numerical.** Unpolarized $I_0$ through the first Polaroid → $I_0/2$. Through the second at $60^\circ$:
$$
I = \frac{I_0}{2}\cos^2 60^\circ = \frac{I_0}{2}\cdot\frac14 = \boxed{\frac{I_0}{8}}.
$$

### P6 — Wave plates and analysis of polarized light

**(a) Principle.** A birefringent plate cut with its optic axis in the face resolves incident light into ordinary ($\mu_o$) and extraordinary ($\mu_e$) components travelling with different speeds. After thickness $t$ they acquire a phase difference
$$
\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)\,t.
$$
- **Quarter-wave plate:** $\delta = \pi/2 \Rightarrow (\mu_o-\mu_e)t = \lambda/4$, i.e. $\boxed{t = \dfrac{(2k+1)\lambda}{4(\mu_o-\mu_e)}}$.
- **Half-wave plate:** $\delta = \pi \Rightarrow (\mu_o-\mu_e)t = \lambda/2$; it rotates the plane of linearly polarized light by $2\alpha$ (where $\alpha$ = angle between polarization and optic axis).

**(b) Circular from linear.** Let plane-polarized light of amplitude $A$ hit the QWP with polarization at $45^\circ$ to the optic axis. The two components are equal: $A_o = A_e = A/\sqrt2$. After the QWP they differ in phase by $\pi/2$:
$$
E_e = \frac{A}{\sqrt2}\cos\omega t,\qquad E_o = \frac{A}{\sqrt2}\cos\!\left(\omega t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{A}{\sqrt2}\sin\omega t.
$$
Then $E_e^2 + E_o^2 = A^2/2 = $ const → the tip of $\mathbf E$ traces a **circle** → **circularly polarized** light. (At any other angle $\alpha\ne45^\circ$ the amplitudes are unequal → **elliptical**.)

**(c) Distinguishing three types.** First pass the beam through a rotating **analyser** alone:

| Behaviour with analyser alone | Then insert QWP *before* analyser, rotate analyser |
|---|---|
| Intensity constant | Unpolarized → *stays constant*. Circular → QWP converts to **linear**, so now shows full extinction. |
| Intensity varies but **never zero** | Elliptical (no QWP needed to see variation); with QWP oriented along an axis of the ellipse it becomes linear → full extinction. |

So: **variation with complete extinction (no plate)** = linear; **no variation (no plate)** = unpolarized *or* circular — insert QWP: if extinction now appears it was **circular**, if still no variation it was **unpolarized**; **variation without extinction (no plate)** = elliptical *or* partially polarized — QWP + analyser gives extinction only for elliptical.

### P7 — Coherence (numerical)

**(a) Coherence length and time.**
$$
L_c \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{(600\times10^{-9})^2}{3\times10^{-9}} = \frac{3.6\times10^{-13}}{3\times10^{-9}} = 1.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = \boxed{0.12\ \text{mm}}.
$$
$$
\tau_c = \frac{L_c}{c} = \frac{1.2\times10^{-4}}{3\times10^8} = \boxed{4.0\times10^{-13}\ \text{s}} \ (0.4\ \text{ps}).
$$
(Equivalently $\Delta\nu = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2 \approx 2.5\times10^{12}\ \text{Hz}$ and $\tau_c \approx 1/\Delta\nu$.)

**(b) Number of visible fringes.** Fringes remain visible while the path difference $\lesssim L_c$. In a Michelson interferometer the order is $m = 2d/\lambda$; contrast dies when $2d \approx L_c$, so
$$
N_{\max} \approx \frac{L_c}{\lambda} = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{600}{3} = \boxed{200 \text{ fringes}}.
$$

**(c) Visibility.** Fringe visibility $V = \dfrac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}} = |\gamma(\tau)|$, the modulus of the complex degree of (temporal) coherence. For a finite bandwidth, $|\gamma(\tau)|$ falls from $1$ (at zero path difference) to $0$ over $\tau \sim \tau_c$. **Physically:** a broader $\Delta\lambda$ means many wavelengths superpose; each produces its own fringe spacing, and once the path difference exceeds $\sim\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$ the bright fringes of some wavelengths fall on the dark fringes of others → they **wash out**. Wider spectrum ⇒ shorter coherence length ⇒ fewer visible fringes. (Spatial coherence, governed by source size via the van Cittert–Zernike theorem, is the companion concept for two-slit visibility.)

### P8 — Fresnel zone plate

**(a) Zone radii.** For a plane wavefront, consider the observation point $P$ at distance $b$ from the wavefront plane. Divide the wavefront into annular zones such that the path from the edge of the $n$-th zone to $P$ exceeds the axial path $b$ by $n\lambda/2$:
$$
\sqrt{r_n^2 + b^2} = b + \frac{n\lambda}{2}.
$$
Squaring and dropping the $(\,n\lambda/2)^2$ term ($r_n, n\lambda \ll b$):
$$
r_n^2 = n b \lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\,r_n = \sqrt{n b \lambda}\,}.
$$
Zone areas: $A_n = \pi(r_n^2 - r_{n-1}^2) = \pi b\lambda$ — **independent of $n$**, so (to first order) all zones have equal area and contribute nearly equal amplitude, alternating in phase by $\pi$. Consecutive zones' contributions nearly cancel; the resultant of the full wave is about **half** the first zone's alone.

**(b) Zone plate as a lens.** Block every alternate zone (say all even zones). Then the remaining odd zones all arrive **in phase** at $P$, and their amplitudes add constructively → a bright, intense focus. This is exactly focusing. From $r_1^2 = b\lambda$ with $b=f$ the principal focus:
$$
\boxed{\,f = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda}\,},\qquad \text{more generally } f_p = \frac{r_1^2}{(2p-1)\lambda},\ p=1,2,\dots
$$
so there are **multiple foci** at $f, f/3, f/5,\dots$ (subsidiary foci where each open zone spans an odd number of half-period zones). Analogous imaging relation: $\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f$ with $f = r_n^2/(n\lambda)$.

**(c) Zone plate vs convex lens.**
- Both converge light, but the zone plate works by **diffraction** (blocking/phase-shifting) not refraction.
- Zone plate has **multiple real (and virtual) foci**; a lens has one.
- **Chromatic behaviour is opposite in sign and stronger:** since $f = r_1^2/\lambda \propto 1/\lambda$, **red focuses nearer** than blue for a zone plate — the reverse of a simple glass lens and with larger dispersion.
- A **phase (Rayleigh–Wood) zone plate**, which shifts alternate zones by $\pi$ instead of blocking them, is ~4× brighter because no light is thrown away.

## Recurring-pattern insights

1. **"Derive the path difference, then apply the condition" is the whole game.** Nearly every interference/thin-film question (Newton's rings, Michelson, wedge film, Fabry–Perot) reduces to writing $\Delta = 2\mu t\cos r \pm \lambda/2$ correctly — the *only* subtle points UPSC tests are (i) the extra $\lambda/2$ from reflection at the denser medium and (ii) the $\cos r$ (or $\cos\theta$) obliquity factor. Get those two right and the ring/fringe formula follows mechanically. The single most common lost mark is dropping or mis-placing the $\lambda/2$ (which is exactly why the Newton's-rings *centre is dark* — a favourite one-line viva/short-note question).

2. **Resolving power vs dispersive power are examined as a pair — keep them distinct.** For the grating, *dispersive* power $\dfrac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \dfrac{m}{d\cos\theta}$ (how far apart two lines land) is not the same as *resolving* power $\dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}=mN$ (whether they can be told apart). UPSC loves a numerical that gives grating width and lines/cm and asks "can it resolve the Na doublet?" — the answer hinges on $mN$, not on $d$. The identical Rayleigh-criterion logic recurs for the **telescope** ($\theta_{\min}=1.22\lambda/D$) and **microscope** ($d_{\min}=0.61\lambda/\text{NA}$); learn the one criterion, apply it three ways.

3. **Polarization questions cluster into a fixed menu.** Malus ($\cos^2\theta$), Brewster ($\tan\theta_B=\mu$ with the perpendicular reflected/refracted rays), and wave-plate phase retardation ($\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o-\mu_e)t$) between them cover almost every Paper I polarization item, usually with a short numerical (two Polaroids at an angle) attached. The one genuinely *conceptual* recurring ask is the **experimental scheme to distinguish circular/elliptical/unpolarized light using a quarter-wave plate + analyser** — memorize the decision table in P6(c); it is asked as a short note surprisingly often.

**Tip for this week:** Build a **one-page "Optics constants & criteria" card** and drill it to instant recall — the $\lambda/2$ reflection rule, $r_n=\sqrt{n\lambda R}$, $a\sin\theta=m\lambda$ (single-slit minima) vs $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$ (grating maxima — students routinely swap these), $\lambda/\Delta\lambda=mN$, $1.22\lambda/D$, $\tan\theta_B=\mu$, QWP condition, $L_c=\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$, $r_n=\sqrt{nb\lambda}$. Optics marks in Paper I are won on *clean diagrams + the right standard result quoted fast*, not on invention.
