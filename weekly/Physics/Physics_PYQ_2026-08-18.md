## Worked solutions

### P1 — Term symbols, Hund's rules, Landé $g$

**(a) $3d^2$: enumerating the terms.**

Two equivalent $d$ electrons: $l_1 = l_2 = 2$, $s_1 = s_2 = \tfrac12$. Unrestricted addition gives $L = 0,1,2,3,4$ and $S = 0,1$, i.e. ten combinations — but the electrons are **equivalent** (same $n$, same $l$), so the Pauli principle kills half of them. The surviving rule for two equivalent electrons is that **$L+S$ must be even**:

$$\boxed{\ ^1S,\ ^3P,\ ^1D,\ ^3F,\ ^1G\ }$$

*Check by state counting:* the number of ways to put 2 electrons in the 10 $d$-spin-orbitals is $\binom{10}{2} = 45$. Degeneracies: $^1S = 1$, $^3P = 3\times3 = 9$, $^1D = 5$, $^3F = 3\times7 = 21$, $^1G = 9$. Sum $= 1+9+5+21+9 = 45$ ✓. **Always show this count — UPSC gives a mark for it and it catches enumeration errors.**

**Hund's rules applied.** (i) Maximum multiplicity: $S = 1$ triplets win → $^3P$ or $^3F$. (ii) For the same $S$, maximum $L$: $^3F$ ($L=3$) beats $^3P$ ($L=1$). (iii) The $d$ shell holds 10 electrons; $3d^2$ is **less than half full**, so the **lowest $J$** lies lowest. For $^3F$: $J = |3-1| \dots 3+1 = 2, 3, 4$, so $J = 2$.

$$\boxed{\text{Ground term of } 3d^2 = {}^3F_2}$$

*Physical reasons (state these, don't just quote the rules):* max $S$ ⇒ symmetric spin function ⇒ antisymmetric spatial function ⇒ electrons avoid each other ⇒ smaller Coulomb repulsion. Max $L$ ⇒ electrons orbit in the same sense, meeting less often ⇒ again less repulsion. The $J$ rule follows from the sign of the spin–orbit constant $\zeta$, which is positive for a less-than-half-filled shell and negative for a more-than-half-filled one.

**(b) Landé $g$ of $^3F_2$.** Here $L=3$, $S=1$, $J=2$:

$$g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)} = 1 + \frac{6 + 2 - 12}{12} = 1 - \frac{4}{12} = \boxed{\frac{2}{3}}$$

**(c) $3d^8$.** By the **electron–hole equivalence**, two holes in a $d$ shell generate exactly the same list of terms: $^1S, ^3P, ^1D, ^3F, ^1G$, and Hund's first two rules again select $^3F$. But $3d^8$ is **more than half filled**, so $\zeta < 0$ and the **highest** $J$ lies lowest:

$$\boxed{\text{Ground term of } 3d^8 = {}^3F_4}, \qquad g = 1 + \frac{20+2-12}{40} = 1 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$$

The multiplet is **inverted** — this is exactly the "explain why Ni ($3d^8$) has an inverted multiplet" question UPSC likes.

**(d) Carbon, $2p^2$.** Two equivalent $p$ electrons, $l = 1$: the $L+S$ even rule gives

$$^1S,\ ^3P,\ ^1D \qquad (\text{count: } 1 + 9 + 5 = 15 = \tbinom{6}{2}\ ✓)$$

Hund: max $S$ → $^3P$; less than half filled ($p$ holds 6) → lowest $J$. $J = 0,1,2$, so

$$\boxed{\text{Ground term of C} = {}^3P_0}$$

Note $J = 0$: **carbon's ground state shows no Zeeman splitting at all** ($g$ is indeterminate and there is a single $m_J = 0$ state). Oxygen, $2p^4$ (more than half full), gives $^3P_2$ — inverted. This C-vs-O contrast is a favourite one-line UPSC question.

### P2 — Spin–orbit coupling and the sodium D doublet

**Derivation.** In the electron's rest frame the nucleus of charge $Ze$ orbits it, producing a magnetic field. From the Biot–Savart law with $\mathbf{v}$ the electron velocity,

$$\mathbf{B} = -\frac{1}{c^2}\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{mc^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\mathbf{L}, \qquad \mathbf{L} = m\,\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}$$

using $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V/(-e) = (\mathbf{r}/r)(1/e)(dV/dr)$ for a central potential. The electron's spin magnetic moment is $\boldsymbol{\mu}_s = -\dfrac{g_s e}{2m}\mathbf{S} \simeq -\dfrac{e}{m}\mathbf{S}$ (since $g_s \simeq 2$), so the interaction energy $-\boldsymbol{\mu}_s\cdot\mathbf{B}$ becomes

$$H_{so} = \frac{1}{m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S} \;\xrightarrow{\ \times\frac12\ \text{Thomas}\ }\; \boxed{H_{so} = \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\ \mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}}$$

The factor $\tfrac12$ is the **Thomas precession** correction, arising because the electron's rest frame is non-inertial (rotating). *Always mention it by name — it is a standing UPSC one-liner.*

**Evaluating $\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}$.** Since $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}$,

$$J^2 = L^2 + S^2 + 2\,\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S} \Rightarrow \mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S} = \tfrac12\left(J^2 - L^2 - S^2\right) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1)\right]$$

so with $\zeta_{nl} \equiv \dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m^2c^2}\left\langle \dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{dV}{dr}\right\rangle$,

$$\boxed{E_{so} = \frac{\zeta_{nl}}{2}\left[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1)\right]}$$

**Splitting into two, separation $\propto (2l+1)$.** For a single electron $s = \tfrac12$, so $j = l \pm \tfrac12$ — exactly **two** levels (except $l=0$, where $j = \tfrac12$ only and there is no splitting). Then

$$E_{j=l+\frac12} = \frac{\zeta}{2}\,l, \qquad E_{j=l-\frac12} = -\frac{\zeta}{2}(l+1)$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{\zeta}{2}\left[l + l + 1\right] = \boxed{\frac{\zeta}{2}(2l+1)}$$

which is the Landé interval rule in its one-electron form. Since $\zeta \propto Z^4/[n^3 l(l+\frac12)(l+1)]$, fine structure grows steeply with $Z$ — the reason Na's doublet (6 Å) is wide while Li's is barely resolvable and Cs's is enormous.

**Sodium D lines.** The valence electron is $3s\ (^2S_{1/2})$. Excitation to $3p$ gives $^2P_{1/2}$ and $^2P_{3/2}$; the two allowed transitions ($\Delta l = -1$, $\Delta J = 0, \pm1$) are

- $D_1$: $^2P_{1/2} \to {}^2S_{1/2}$ at 5896 Å
- $D_2$: $^2P_{3/2} \to {}^2S_{1/2}$ at 5890 Å

The lower level $^2S_{1/2}$ has $l=0$ and is unsplit, so **the entire doublet separation is the $3p$ fine structure.**

**Numerical.** Working in wavenumbers avoids all constants:

$$\Delta\tilde\nu = \frac{1}{\lambda_1} - \frac{1}{\lambda_2} \simeq \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda^2} = \frac{6\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{cm}}{(5.89\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm})^2} = \frac{6\times10^{-8}}{3.469\times10^{-9}} = \boxed{17.3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}}$$

In energy: $\Delta E = hc\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2 = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\,nm})(0.6\ \mathrm{nm})/(589\ \mathrm{nm})^2 = \boxed{2.14\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}}$ (2.1 meV).

**Coupling constant.** For $l = 1$, $\Delta E = \tfrac{\zeta}{2}(2l+1) = \tfrac{3}{2}\zeta$, hence

$$\zeta_{3p} = \tfrac{2}{3}(17.3) = \boxed{11.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}} = 1.43\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$$

*Sanity check:* the accepted Na D separation is 17.2 cm$^{-1}$ ✓.

### P3 — Zeeman effect, Landé factor, and the full D-line pattern

**Normal vs anomalous.** The *normal* Zeeman effect is the splitting of a line into a **triplet** (one $\pi$ component undisplaced, two $\sigma$ components at $\pm \mu_B B/hc$) with the classical Lorentz displacement. It occurs **only between singlet levels** ($S = 0$), where $g_J = 1$ for both levels and the spin plays no role. The *anomalous* effect — actually the general case — occurs whenever $S \neq 0$: because $g$ differs between the upper and lower levels, the pattern has more than three components and non-classical spacings. Historically the anomalous effect was the direct experimental evidence for **electron spin**.

**Derivation of $g_J$ (vector atom model).** The magnetic moment of the atom is

$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\left(\mathbf{L} + g_s \mathbf{S}\right) \simeq -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\left(\mathbf{L} + 2\mathbf{S}\right) = -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\left(\mathbf{J} + \mathbf{S}\right)$$

Because $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ precess rapidly about $\mathbf{J}$ (LS coupling assumed strong compared with the field), only the component of $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ **along $\mathbf{J}$** survives the time average — the projection theorem:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eff}} = \frac{(\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{J})}{J^2}\,\mathbf{J} = -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\left[1 + \frac{\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{J}}{J^2}\right]\mathbf{J}$$

Now $\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{J} = \tfrac12(J^2 + S^2 - L^2)$, so

$$\boxed{g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}}$$

and $\Delta E = -\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{eff}}\cdot\mathbf{B} = g_J\,\mu_B B\, m_J$. Note the limits: $S=0 \Rightarrow g = 1$ (normal); $L=0 \Rightarrow g = 2$ (pure spin).

**Displacement unit at 1 T.**

$$\frac{\mu_B B}{hc} = \frac{9.274\times10^{-24}}{(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{10})} = \boxed{0.467\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}\ T^{-1}}}$$

equivalently $\mu_B B/h = 14.0$ GHz T$^{-1}$, or $5.79\times10^{-5}$ eV T$^{-1}$. **Memorise 0.467 cm$^{-1}$/T — it converts every Zeeman question into arithmetic.**

**$g$-factors of the sodium levels.**

$$g(^2S_{1/2}) = 1 + \frac{\tfrac34 + \tfrac34 - 0}{2\cdot\tfrac34} = 2, \quad g(^2P_{1/2}) = 1 + \frac{\tfrac34+\tfrac34-2}{\tfrac32} = \frac{2}{3}, \quad g(^2P_{3/2}) = 1 + \frac{\tfrac{15}{4}+\tfrac34-2}{\tfrac{15}{2}} = \frac{4}{3}$$

**$D_1$ pattern ($^2P_{1/2} \to {}^2S_{1/2}$).** Upper: $g m = \pm\tfrac13$. Lower: $g m = \pm 1$. Selection rule $\Delta m_J = 0, \pm1$ permits all four combinations. Shifts $(g_u m_u - g_l m_l)$ in units of $\mu_B B$:

| $m_u \to m_l$ | shift | type |
|---|---|---|
| $+\tfrac12 \to +\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac13 - 1 = -\tfrac23$ | $\pi$ |
| $-\tfrac12 \to -\tfrac12$ | $-\tfrac13 + 1 = +\tfrac23$ | $\pi$ |
| $+\tfrac12 \to -\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac13 + 1 = +\tfrac43$ | $\sigma$ |
| $-\tfrac12 \to +\tfrac12$ | $-\tfrac13 - 1 = -\tfrac43$ | $\sigma$ |

$$\boxed{D_1: \text{4 components at } \pm\tfrac23,\ \pm\tfrac43 \text{ in units of } \mu_B B \Rightarrow \pm 0.311,\ \pm 0.623\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} \text{ at 1 T}}$$

**$D_2$ pattern ($^2P_{3/2} \to {}^2S_{1/2}$).** Upper $g m = \pm2, \pm\tfrac23$; lower $\pm1$. Allowed transitions give shifts $\pm\tfrac13, \pm 1, \pm\tfrac53$:

$$\boxed{D_2: \text{6 components at } \pm\tfrac13,\ \pm1,\ \pm\tfrac53 \Rightarrow \pm0.156,\ \pm0.467,\ \pm0.778\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} \text{ at 1 T}}$$

Neither is a triplet — hence "anomalous." The 4-and-6 count for the Na D lines is the single most-asked Zeeman numerical; know it cold.

**Strong fields — Paschen–Back effect.** When $\mu_B B \gg$ the fine-structure splitting (for Na, $\zeta \approx 11.5$ cm$^{-1}$, so we need $B \gg 11.5/0.467 \approx 25$ T), the field decouples $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$; they precess independently about $\mathbf{B}$ and $J$ ceases to be a good quantum number. Then

$$\Delta E = \mu_B B\,(m_l + 2 m_s)$$

and with the rules $\Delta m_l = 0, \pm1$, $\Delta m_s = 0$, the anomalous pattern **collapses back to a normal triplet**. This "anomalous at low field → normal at high field" transition is the standard 5-mark rider.

### P4 — Linear Stark effect in hydrogen

**Why the ground state has no linear effect.** The perturbation is $H' = e\mathcal{E}z$ (field along $z$; the electron charge is $-e$ so $H' = +e\mathcal{E}z$ up to sign convention). The first-order shift is

$$E^{(1)} = \langle 100 | e\mathcal{E}z | 100\rangle$$

The state $|100\rangle$ has definite **parity** (even), while $z$ is odd. The integrand is odd, so the integral vanishes:

$$\boxed{E^{(1)}_{1s} = 0}$$

Physically: a non-degenerate, parity-definite state has **no permanent electric dipole moment**; it can only be polarised, giving a *quadratic* (second-order) shift $\Delta E = -\tfrac12\alpha \mathcal{E}^2$ with $\alpha = \tfrac92 4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0^3$ for hydrogen.

**Why $n = 2$ is different.** The $n=2$ level is **4-fold degenerate** — $|200\rangle$ ($2s$), $|210\rangle$, $|21{\pm}1\rangle$ ($2p$) — and $2s$ and $2p$ have **opposite parity**. Degenerate perturbation theory requires diagonalising $H'$ within the degenerate subspace.

**Which matrix elements survive.** Two selection rules kill almost everything:
- $z$ commutes with $L_z$, so $\langle n l m | z | n l' m'\rangle = 0$ unless $m = m'$. This forbids anything connecting $|21{\pm}1\rangle$ to the others.
- $z$ is odd, so we need $\Delta l = \pm 1$ (parity). This forbids $\langle 200|z|200\rangle$ and $\langle 210|z|210\rangle$.

Only $\langle 200 | z | 210\rangle$ survives. Using $\psi_{200} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{32\pi a_0^3}}\left(2 - \frac{r}{a_0}\right)e^{-r/2a_0}$ and $\psi_{210} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{32\pi a_0^3}}\frac{r}{a_0}e^{-r/2a_0}\cos\theta$, the angular part gives $\int \cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3$ and the radial integral evaluates to

$$\langle 200 | z | 210\rangle = -3a_0$$

**Diagonalising.** In the $\{|200\rangle, |210\rangle\}$ block,

$$H' = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -3ea_0\mathcal{E} \\ -3ea_0\mathcal{E} & 0\end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow \text{eigenvalues } \mp 3ea_0\mathcal{E}, \quad \text{eigenvectors } \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|200\rangle \pm |210\rangle\right)$$

$$\boxed{E^{(1)} = \pm\,3 e a_0 \mathcal{E}\ \ (\text{one each}), \qquad E^{(1)} = 0 \ \ (\text{twofold, } m = \pm1)}$$

So the 4-fold degenerate $n=2$ level splits into **three** levels with degeneracies 1, 2, 1 — total splitting $6ea_0\mathcal{E}$. The shift is **linear** in $\mathcal{E}$ because the mixed states $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|200\rangle \pm |210\rangle)$ are parity-indefinite and therefore carry a **permanent dipole moment** of magnitude $3ea_0$. The $m = \pm1$ states remain unshifted to first order — their residual degeneracy is protected by the rotational symmetry that survives about the field axis.

**Numerical.** With $\mathcal{E} = 10^7\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ and $a_0 = 5.292\times10^{-11}$ m,

$$3 e a_0 \mathcal{E} = 3(1.602\times10^{-19})(5.292\times10^{-11})(10^7) = 2.54\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{J} = \boxed{1.59\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}}$$

Total splitting between the outer levels $= 6ea_0\mathcal{E} = 3.18$ meV $\approx 25.6\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. *Note this is comparable to the hydrogen fine structure, so at this field the two effects must be treated together — a good closing remark for full marks.*

### P5 — Moseley's law, screening, and the Auger process

**Origin of characteristic X-rays.** A high-energy electron ejects an inner-shell electron; an outer electron drops into the vacancy, emitting a photon of energy equal to the level difference. Vacancy in $K$ ($n=1$) → the $K$ series; the $L\to K$ transition is $K_\alpha$, $M\to K$ is $K_\beta$, etc.

**Derivation.** Treat the transition hydrogen-like but with the nuclear charge **screened** by the remaining inner electrons, $Z \to (Z-\sigma)$. Then

$$\tilde\nu = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R(Z-\sigma)^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$$

Taking the square root,

$$\boxed{\sqrt{\nu} = a\,(Z - \sigma), \qquad a = \sqrt{cR\left(\tfrac{1}{n_1^2} - \tfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)}}$$

A plot of $\sqrt{\nu}$ against $Z$ is a straight line — Moseley's great result, which established that **atomic number, not atomic weight, orders the periodic table**, and which predicted the then-missing elements $Z = 43, 61, 72, 75$.

For $K_\alpha$ ($n_1 = 1 \to n_2 = 2$): $\ \tilde\nu = \tfrac34 R(Z-1)^2$.

**Why $\sigma \approx 1$ for $K_\alpha$.** After one $K$ electron is ejected, exactly **one** electron remains in the $K$ shell, and it screens the nucleus from the $L$ electron making the jump; hence $\sigma \approx 1$. For $L_\alpha$ ($M \to L$), the jumping electron is screened by the 2 $K$ electrons plus most of the remaining $L$ electrons, giving the empirical $\sigma \approx 7.4$. **The screening constant is not a fudge factor — it counts the electrons inside the orbit of the transitioning electron.**

**Numerical: $K_\alpha$ of molybdenum, $Z = 42$.**

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac34 R (Z-1)^2 = 0.75 \times (1.097\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}) \times (41)^2 = 0.75 \times 1.097\times10^7 \times 1681 = 1.383\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$$

$$\lambda = 7.23\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} = \boxed{0.723\ \text{Å}}$$

Photon energy $= 1240/0.0723\ \mathrm{nm} \approx 17.1$ keV. *Sanity check:* the tabulated Mo $K_\alpha$ is 0.710 Å / 17.5 keV — the 2% discrepancy comes from ignoring the $2p$ fine structure ($K_{\alpha_1}/K_{\alpha_2}$) and from $\sigma$ not being exactly 1. **Say this in the answer; UPSC rewards the error estimate.**

**Auger effect and fluorescence yield.** The energy released when the outer electron fills the vacancy need not appear as a photon: it can instead be transferred **radiationlessly** to another outer electron, which is ejected with kinetic energy

$$E_{\text{Auger}} = E_K - E_L - E_{M}$$

(the ejected "Auger electron" leaves a **doubly** ionised atom). The competition is quantified by the **fluorescence yield**

$$\omega_K = \frac{\text{number of } K \text{ X-ray photons emitted}}{\text{number of } K \text{ vacancies created}}$$

$\omega_K$ is small ($\lesssim 0.1$) for light elements — Auger emission dominates — and approaches 1 for heavy elements, because the radiative rate scales roughly as $Z^4$ while the Auger rate is nearly $Z$-independent. Key contrasts to state: characteristic X-rays give a **line spectrum with $Z$-dependent energies**; Auger electrons have energies **independent of the incident beam energy** (unlike photoelectrons), which is what makes Auger spectroscopy a surface-composition probe.

### P6 — Rigid rotor: CO bond length

**Setting up.** Two masses $m_1, m_2$ separated by a fixed distance $r$ rotate about the centre of mass. The two-body problem reduces to a single particle of **reduced mass** $\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ at distance $r$, with moment of inertia

$$I = m_1 r_1^2 + m_2 r_2^2 = \mu r^2$$

The Hamiltonian is purely rotational, $H = L^2/2I$, whose eigenvalues are already known from angular momentum theory:

$$E_J = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I}J(J+1), \qquad J = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Each level is $(2J+1)$-fold degenerate in $m_J$. In spectroscopists' wavenumber units,

$$\boxed{F(J) = \frac{E_J}{hc} = B\,J(J+1), \qquad B = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 c I}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}}$$

**Spectrum.** The dipole selection rule for a rotating permanent dipole is $\Delta J = +1$ in absorption, so

$$\tilde\nu_{J\to J+1} = B(J+1)(J+2) - BJ(J+1) = \boxed{2B(J+1)}$$

giving lines at $2B, 4B, 6B, \dots$ — **equally spaced by $2B$**, with the first line at $2B$ (not at the origin). This equal spacing is the experimental signature of rigid rotation.

**Numerical for $^{12}$C$^{16}$O.** Observed spacing $2B = 3.8626\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} \Rightarrow B = 1.9313\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.

$$I = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 c B} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{(78.957)(2.998\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}})(1.9313\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}})}$$

Denominator $= 78.957 \times 5.790\times10^{10} = 4.572\times10^{12}$, so

$$\boxed{I = 1.449\times10^{-46}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}}$$

Reduced mass: $\mu = \dfrac{12 \times 16}{12+16} = 6.857$ u $= 6.857 \times 1.6605\times10^{-27} = 1.1385\times10^{-26}$ kg.

$$r = \sqrt{\frac{I}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.449\times10^{-46}}{1.1385\times10^{-26}}} = \sqrt{1.273\times10^{-20}} = 1.128\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m} = \boxed{1.128\ \text{Å}}$$

*Sanity check:* the accepted CO bond length is 1.128 Å ✓. **Watch the units:** $c$ must be in cm s$^{-1}$ when $B$ is in cm$^{-1}$. This is where most candidates lose the numerical.

**Centrifugal distortion.** A real bond stretches as $J$ increases, so $I$ grows and the effective $B$ falls. Treating the bond as a spring gives

$$F(J) = BJ(J+1) - D\,J^2(J+1)^2, \qquad D = \frac{4B^3}{\omega_e^2} \ (\ll B)$$

Lines therefore appear at $\tilde\nu = 2B(J+1) - 4D(J+1)^3$: **the spacing shrinks slightly at high $J$** and the spectrum converges rather than staying uniform. Because $D \propto 1/\omega_e^2$, a stiff bond distorts less.

**Why N$_2$ has no pure rotational spectrum.** Pure rotational absorption requires an oscillating dipole in the laboratory frame, i.e. a **permanent electric dipole moment**. Homonuclear diatomics (N$_2$, O$_2$, H$_2$, Cl$_2$) are symmetric, $\mu_{\text{el}} = 0$, and rotation produces no oscillating dipole — hence they are **microwave-inactive**. CO, with its small but non-zero dipole (0.11 D), is active. **However, N$_2$ *is* rotational-Raman active** (see P8), because Raman requires only anisotropic **polarisability**, which every diatomic has. This IR-inactive-but-Raman-active pairing is a favourite UPSC rider — carry it into P8.

### P7 — Anharmonicity, the Morse potential, and HCl

**The Morse potential.** The harmonic oscillator is wrong at large $r$ (it never dissociates) and at small $r$ (it is too soft against nuclear repulsion). Morse's empirical form fixes both:

$$\boxed{V(r) = D_e\left[1 - e^{-\beta(r - r_e)}\right]^2}$$

with $V(r_e) = 0$ (minimum), $V(\infty) = D_e$ (dissociation), and $V \to$ large as $r \to 0$. Expanding about $r_e$: $V \approx D_e\beta^2(r-r_e)^2$, so the harmonic force constant is $k = 2D_e\beta^2$.

**Term values.** Solving the Schrödinger equation with the Morse potential gives *exactly*

$$\boxed{G(v) = \omega_e\left(v + \tfrac12\right) - \omega_e x_e\left(v+\tfrac12\right)^2}, \qquad v = 0,1,2,\dots$$

The negative quadratic term means the levels **converge** as $v$ increases, meeting at dissociation. Anharmonicity also relaxes the harmonic selection rule from $\Delta v = \pm1$ to $\Delta v = \pm1, \pm2, \pm3,\dots$, with rapidly falling intensity — this is what makes overtones observable at all.

**(i) Fundamental band ($v = 0 \to 1$).**

$$\tilde\nu = G(1) - G(0) = \left[\tfrac32\omega_e - \tfrac94\omega_ex_e\right] - \left[\tfrac12\omega_e - \tfrac14\omega_ex_e\right] = \omega_e - 2\omega_e x_e$$

$$= 2989.7 - 2(52.05) = 2989.7 - 104.1 = \boxed{2885.6\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}}$$

*(observed: 2885.9 cm$^{-1}$ ✓)*

**(ii) First overtone ($v = 0 \to 2$).**

$$\tilde\nu = G(2) - G(0) = 2\omega_e - 6\omega_e x_e = 5979.4 - 312.3 = \boxed{5667.1\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}}$$

*(observed: 5668 cm$^{-1}$ ✓)*. Note it is slightly **less** than twice the fundamental — the classic experimental signature of anharmonicity.

**(iii) Dissociation energies.** The levels stop when the spacing vanishes: $dG/dv = 0$ at $v_{\max} + \tfrac12 = \omega_e/2\omega_e x_e$, giving

$$D_e = G(v_{\max}) = \frac{\omega_e^2}{4\omega_e x_e} = \frac{(2989.7)^2}{4(52.05)} = \frac{8.9383\times10^6}{208.2} = \boxed{4.293\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 5.32\ \mathrm{eV}}$$

The **measurable** (spectroscopic) dissociation energy is measured from the $v=0$ level, not from the potential minimum:

$$D_0 = D_e - G(0) = D_e - \frac{\omega_e}{2} + \frac{\omega_e x_e}{4} = 42931 - 1494.9 + 13.0 = \boxed{4.145\times10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 5.14\ \mathrm{eV}}$$

The difference $G(0) = 1482$ cm$^{-1}$ is the **zero-point energy** — always state that $D_0 < D_e$ by exactly the ZPE.

**Comment on accuracy.** The experimental $D_0$ for HCl is 4.43 eV, so the Morse/Birge–Sponer estimate **overestimates by ~16%**. Reason: the formula extrapolates the *quadratic* term-value expression all the way to dissociation, but real potentials flatten faster than Morse near the asymptote, so the true levels converge sooner. **Always flag this** — a numerical answer of 5.14 eV presented as exact loses marks; presented with this caveat it gains them.

**P and R branches.** At room temperature the vibrational transition is accompanied by a rotational change, $\Delta J = \pm1$. Writing $S(v,J) = G(v) + BJ(J+1)$ (taking $B' \approx B''= B$ for the simple treatment):

- **R branch** ($\Delta J = +1$, $J \to J+1$): $\ \tilde\nu_R = \tilde\nu_0 + 2B(J+1)$, $J = 0,1,2,\dots$ — lines **above** the band origin
- **P branch** ($\Delta J = -1$, $J \to J-1$): $\ \tilde\nu_P = \tilde\nu_0 - 2BJ$, $J = 1,2,3,\dots$ — lines **below** the band origin

giving two combs of lines spaced $2B$, separated by a **gap of $4B$** at the centre where the band origin $\tilde\nu_0$ sits.

**Why no Q branch.** A $Q$ branch would be $\Delta J = 0$. For a diatomic in a $^1\Sigma$ state the molecule has **no angular momentum about the internuclear axis** ($\Lambda = 0$), and the photon must carry off one unit of angular momentum, so $\Delta J = 0$ is forbidden — hence the empty centre. (A $Q$ branch *does* appear for molecules with $\Lambda \neq 0$, e.g. NO in a $^2\Pi$ state, and for perpendicular bands of polyatomics — worth one sentence.)

**Refinement worth a mark:** in reality $B' < B''$ because the bond is longer in $v=1$. This makes the R-branch lines converge and the P-branch lines diverge, producing the characteristic **band head** on the R side.

### P8 — Raman effect

**Classical theory.** An incident field $E = E_0\cos(2\pi\nu_0 t)$ induces a dipole in the molecule,

$$p = \alpha E$$

If the molecule vibrates at frequency $\nu_{\text{vib}}$, the nuclear coordinate is $q = q_0\cos(2\pi\nu_{\text{vib}}t)$ and the polarisability is modulated:

$$\alpha = \alpha_0 + \left(\frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial q}\right)_0 q_0\cos(2\pi\nu_{\text{vib}}t)$$

Substituting,

$$p = \alpha_0 E_0\cos(2\pi\nu_0 t) + \left(\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial q}\right)_0 q_0 E_0\,\cos(2\pi\nu_0 t)\cos(2\pi\nu_{\text{vib}}t)$$

and using $\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]$:

$$\boxed{p = \underbrace{\alpha_0E_0\cos 2\pi\nu_0 t}_{\text{Rayleigh}} + \tfrac12\left(\tfrac{\partial\alpha}{\partial q}\right)_0 q_0E_0\left[\underbrace{\cos 2\pi(\nu_0 - \nu_{\text{vib}})t}_{\text{Stokes}} + \underbrace{\cos 2\pi(\nu_0+\nu_{\text{vib}})t}_{\text{anti-Stokes}}\right]}$$

An oscillating dipole radiates at its own frequency, so the scattered light contains three frequencies. **The crucial conclusion:** the Raman terms vanish unless $(\partial\alpha/\partial q)_0 \neq 0$ — **Raman activity requires the polarisability to change during the vibration**, not the dipole moment. That is the fundamental distinction from infrared absorption.

*Classical theory's failure:* it predicts Stokes and anti-Stokes with **equal** intensity, contrary to observation. Only the quantum picture — scattering from a virtual level, with the initial-state populations weighted by Boltzmann — gets the ratio right.

**Rotational Raman selection rule and line positions.** For a linear molecule the rule is $\Delta J = 0, \pm2$ (the $\pm2$ because Raman is a **two-photon** process; the polarisability is a second-rank tensor, so it can transfer two units of angular momentum). Stokes lines ($J \to J+2$):

$$\Delta\tilde\nu = F(J+2) - F(J) = B\left[(J+2)(J+3) - J(J+1)\right] = B(4J + 6)$$

$$\boxed{\Delta\tilde\nu_{\text{Stokes}} = B(4J+6),\ J = 0,1,2,\dots \Rightarrow 6B, 10B, 14B,\dots}$$

So the **first line lies $6B$ from the Rayleigh line** and subsequent lines are spaced $4B$ — note the contrast with the pure rotational (microwave) spectrum, which starts at $2B$ with spacing $2B$. Anti-Stokes lines mirror these on the high-frequency side.

**Why anti-Stokes are weaker.** A Stokes photon leaves the molecule in a *higher* state, so it starts from the populous ground level. An anti-Stokes photon requires the molecule to *begin* in an excited level, whose population is Boltzmann-suppressed. Hence

$$\frac{I_{aS}}{I_{S}} = \left(\frac{\nu_0 + \nu_m}{\nu_0 - \nu_m}\right)^4 \exp\left(-\frac{hc\tilde\nu_m}{kT}\right)$$

the quartic factor being the $\nu^4$ Rayleigh-scattering dependence, and the exponential being the population ratio.

**Numerical: N$_2$, $\tilde\nu_m = 2331\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at 300 K.** Using $kT/hc = 208.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at 300 K,

$$\frac{hc\tilde\nu_m}{kT} = \frac{2331}{208.5} = 11.18 \Rightarrow \frac{I_{aS}}{I_S} \approx e^{-11.18} = \boxed{1.4\times10^{-5}}$$

Ignoring the $\nu^4$ factor (which for visible excitation contributes only a few tens of percent). So the anti-Stokes vibrational band of N$_2$ is ~$10^{5}$ times weaker — effectively invisible at room temperature. **Corollary worth stating:** because the ratio is a pure Boltzmann factor, measuring $I_{aS}/I_S$ is a genuine, non-contact **thermometer** — the basis of Raman thermometry and of distributed fibre temperature sensing.

*Contrast with rotation:* rotational Raman shifts are only a few cm$^{-1}$, so $hc\tilde\nu/kT \ll 1$ and the rotational anti-Stokes lines are almost as strong as the Stokes lines — which is why rotational Raman spectra look symmetric about the exciting line while vibrational ones do not.

**CO$_2$: the rule of mutual exclusion.** CO$_2$ is linear and centrosymmetric (O=C=O), with four normal modes:

| Mode | Description | IR | Raman |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\nu_1$ (1388 cm$^{-1}$) | symmetric stretch | inactive ($\mu$ unchanged) | **active** ($\alpha$ changes) |
| $\nu_2$ (667 cm$^{-1}$, doubly degenerate) | bend | **active** | inactive |
| $\nu_3$ (2349 cm$^{-1}$) | antisymmetric stretch | **active** | inactive |

$$\boxed{\textbf{Rule of mutual exclusion: for a molecule with a centre of symmetry, no mode is both IR and Raman active.}}$$

This is why the two techniques are complementary, and why observing a coincident IR/Raman band **proves** the molecule lacks a centre of symmetry — a standard structure-determination argument UPSC asks for in words. (Compare H$_2$O, which is bent and non-centrosymmetric: all three of its modes are both IR and Raman active.)

## Recurring-pattern insights

**1. UPSC asks Atomic & Molecular as a *bookkeeping* subject, not a derivation subject — and marks it that way.**
Look at the pattern across papers: the questions are "find the ground term of X", "how many Zeeman components", "compute the bond length from the line spacing", "compute $D_e$ from $\omega_e$ and $\omega_e x_e$". Each is a 3-to-5-step recipe with almost no creative content. This is the **highest marks-per-hour section in Paper II** — far higher than QM scattering or Solid State — precisely because the recipes are short and fixed. But the marks are lost in *sequence errors*: forgetting the $L+S$-even restriction for equivalent electrons, forgetting whether the shell is more or less than half full, using $c$ in m s$^{-1}$ with $B$ in cm$^{-1}$. **Drill the sequences, not the derivations.** Write out the five recipes (term symbol → Hund → $g_J$; $\Delta\lambda \to \Delta\tilde\nu \to \zeta$; spacing $\to B \to I \to r$; $\omega_e, \omega_e x_e \to$ fundamental, overtone, $D_e$, $D_0$; $\tilde\nu_m, T \to I_{aS}/I_S$) on one card and rehearse them until each takes under 90 seconds.

**2. The "why is X inactive?" question recurs every few years, in three interchangeable costumes.**
It appears as: *why does N$_2$ show no microwave spectrum?* (no permanent dipole), *why is the symmetric stretch of CO$_2$ IR-inactive?* (rule of mutual exclusion / no dipole change), *why is there no Q branch in the HCl rovibrational band?* ($\Lambda = 0$, photon must carry a unit of angular momentum), and its atomic cousins: *why does the ground state of hydrogen show no linear Stark effect?* (parity), *why is there no fine structure for an s state?* ($l = 0 \Rightarrow$ single $j$), *why does carbon's $^3P_0$ ground state not split in a magnetic field?* ($J = 0$). **Every one of these is answered by naming a symmetry or a vanishing matrix element.** Prepare a single one-page "selection rules and why things vanish" sheet; it covers a guaranteed 10–15 marks per paper and costs almost nothing to maintain.

**3. Fine structure, Zeeman, and Stark are the same calculation three times — and UPSC exploits the fact that students treat them as three topics.**
All three are: *take a degenerate level, add a perturbation, diagonalise it in the degenerate subspace.* Spin–orbit uses $\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}$ and is diagonal in the $|j m_j\rangle$ basis; Zeeman uses $\mu_B B(L_z + 2S_z)$ and is diagonal in $|j m_j\rangle$ at weak field but in $|m_l m_s\rangle$ at strong field (Paschen–Back); Stark uses $e\mathcal{E}z$ and needs an explicit $2\times2$ diagonalisation. **The competition between the perturbation and the existing splitting is the whole story** — weak-field vs strong-field is the same question in every case, and UPSC's "what happens as $B$ is increased greatly?" riders are testing exactly this. If you can state *which term dominates and therefore which basis is good*, you can answer the general version of any of these questions even when the specific system is unfamiliar. This also connects straight back to last week's time-independent perturbation theory set (`2026-08-04`) — same machinery, new labels.

## One tip

**Build a "constants in spectroscopist's units" strip and put it on the inside cover of your formula log.** Almost every lost mark in this section is a units failure, not a physics failure. The strip:

- $\mu_B B/hc = 0.4669\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ per tesla · $\mu_B/h = 14.0$ GHz T$^{-1}$ · $\mu_B = 5.788\times10^{-5}$ eV T$^{-1}$
- $kT/hc = 208.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at 300 K (so $kT/hc \approx 0.695\,T$ in cm$^{-1}$)
- $1\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 1.2398\times10^{-4}$ eV $= 29.98$ GHz
- $hc = 1240$ eV·nm · $R_\infty = 1.097\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} = 109737\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ · $a_0 = 0.529$ Å
- $1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg · $8\pi^2 = 78.96$ · $c = 2.998\times10^{10}$ cm s$^{-1}$ **(cm, not m, whenever $B$ is in cm$^{-1}$)**
- $\Delta\tilde\nu = \Delta\lambda/\lambda^2$ — the shortcut that turns every doublet-separation question into one division

Working the whole section in cm$^{-1}$ rather than joules removes $h$, $c$ and $e$ from the algebra entirely: the sodium doublet becomes $0.6/589^2$, the Raman ratio becomes $e^{-2331/208.5}$, the Zeeman pattern becomes multiples of $0.467$. In a 3-hour paper that is worth several minutes per question and, more importantly, removes the single most common source of a wrong final answer with a correct method.
