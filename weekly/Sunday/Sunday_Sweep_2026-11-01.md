### Mains skeletons

**M1 — Asymmetric federalism (GS2)**
- **Intro:** India is "an indestructible Union of destructible States" with built-in asymmetry: Sixth Schedule, Arts 371–371J, Fifth Schedule, statutory councils, and now the **FNTA**.
- **Strengthens:** accommodates identity without secession (Mizoram 1986, Bodoland 2003/2020); brings governance closer to people; peace-building tool (FNTA after the ENPO demand; MoA of 5 Feb 2026); flexible, because statutory councils can be tuned (Ladakh's LAHDC 2026 amendment).
- **Weakens or strains:** a patchwork of overlapping bodies with unclear fiscal powers; demand cascades (every group seeks its own council); dependence on the Centre/State for funds; Sixth Schedule councils' weak accountability (CAG remarks; the 125th Amendment Bill, 2019 is pending); tension with Part IX/IXA uniformity.
- **Way forward:** fiscal devolution with audit; a sunset/review clause for each arrangement; Inter-State Council deliberation; minimum standards on elections and accounts (SEC-run polls).
- **Conclusion:** asymmetry is a *means* of cooperative federalism when it is negotiated, audited and reviewed. It is not a substitute for capacity.

**M2 — Fiscal consolidation vs capex (GS3)**
- **Intro:** H1 FY27 fiscal deficit at **42.1% of BE (₹7.14 lakh cr vs ₹16.96 lakh cr target, 4.3% of GDP)**, ahead of last year's pace.
- **Why consolidate:** interest burden and crowding out; bond yields and the cost of capital; ratings; FRBM/N.K. Singh debt anchor; space for shocks (El Niño, a deficient monsoon).
- **Why keep capex:** multiplier ~2.5 for capex vs <1 for revenue spending (cite as RBI/NIPFP estimates and *flag* them); crowd-in of private investment; logistics (container scheme, ports); employment.
- **Reconciling:** cut revenue-expenditure leakages (subsidy targeting, DBT); asset monetisation; disinvestment; tax buoyancy from GST/I-T Act 2025; quality of expenditure (outcome budgets); State capex through the 50-year interest-free loans.
- **Conclusion:** consolidate through *composition*, protecting capex and compressing unproductive revenue spending, rather than through headline cuts.

### Ethics case — model approach

- **Issues:** the customer's **autonomy vs protection from harm** (soft paternalism); the bank's **fiduciary duty and duty of care**; pressure from targets and the regional office's warning vs integrity; **due diligence** and the SC's signal on liability; dignity and privacy of an elderly customer; the junior officer's **legalism** ("paperwork is in order").
- **Stakeholders:** the customer and her family; the bank and its depositors' trust; you and your staff; law enforcement; the wider public (fraud networks feed on silence).
- **Options:** (1) process it as instructed: legal on paper, but a breach of duty of care, so reject; (2) refuse outright: paternalistic and may humiliate her, so reject; (3) **pause with respect and verify**: speak to her privately, explain that **no agency conducts "digital arrests"** or demands transfers on video calls, invoke the bank's **temporary debit-hold / cooling-off** where permissible, with her informed consent call the **1930 cyber helpline / National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal**, and offer to contact a family member she names; **(4) record and escalate**: document the interaction, inform the regional office's fraud cell, flag the beneficiary account for mule-account checks.
- **Choose (3) + (4).** This respects autonomy (she decides after she is informed), fulfils the duty of care, and creates an audit trail that protects both her and the bank.
- **Systemic:** a red-flag SOP for senior citizens' large unusual transfers; staff training scripts; mule-account analytics; awareness at the counter; no target pressure on FD premature closures; a feedback loop with the State Cyber Crime Coordination Centre.
- **Values to name:** integrity, compassion, courage of conviction, accountability, **"liability follows due diligence"**.

### Essay — model approach: *"Rivers do not recognise borders; neither should our responsibility towards them."*

- **Opening:** an image, e.g. a fisher on the Siang who cannot see Medog but lives with its consequences; or the Ganga carrying the effluent of five States to the Bay.
- **Thesis:** a river is a *shared system*. Responsibility for it must be scaled to the basin, not to the map, and that applies at three levels: international, inter-State and the individual/community.
- **Body (pick 4–5 strands):**
  1. *Nature of rivers:* hydrology is basin-wide (Himalayan rivers, glacier-fed flows; hanging glaciers above Badrinath).
  2. *International:* Indus Waters Treaty (1960) and its strains; Brahmaputra data-sharing; Medog vs Siang; the Ganga Water Treaty with Bangladesh (1996).
  3. *Inter-State:* Art 262; Cauvery (CWMA 2018), Krishna, Mahadayi; the "upstream advantage" vs downstream rights; interlinking (Ken–Betwa).
  4. *Local:* sand mining, effluent (last week's case of a CETP below capacity), encroachment of floodplains; the community as first custodian.
  5. *Ethical lens:* intergenerational equity, the public trust doctrine (*M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath*, 1997), **rights of rivers** (Uttarakhand HC 2017, later stayed by the SC; New Zealand's Whanganui Act 2017).
- **Counter-view:** sovereignty and development needs are legitimate (hydropower for energy security; strategic counter-dams). Answer it: responsibility does not mean veto. It means **notification, data-sharing, impact assessment and benefit-sharing**.
- **Way forward:** basin authorities with teeth; real-time data-sharing; environmental flows; Namami Gange-style convergence; community river guardians.
- **Close:** return to the opening image. The river will reach the sea either way. What reaches it with the river is the measure of our responsibility.
