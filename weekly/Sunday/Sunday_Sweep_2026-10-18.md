### GS Mains — answer skeletons

**M1 — Children, the internet and fundamental rights (GS2)**
- **Intro:** the SC (Pardiwala & Chandran JJ., Oct 2026) rejected blanket age-based bans and framed child safety and freedom as complementary. Access to information is part of **Art 19(1)(a)**, and the internet is linked to education and dignity under **Art 21/21A** (*Anuradha Bhasin* 2020; *Faheema Shirin*, Kerala HC 2019).
- **Body, the case for protection:** CSEAM, grooming, addiction and data harms; **POCSO 2012**, **IT Act s.67B**, **IT Rules 2021**, **DPDP Act 2023** (verifiable parental consent for under-18s).
- **Body, why blanket bans fail:** proportionality (*Puttaswamy*, *Modern Dental*), the digital divide, chilling effects, and the evidence that bans push children to unsafe spaces.
- **Way forward:** the court's own design: **hash and URL databases** with safeguards, grievance redress for wrong matches and a bar on repurposing them; safety-by-design duties on platforms; digital-literacy curricula; age-appropriate design codes (cf. UK).
- **Conclusion:** protection should be **targeted at harms, not at access**. That fits the court's line.

**M2 — IDBI Bank strategic sale (GS3)**
- **Intro:** Cabinet cleared the sale of **60.72% (Govt 30.48% + LIC 30.24%)** to a Fairfax subsidiary for **₹52,878 cr** at ₹81/share. It is the largest strategic disinvestment since Air India, subject to the RBI's fit-and-proper approval.
- **Rationale:** frees capital (no more recapitalisation), professional management, LIC's exit from an unrelated bank, a large foreign investment in the financial sector, and a signal for the **2021 PSE policy** (strategic vs non-strategic sectors).
- **Concerns:** valuation and timing; foreign control of a bank (RBI ownership norms, voting caps); job security and priority-sector lending; financial inclusion in rural branches; the government's residual 15% and its exit route.
- **Way forward:** strong RBI oversight, ring-fenced commitments on staff and lending, transparent sale of the residual stake, a lesson for the next PSB privatisation.
- **Conclusion:** the test is better credit allocation and governance, not just the cheque.

### GS4 case — model approach

- **Stakeholders:** boat operators and their families; tourists; residents who depend on the lake (water, fisheries); the lake ecosystem; the High Court; your staff; the legislator; the State's credibility.
- **Ethical issues:** **rule of law vs livelihoods**; **public interest vs local pressure**; **conflict of interest** (staff with relatives running boats); **safety** (the jet-ski injury); **integrity** (a quiet "far side" deal = disobeying a court order); compassion for indebted operators.
- **Options:**
  1. *Delay until after the season* → contempt; unsafe; sends the message that orders are negotiable. Reject.
  2. *Allow "self-regulation" on part of the lake* → covert non-compliance; unenforceable; corrupt in effect. Reject.
  3. *Strict enforcement with no relief* → lawful, but harsh. It breeds resentment and later violations.
  4. **Enforce fully, with a time-bound transition package** → recommended.
- **Course of action:** (i) Enforce the ban from day one, with patrols, prioritising jet skis and speedboats; (ii) **recuse the two conflicted staff** from enforcement and record it; (iii) document the injury and the enforcement steps for the Court; (iv) move the Court (through the State) for **specific transition directions**: registered operators, an electric/solar conversion scheme, interest subvention or loan restructuring through banks, a pool of **non-motorised and e-boats for the season**, and priority licences for affected operators; (v) open a grievance cell and talk to the association; (vi) reply to the legislator in writing: the order binds everyone, and here is the relief plan for his constituents.
- **Values:** integrity, impartiality, empathy, accountability, courage of conviction. Close with **graded, humane enforcement inside the law, never outside it.**

### Essay — model approach: "Freedom and safety are not rivals."

- **Hook:** a seatbelt makes a car faster to drive, not slower: drivers go further because they are safe. Or the SC's line this October on children and the internet.
- **Thesis:** the rivalry between freedom and safety is mostly a failure of design. Well-designed safety *enables* freedom, and real freedom (to speak, question and report) makes societies safer. Trade-offs exist, but they are narrower than they look.
- **Dimensions (pick 5–6):**
  1. *Philosophical:* Hobbes (security first) vs Mill's harm principle vs Berlin's negative/positive liberty; Amartya Sen's freedom as capability needs basic security.
  2. *Constitutional:* Art 19(2)–(6) reasonable restrictions; proportionality (*Puttaswamy*, *Anuradha Bhasin*); the Emergency (1975–77) as the cautionary tale of "safety" overriding liberty; preventive-detention and UAPA bail debates.
  3. *Digital:* child safety online; the DPDP Act; hash databases with safeguards vs blanket bans; encryption debates.
  4. *Public health:* the COVID-19 lockdowns: safety measures that worked best kept trust and information flowing.
  5. *Economic:* deposit insurance and financial regulation let people take risks (start firms, invest); rules make markets free.
  6. *Gender:* safe streets expand women's freedom of movement and work; women's labour-force participation and safe transport.
  7. *Environment/everyday:* road safety, building codes, Dal-Lake-type enforcement that protects the commons others depend on.
- **Counter-view:** in real emergencies (terrorism, pandemics) trade-offs sharpen, and "safety" is often a pretext for control. Answer: time-limited, necessary, proportionate, reviewable.
- **Way forward:** proportionality as the everyday test; independent oversight; sunset clauses; safety-by-design over bans; public trust as the shared foundation.
- **Conclusion:** freedom without safety is fragile, and safety without freedom is a cage. A mature democracy designs for both.
