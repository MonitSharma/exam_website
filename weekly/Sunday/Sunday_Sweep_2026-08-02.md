## ETHICS CASE + ESSAY

### GS4 case study (250 words, 20 min) — write this on **Saturday 8 Aug**

You are the Sub-Divisional Magistrate of a hill-foot subdivision that has just been hit by devastating flash floods. Sixty-two people are dead and about a lakh are displaced.

Within hours, the State government's press release attributed the disaster to a **"cloudburst"** — an unforeseeable natural event. Two days later the technical assessment from the meteorological department reaches your desk: there was **no cloudburst**. Rainfall over the upstream catchment was extreme but forecastable, and the district's own drainage channels — which your predecessor's file notes flag as encroached and desilting-overdue for three years — failed.

Your District Magistrate calls you. Her position is pragmatic and not obviously cynical: the "cloudburst" framing is what unlocks the **State Disaster Response Fund** category under which relief is being released quickly; a public correction now will stall disbursement, trigger a blame contest between the State and the Centre, and invite an inquiry that will paralyse the relief machinery for weeks. She asks you to sign the situation report retaining the cloudburst description, and to "raise the drainage issue separately, later."

Relief is genuinely flowing. People genuinely need it. And the report you are asked to sign is genuinely false.

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved.

## Mains skeletons

### M1 (GS2) — basic structure vs democratic will

**Intro (2 lines).** Kesavananda (1973) placed an unwritten limit on a written Constitution; the question is whether that limit protects democracy or displaces it.

**Body — the case for the doctrine.**

- **Textual anchor:** Art 368 uses "amend", not "repeal" or "re-enact" — amendment presupposes a continuing identity.
- **Historical necessity:** the doctrine's real test was the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, passed by a Parliament sitting in an Emergency it had itself extended. Minerva Mills (1980) is the answer to the objection that no majority could ever go so far — one did.
- **Counter-majoritarian logic:** Fundamental Rights protect *minorities against majorities*; a right amendable by the majority is not a right. S.R. Bommai (1994) extended the logic to secularism and federalism.
- **Restraint in practice:** the doctrine has been invoked sparingly — the Court has upheld far more amendments than it has struck down.

**Body — the case against.**

- **Indeterminacy:** "basic structure" has no closed list. Each bench adds to it (secularism, federalism, judicial review, free and fair elections, rule of law, separation of powers) — a limit whose content the limiting body defines.
- **Democratic deficit:** an unelected court overrides a special majority of both Houses *plus*, in some cases, half the State legislatures — the most demanding consent threshold the Constitution knows.
- **Institutional self-interest:** **NJAC (2015)** is the sharpest example — the Court used the doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment governing *its own* appointments. Nemo judex in causa sua is a real objection here, whatever one thinks of the outcome.
- **Comparative:** most democracies manage without it; those with eternity clauses (Germany's Art 79(3), the Basic Law) have them *written in*, not judicially discovered.

**Synthesis.** The doctrine is best defended not as a general limit on democracy but as a **circuit-breaker against constitutional self-destruction** — the scenario where a transient majority converts a temporary mandate into a permanent one. Its legitimacy is therefore inversely proportional to how often it is used. The reform direction is not abolition but discipline: a reasoned, cumulative and self-limiting jurisprudence, and a Court that is visibly most reluctant where its own interests are engaged.

**Conclusion.** The doctrine does insulate the Constitution from the electorate — deliberately, and only at the margin. Whether that is a defect depends on whether one thinks constitutions exist to record majorities or to outlast them.

### M2 (GS3) — from deficit rule to debt anchor

**Intro.** FRBM (2003) targeted a flow — the fiscal deficit. The N.K. Singh Committee (2017) shifted the frame to a stock — the debt-to-GDP ratio. The change is conceptual, not cosmetic.

**Rationale for the shift.**

- **Solvency is a stock question.** Sustainability depends on the **r − g** differential (interest rate vs nominal growth) and the primary balance, not on any single year's deficit. A 3% deficit is benign at 11% nominal growth and dangerous at 5%.
- **Deficit targets are procyclical.** Hitting a fixed number in a downturn forces contraction exactly when the economy needs support — the flaw the euro-area Stability and Growth Pact exposed.
- **Gaming.** Flow targets invite off-budget borrowing (FCI, NSSF, oil bonds, PSU borrowing) that hits the deficit line but not the debt reality. A debt anchor is harder to dress up.
- **Composition neutrality.** A debt path allows a government to raise *capital* expenditure while consolidating, which a rigid deficit ceiling penalises equally with revenue spending.
- **The numbers:** a 60% general-government target (40% Centre, 20% States), with the fiscal deficit reduced to an operational instrument and an explicit escape clause.

**Risks.**

- **Denominator dependence.** The ratio improves with nominal growth or inflation as easily as with restraint. Consolidation can be *achieved* without any actual fiscal effort — and can reverse with one growth shock.
- **Weaker annual accountability.** A 2030 debt path is not enforceable in a 2027 Budget the way a 3% number is. The discipline is deferred and therefore diluted.
- **Escape-clause creep.** COVID demonstrated how fast an exception becomes a floor; re-entry into the glide path is politically far harder than exit.
- **State-level asymmetry.** A general-government target is meaningless without a binding mechanism for States, whose guarantees and off-budget liabilities are least visible.
- **Interest burden.** With interest payments the single largest revenue-expenditure item, a debt ratio that stabilises at a *high* level still crowds out spending — stabilisation is not sufficiency.

**Way forward.** Keep the debt anchor, but pair it with (i) an **operational annual balance rule** — most usefully on the *primary* balance, which is what governments actually control; (ii) a statutorily mandated, transparent accounting of off-budget and contingent liabilities; (iii) an **independent fiscal council** to score budgets ex ante; (iv) a symmetric, pre-specified escape clause with an automatic re-entry path.

**Conclusion.** A debt anchor is the analytically correct target and the weaker disciplinary instrument. India needs both: the anchor for the decade, an operational rule for the year.

## GS4 case study — model approach

**(a) Ethical issues.**

1. **Truthfulness vs beneficence** — the classical conflict. The falsehood is instrumental to a genuine good (fast relief), which is what makes it hard rather than obvious.
2. **Integrity of the public record.** A situation report is not a press statement; it enters the official record and becomes the factual basis for inquiry, audit, compensation and future planning. Falsifying it corrupts every downstream decision.
3. **Accountability displacement.** Reclassifying an administrative failure (encroached, un-desilted drains flagged for three years) as an act of God erases the *very* causal link that would prevent recurrence. This is the deepest harm and it is invisible today.
4. **Justice to the victims.** Sixty-two people died partly because of a preventable failure. Their families have a claim to an accurate account, and possibly to a different category of liability than "natural disaster."
5. **Institutional loyalty vs constitutional duty.** The DM is your superior; the oath is to the Constitution. Conduct Rules require obedience to *lawful* directions.
6. **Moral hazard.** If this framing works once, it becomes the default. The same drains will be un-desilted next monsoon.

**(b) Options.**

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| Sign as directed; raise drainage later | Relief flows uninterrupted; no confrontation; you retain the position from which you can act | Complicity in a false official record; "later" is structurally unlikely to arrive; you become a witness who cannot testify |
| Refuse outright and go public | Morally clean; maximum transparency | Relief stalls; you are removed and replaced by someone more pliable; net harm to victims may exceed the benefit; disloyalty without due process |
| Sign only what is true; record the technical finding in writing; decouple relief from causation | Preserves record integrity *and* relief; uses legitimate channels; creates a durable paper trail | Slower; requires courage in a live crisis; risks displeasing the DM |
| Anonymous leak to the press | Fast exposure | Cowardly, unverifiable, converts a governance failure into a political scandal; destroys internal trust permanently |

**(c) Course of action — reasoned.**

I would refuse to sign a report describing the event as a cloudburst, while doing everything possible to ensure relief is not delayed.

Concretely: **(i)** sign a situation report that is factually accurate — extreme rainfall over the upstream catchment, drainage failure, casualty and displacement figures — because *nothing in the accurate version reduces the severity of the event*, and severity is what relief eligibility turns on; **(ii)** put on file, in writing to the DM, the meteorological assessment and my reasons, so that the disagreement is documented and does not depend on anyone's memory; **(iii)** immediately escalate the *relief-eligibility* question separately and urgently — if the SDRF category genuinely depends on the label, that is a rule defect to be resolved by the State Executive Committee or through NDRF norms, not by falsifying a report; **(iv)** simultaneously initiate the drainage restoration and encroachment removal file, with a timeline, so the corrective action starts while attention is high rather than after it fades.

**The reasoning.** The DM's argument rests on a premise worth testing rather than accepting: that accurate reporting would stall relief. Disaster relief is triggered by *severity and category of event*, not by attributing blame — a flash flood from extreme rainfall is a notified disaster in its own right. The dilemma is probably softer than it is being presented, and my first duty is to find that out rather than to resolve a hard case I have not verified is hard.

Where it is genuinely hard — where a rule really does reward a false description — the answer is still not to lie, but to escalate the defective rule. An officer who falsifies a record to make a broken system deliver has kept the system broken *and* lost the standing to fix it.

**Values demonstrated:** integrity, moral courage, objectivity, accountability, and — importantly — **practical wisdom**: I do not resign, leak, or grandstand. I stay in the post, keep relief moving, and use the file, which is the instrument the system actually respects.

## Essay — "Institutions outlive intentions"

**Direction.** A double-edged proposition, and the essay must cut both ways. Institutions preserve good intentions past the lifespan of the people who held them — *and* they preserve bad ones past the moment anyone still believes in them. The thesis to defend: **the durability of institutions is morally neutral; what makes it valuable is the capacity for revision built in at the founding.**

**Structure.**

1. **Opening (narrative).** A founding moment where an intention was deliberately converted into a structure. The Constituent Assembly is the obvious one; the Election Commission or the design of the All-India Services are less obvious and therefore better.
2. **Section 1 — Institutions as intentions made durable.** The case for the proposition's optimistic reading. Ambedkar's insistence on constitutional morality as something that must be *cultivated* because it is not natural. The RTI Act outliving the movement that produced it. The Green Revolution's research institutions outliving the food crisis that justified them. Universities, courts, central banks: all devices for making a conviction survive the conviction-holder.
3. **Section 2 — Institutions as intentions fossilised.** The dark reading. The colonial police architecture surviving colonialism (Police Act 1861, still substantially in force). Licence-raj bodies outliving the economic theory that created them. Caste as the most durable institution in Indian life, outliving every intention ever directed at it. The general point: **an institution cannot distinguish between the intention it was built for and the interests it accumulates afterwards.**
4. **Section 3 — Why the gap opens.** Institutions optimise for their own continuity; intentions are held by people who die, change their minds, or lose elections. Path dependence, the constituency an institution grows around itself, the difficulty of proving a preventive institution is still needed.
5. **Section 4 — Designing for revision.** The resolution. Sunset clauses. Periodic review. The amendment procedure — Art 368's graded majorities are exactly a mechanism for letting a Constitution change without letting it be replaced. Basic structure as a limit *on* revision that preserves the *capacity* for revision. The Finance Commission's five-year reconstitution as a small, elegant example of an institution designed to be rebuilt.
6. **Section 5 — The individual in the institution.** Institutions do not act; people acting under rules do. The officer who follows the rule when no one is watching is what "institution" actually means in practice.
7. **Conclusion.** Institutions outliving intentions is not a warning or a consolation — it is simply the condition of collective life. The question a society answers well or badly is whether it built the door back in.

**Fodder to have ready:** Ambedkar on constitutional morality ("a top-dressing on an Indian soil which is essentially undemocratic"); North on institutions as "the rules of the game"; Acemoglu–Robinson on extractive vs inclusive institutions; Burke on society as a partnership between the living, the dead and the unborn; Gandhi's Constructive Programme; the Police Act 1861; the Kesavananda–Minerva line; Nehru's "tryst with destiny" as intention, the Constitution as institution.

**Traps to avoid:** don't write a definitional essay on "what is an institution"; don't turn it into a polity answer with headings; keep at least a third of the material non-Indian and non-political — families, religions, languages, scientific disciplines and firms are all institutions, and the examiner has read the Constitution essay four hundred times.
