### M1 — GS2 skeleton (Governor's discretion and federalism)

**Intro (2–3 lines).** The Governor is a constitutional head who is not elected, is appointed by the Union, and holds office at its pleasure — an office whose *powers* are small but whose *discretion* is constitutionally insulated. State the paradox, don't narrate the office.

**Body — Part 1: where the design creates discretion.**

- **Art 163(1)**: aid and advice binds him **except** where the Constitution requires him to act in his discretion. **Art 163(2)**: **his own decision on what is discretionary is final and cannot be questioned** — a self-certifying discretion with no parallel for the President.
- **Art 200**: four options on a Bill — assent, withhold, return (non-Money Bills), reserve for the President — with **no time limit stated**. **Art 201** likewise binds the President to no timeline.
- **Art 356**: the Governor's report is the trigger for President's Rule.
- **Art 213**: ordinance-making, with three cases requiring the President's prior instruction.
- Situational discretion: **appointing a CM in a hung Assembly, dismissing a Ministry that has lost confidence, recommending dissolution**.

**Body — Part 2: the consequences.**

- **Bills stalled indefinitely**, converting a revising power into a veto the Constitution never granted; State legislative agendas become contingent on Union goodwill.
- **Art 356 misuse** before *Bommai*, and the residual risk after it.
- **Government formation** in hung Assemblies decided in Raj Bhavan rather than on the floor.
- The office's **legitimacy deficit**: security of tenure is nominal (**Art 156** pleasure; ***B.P. Singhal*, 2010** — removal need not be for cause though it cannot be arbitrary), so the incumbent's incentives point towards the Union.
- Counter-point, and include it: the discretion is not gratuitous. It exists for **constitutional breakdown, tribal-area responsibilities under Part XXI, and inter-State/Union interests** — a Governor with **no** discretion is a rubber stamp in exactly the situations where one is needed.

**Body — Part 3: reforms proposed.**

- **Sarkaria Commission**: eminent person, outsider to the State, not recently active in politics, consultation with the Chief Minister; **five-year term not disturbed except for compelling reasons**.
- **Punchhi Commission**: **removal only by a resolution of the State legislature**; a **committee including the PM, Home Minister, Speaker and the State Chief Minister** to select; **fixed timelines for assent**; deletion of "pleasure" as an unqualified doctrine; the "doctrine of pleasure" to be circumscribed.
- **Judicial route**: *Bommai* (floor test), *Nabam Rebia* (limits on discretion in summoning/dissolution), *Shamsher Singh* (the Governor acts on advice save in exceptional cases), and the recent line of assent litigation — **describe it as evolving and date your claim**.
- **Convention route**: publishing reasons for withholding assent; a self-imposed timeline.

**Conclusion.** The problem is not the existence of discretion but its **untimed and self-certifying** character. Reform should aim at **procedure — timelines, reasons, and a transparent appointment and removal process — rather than at abolition**, since a purely ceremonial Governor removes a safeguard without removing the politics.

### M2 — GS3 skeleton (land reform's asymmetric outcome)

**Intro.** One line stating the asymmetry as a fact: intermediary abolition succeeded, tenancy reform and ceilings did not, and the difference is not about intent.

**Body — Part 1: why abolition succeeded.**

- A **single, visible, legally identifiable** class of intermediaries; the beneficiary (the State) was also the enforcer; the political coalition of the 1950s was aligned against zamindars; and the laws were constitutionally protected — **First Amendment, 1951**, and the **Ninth Schedule**.
- But note the qualification: much of the gain accrued to **upper-tenure holders**, not to the actual tiller; *sir* and *khudkasht* resumption displaced many.

**Body — Part 2: why tenancy reform and ceilings failed.**

- **Tenancy**: tenancies were **oral and unrecorded**; "voluntary surrender" provisions were exploited; pre-emptive eviction outran the law; land became something to be kept idle rather than leased.
- **Ceilings**: **benami transfers, intra-family partition, exempted categories** (plantations, orchards, cooperatives, religious and charitable trusts), and **litigation**; the shift to a family basis only came in 1972; the surplus actually declared, taken possession of and distributed is a small fraction of operated area.
- **Structural causes common to both**: land is a **State subject**; the implementing bureaucracy was locally embedded; **land records were and are poor**; and the political coalition that had opposed zamindars **included** the middle peasantry that ceilings threatened.
- **The exceptions prove the mechanism**: **West Bengal (Operation Barga)** and **Kerala** succeeded because implementation was backed by mobilised beneficiaries and, crucially, by **recording** the tenant — a record is enforceable, a right is not.

**Body — Part 3: the successor agenda, argued both ways.**

- **The case for records and leasing**: **DILRMP** and **SVAMITVA** attack the actual binding constraint — nobody can enforce a right in land they cannot prove. The **NITI Aayog Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act, 2016** argues that **prohibition drives tenancy underground**, denying tenants credit, insurance and disaster relief while also locking land away from those who would cultivate it; legalising leasing with a recorded, time-bound contract protects both parties, and supports consolidation and scale.
- **The case against**: legal leasing without a floor of security may **accelerate reverse tenancy** and concentration; digitised records **freeze existing inequities** and can dispossess those whose claims are customary or undocumented — women's land rights and tribal land under the **Fifth Schedule, PESA and the Forest Rights Act** are the sharpest cases.
- **Synthesis**: records and leasing are **necessary but not sufficient**. They should be paired with recognition of women's rights in land, protection of Scheduled Area transfers, and a functioning grievance route.

**Conclusion.** The first-generation agenda failed less on ambition than on **information and enforcement**; the second-generation agenda fixes information but must not be mistaken for redistribution. Judge it by the **1913-style question UPSC actually asks — did productivity rise and did poverty fall** — and not by the number of records digitised.

### GS4 case study — model approach

**(a) Ethical issues.**

- **Justice and the claims of the most vulnerable**: 112 landless SC/ST households, many cultivating the land already, against a diffuse and future public benefit (employment).
- **Rule of law and the finality of adjudication**: a lawful order has been passed; administrative discretion over *sequencing* is being used to achieve what could not be achieved lawfully.
- **Political neutrality and the integrity of public office**: the request is lawful on its face and is precisely for that reason harder to refuse; the ethical objection is to the **purpose**, which is electoral.
- **Honesty and institutional credibility**: the Additional Collector's informal assurances were improper, but the families' expectation is now real, and repeated deferral has already destroyed trust three times.
- **Conflicting goods**: distributive justice vs employment-led development — a genuine conflict, not a good-vs-evil case, and your answer must say so.
- **Transparency and accountability**: the pending RTI means the file's movement will be public; act as though every note you record will be read, because it will be.
- **Intergenerational and historical justice**: two generations of unrecorded sharecropping is itself the failure this order is correcting.

**(b) Options.**

- **Option 1 — Distribute all 240 acres and cancel the industrial notification.** *Merits*: full compliance with the order, equal treatment of all 112 families, no discretion abused. *Demerits*: forfeits a major employment project; may itself be legally vulnerable if the notification was validly made; treats a real public interest as if it did not exist.
- **Option 2 — Accept the "sequencing" request.** *Merits*: lawful on its face; avoids conflict; buys time. *Demerits*: uses lawful discretion for an unlawful purpose; on all available evidence the deferral becomes permanent; 34 families bear the entire cost; and it is indefensible on the RTI record.
- **Option 3 — Distribute the uncontested 180 acres immediately and resolve the 60 acres through a transparent, time-bound process** — a written order fixing a deadline, a joint reference to the Industries and Revenue Departments, and a public commitment that the 34 families will receive **either land of equivalent value elsewhere in the district or compensation and a first claim on rehabilitation and employment from the project**. *Merits*: nothing is deferred without a date and a named alternative; the genuine conflict is resolved on the merits rather than by silence; it is fully defensible on the record. *Demerits*: slower for 34 families; requires alternative land or funds to actually exist; needs sustained follow-through that outlasts your tenure.

**(c) Course of action.** Take **Option 3**, and make three things structural rather than personal:

1. **Distribute the 180 acres this week**, with possession and mutation recorded, not merely papers issued.
2. **Record a written, reasoned order** on the 60 acres with a **fixed outer date** and the alternative package specified, and place it in the public domain **before** the RTI reply is due. A timeline that exists on paper survives your transfer; an assurance does not.
3. **Respond to the political functionary in writing**, courteously, recording that the matter is being decided on a fixed timeline. Do not refuse orally and leave no trace; the record is the protection, for you and for the families.
4. **Tell the Additional Collector plainly** that informal assurances were wrong, and then **make the assurance true through the order** — which is the only way to keep him from being a liar without pretending the impropriety did not happen.

**Closing line.** Administrative discretion is legitimate when it is used to *decide better*, and illegitimate when it is used to *avoid deciding*. Every deferral here has been the second kind. The value at stake is not courage; it is **the credibility of the State's word to people who have been told three times already**.

### Essay — model approach

> **"An institution is judged not by the powers it is given, but by the restraint with which it uses them."**

**Framing.** Do not write a civics essay. The claim is contestable, and the best essays this week will *test* it rather than illustrate it. The central move: **restraint is a virtue only where power is real and reviewable** — an institution that is weak is not restrained, it is merely ineffective; and an institution that shows restraint only because it is watched is not restrained, it is deterred. So the essay is really about **what makes restraint genuine**.

**A workable structure.**

1. **Open with a concrete scene, not a definition.** A Governor's file that is neither signed nor returned; an office exercising a power the Constitution never gave it by declining to exercise one it did. Restraint's opposite is not always action — sometimes it is **inaction used as an instrument**.
2. **The case for the proposition.** Powers are enumerated; conduct is not. The Constitution gave the Supreme Court Art 142 and the President Art 72; it could not give either the judgement to use them well. The doctrine of **basic structure** is itself an act of restraint in the guise of an assertion. The CAG audits after the fact and is stronger for not controlling issue. Convention held the Indian Parliament together in decades when the text alone would not have.
3. **The case against — and this is where a good essay separates.** Restraint can be a euphemism for **abdication**. A regulator that does not act, a commission that does not summon, a legislature that does not scrutinise — each can call itself restrained. **Restraint without capacity is not a virtue**, and institutions have used the vocabulary of self-limitation to avoid accountability. Distinguish **principled forbearance** from **institutional timidity**.
4. **The synthesis.** Restraint is judged by three tests: **is the power real** (could it have been used?), **is the reason given** (was the forbearance reasoned and recorded?), and **is it consistent** (does the institution restrain itself against its own interest, not only against a weak opponent?). An institution that passes all three is genuinely restrained. One that passes none is merely inert.
5. **Widen the aperture in the last third.** The argument is not only constitutional. It applies to **states in the international system** (the "net security provider" who is judged by what he does *not* do in his neighbourhood), to **markets and platform monopolies**, to **science and technology** (the genome-editing moratoria; the CDR debate, where the temptation is to use a technology's promise as a licence to delay), and to **personal ethics** — self-restraint is the private form of the same virtue, and the essay should land there.
6. **Close by returning to the scene you opened with**, now answered.

**Material you can actually deploy this week** — *Bommai* and the floor test; Art 163(2); the Ninth Schedule and *I.R. Coelho*; the CAG's "auditor not comptroller" design; **Ashoka after Kalinga**, which is the oldest and best Indian instance of power renounced at the height of its exercise, and worth two sentences rather than a paragraph; UNEP's insistence that carbon removal not become a licence to emit; and the Punchhi Commission's proposals. **Use three of these, not ten.** An essay that deploys three examples in depth beats one that lists nine.
