### Mains skeletons

**M1 (validating Acts):** *Intro:* separation of powers is not written as a doctrine in the Constitution, but it is part of the basic structure (*Indira Gandhi* 1975, *Kesavananda*). *Body:* (1) **What legislatures may do:** change the law prospectively or retrospectively within competence; cure the defect a court identified (*Shri Prithvi Cotton Mills* 1969, *Indian Aluminium* 1996). (2) **What they may not do:** directly annul a judgment or command the court (*Madan Mohan Pathak* 1978, *Cauvery Disputes Tribunal* 1991 reference, *Medical Council v. State of Kerala* 2018, *Srinivasa T.* 2026). (3) **Tests:** is the defect actually cured? Is the law general, or aimed at a specific judgment's beneficiaries? (4) **Why it matters:** judicial finality (Art 141, 144), rule of law, public trust in recruitment and governance. (5) **Grey areas:** retrospective tax laws; tribunal reform Acts re-enacting struck-down provisions (*Madras Bar Association* 2021). *Conclusion:* legislatures can respond to courts by changing the law; they cannot change the verdict. That restraint protects both institutions.

**M2 (FIT and food shocks):** *Intro:* CPI Oct 2026 6.09% (CFPI 8.94%), first breach in ~2 years; 4% ± 2% target retained for 2026–31. *Body:* (1) **FIT record since 2016:** anchored expectations; lower average inflation; credibility. (2) **Limits:** food has a large weight in CPI; supply shocks (onion +116%) are not curable by the repo rate; rate hikes hurt growth without fixing supply. (3) **Design answers:** the MPC "looks through" transient shocks but watches second-round effects (core, wages, expectations); the **three-quarter failure clause** builds in tolerance. (4) **Debates:** headline vs core targeting; reweighting food in the new CPI series (base 2024); coordination with fiscal and supply-side tools (buffer stocks, export curbs, storage, Operation Greens). *Conclusion:* keep headline targeting for credibility, but pair it with supply-side policy; FIT is the anchor, not the whole ship.

### Ethics case — model approach

**Stakeholders:** PVTG and other claimant households; the Gram Sabha; Forest Department and the tiger corridor; the State government; the Supreme Court's directions; NGOs; honest and compromised SDLC members; you.
**Ethical issues:** rule of law vs substantive justice; documentary formalism vs the FRA's own allowance for oral evidence and elders' statements (Rule 13 of the FRA Rules lists such evidence); conservation vs livelihood; corruption in the SDLC; deadline pressure vs due process; protection of the most vulnerable (Art 46).
**Options:** (1) Reject undocumented claims fast: meets the deadline, but violates the Act's evidentiary scheme and the PVTG households' rights. (2) Approve broadly to avoid hardship: defies the SC ruling and invites contempt and ecological damage. (3) **Recommended:** a structured, time-bound re-verification that follows the Court and the Act together.
**Action plan:** (a) Triage the 4,200 claims into clear-accept, clear-reject (pure commercial cultivation, absentee claimants) and needs-evidence. (b) For the third group, hold **Gram Sabha-led field verification camps** with elders' testimony, satellite imagery from the cut-off year, and interpreters for the Kurumba-type language groups; record reasons in writing. (c) Handle the landslide-displaced cases explicitly: displacement within the forest is not loss of residence. (d) Report the bribery allegation to the vigilance department, recuse those members, and re-examine every claim they cleared. (e) Coordinate with the Forest Department on the corridor through a joint mapping exercise, and refer genuinely conflicting cases to the SC-appointed Special Committee. (f) Publish weekly progress and give rejected claimants written reasons and appeal information.
**Values:** integrity, impartiality, compassion for the weaker sections, accountability, procedural justice. *Closing line:* speed and fairness are not opposites if the process is designed before the deadline, not after it.

### Essay — model approach

**Thesis:** a museum is never a neutral store; it chooses what to show, how to label it and whose story it tells. A confident nation uses museums to *argue* with its past, not merely to admire it.
**Structure:**
1. **Hook:** the Dancing Girl and the Pashupati seal moved from the National Museum for the **Yuge Yugeen Bharat Museum** in North and South Block: colonial seats of power turned into a house of civilisational memory.
2. **What museums do:** preserve (conservation), interpret (labels, narratives), include or exclude (whose objects, whose language).
3. **Arguments a nation has with itself:** continuity vs change; plurality (Buddhist, Jain, tribal, Islamic, colonial pasts); pride vs reckoning (the Partition Museum, Amritsar; Jallianwala Bagh); restitution debates (the Piprahwa relics returned for exposition, the Kohinoor question).
4. **Documentary memory:** Rajatarangini, Arthashastra and Mahabharata manuscripts on UNESCO's MOWCAP register: memory beyond objects.
5. **Risks:** curation as propaganda; spectacle over scholarship; neglect of small, regional and tribal museums; digital divide.
6. **The way forward:** curatorial independence, scholarly review, multilingual labels, community museums (tribal freedom-fighter museums), digitisation (IGNCA, Indian Culture portal), school partnerships.
**Conclusion:** a nation that can argue honestly with its memory will not be afraid of its future.
