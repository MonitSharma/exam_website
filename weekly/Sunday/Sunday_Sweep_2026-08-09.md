## Mains skeletons

### M1 (GS2) — cooperative institutions, competitive incentives, fiscal tension

**Intro (2 lines).** India's federalism has always been asymmetric in law; what is new is that the asymmetry has migrated from the constitutional text to the fiscal machinery — the GST Council and the Finance Commission are now where the argument happens.

**Body — the cooperative architecture.**

- **GST Council (Art 279A)** as the only body in the Constitution where Union and States vote together on a shared tax base. The voting formula — **Centre 1/3, States 2/3, decisions by 3/4 of weighted votes present and voting** — is deliberately built so neither side can act alone. Most decisions to date have been by consensus rather than vote.
- **Inter-State Council (Art 263)**, **Zonal Councils**, **NITI Aayog's Governing Council** — the institutional layer that replaced command planning with negotiated planning.
- **Finance Commission (Art 280)** as the constitutional referee: the **16th FC** (Arvind Panagariya) covers **2026–31**; vertical devolution and a horizontal formula built on income distance, area, population, demographic performance, forest & ecology and tax effort.

**Body — the competitive incentives.**

- **Competitive federalism by design:** ranking frameworks — Ease of Doing Business, aspirational districts, State investment indices — reward States for out-competing each other, which is efficient but corrodes the collective bargaining States need in the Council.
- **The divisible-pool problem:** cesses and surcharges sit **outside** the divisible pool, so the Centre can raise revenue that never has to be shared. The devolution *percentage* can rise while the devolved *share of gross revenue* falls — this is the single most examinable fact in the whole debate.
- **Post-compensation-cess anxiety:** States surrendered autonomous indirect-tax power for a guarantee that has since lapsed. The bargain's second half is what is now contested.
- **Conditional transfers:** centrally sponsored schemes with matching-share requirements convert untied fiscal space into tied fiscal space, and the States' own priorities into the Centre's.

**The synthesis.** The tension is not Centre-versus-States; it is between **two conceptions of the Union** — the Union as a shared enterprise with negotiated rules, and the Union as a principal contracting with agents. The GST Council embodies the first; the cess-and-CSS route embodies the second. Both are constitutional.

**Way forward.** A statutory ceiling or sunset on cesses and surcharges; a formal dispute-resolution mechanism under Art 279A(11), which the Constitution provides for but which has never been constituted; greater untied transfers with outcome rather than input conditionality; a revived Inter-State Council meeting on a fixed calendar.

**Conclusion.** Federalism survives on the credibility of its bargains. India's fiscal institutions are strong enough to be worth defending and asymmetric enough to be worth reforming — the two propositions are not in conflict.

### M2 (GS1) — Chauri Chaura and the movement Gandhi would not lead

**Intro.** On 5 February 1922 a crowd at Chauri Chaura burned a police station and killed twenty-two policemen; within a week Gandhi had withdrawn a movement at its peak. The question is whether the withdrawal was a judgement about **violence** or a judgement about **control**.

**The case that it revealed an ungovernable movement.**

- The Non-Cooperation programme had by 1921–22 attracted constituencies with their **own grievances**, not Gandhi's: Awadh's kisan sabhas, Malabar's Moplah peasants, Assam's tea-garden labour, the Akali agitation. The Congress's authority over them was nominal.
- Mass mobilisation had outrun mass discipline. **Satyagraha requires trained satyagrahis**; a movement of millions cannot be trained.
- The Bardoli no-tax campaign, planned as the next escalation, would have converted a political movement into an agrarian one — precisely the transformation Gandhi feared, because a no-tax campaign puts class conflict, not colonial rule, at the centre.
- Gandhi's own explanation is consistent with this: he called the violence a warning from God and said the country was "not ready".

**The case that the withdrawal was a failure of nerve.**

- **C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru and Lajpat Rai** were in jail and reacted with anger; Subhas Bose called it a "national calamity" that the movement should be halted "when public enthusiasm was at boiling point".
- The Raj was, on several contemporary assessments, genuinely rattled; withdrawal relieved the pressure at the moment of maximum leverage.
- The immediate cost was demoralisation, the fragmentation of 1922–29 into Swarajists and No-changers, and the drift of a younger cohort towards **revolutionary terrorism (HRA/HSRA)** and towards **socialism** — the very radicalisation Gandhi wished to avoid.
- The Hindu–Muslim unity built on the Khilafat platform did not survive; communal violence rose sharply after 1922.

**The synthesis.** Both readings are partly right, and the sharper point is that they are not really in conflict. Gandhi's non-violence was **not a tactic that failed but a constraint that held** — and a constraint of that kind necessarily forfeits opportunities that an unconstrained movement would take. What Chauri Chaura revealed is that Gandhi was leading a movement whose social base he had created but whose social objectives he did not share.

**Conclusion.** The withdrawal cost the movement a moment and bought it a method. Whether that was a bargain depends on whether one believes 1922 could have delivered independence — and the Raj's post-1922 recovery suggests it could not.

## GS4 case study — model approach

**(a) Ethical issues.**

1. **Legality versus legitimacy.** The Gram Sabha resolution is procedurally valid and substantively hollow. The central ethical fact of the case is that *nothing here is illegal* — which is exactly why an officer's judgement, and not merely his compliance, is what is being tested.
2. **Conflict of interest.** The *sarpanch*'s family connection to the bidder is undisclosed and unrecorded. Even if the decision would have been identical without it, the **appearance** of interest destroys the Gram Sabha's standing as the community's voice.
3. **Free, prior and informed consent.** The pastoralists were not effectively informed. Consent that is uninformed is not consent, whatever the attendance register says.
4. **Distributive justice.** The benefits — 40,000 households, 120 jobs, 18,000 tCO₂e — are large, diffuse and mostly accrue to people who are not bearing the cost. The cost is concentrated on **300 landless families** who are among the least powerful in the block, and it falls on a commons, which is the only productive asset the landless typically have.
5. **Utilitarian calculus versus rights.** The aggregate case for the plant is strong. That is precisely the structure of argument under which the poor are usually dispossessed — the numbers are always larger on the other side.
6. **Institutional pressure versus independent judgement.** The Collector's instruction is lawful, and the deadline pressure is real. Obedience is a duty; abdication of judgement is not.
7. **Moral hazard and precedent.** If a thinly attended Gram Sabha with an interested mover suffices once, it becomes the template for every subsequent land transfer in the district.

**(b) Options.**

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| Process the transfer as instructed | Meets the deadline; legally defensible; district retains the allocation; large public benefit realised quickly | Ratifies a tainted process; dispossesses 300 landless families without genuine consent; sets a precedent; near-certain litigation later, which will delay the plant far longer than a re-convened Gram Sabha would |
| Refuse and escalate a complaint against the *sarpanch* | Morally clean; addresses the conflict of interest squarely | Slow, adversarial, and treats a solvable process defect as a disciplinary matter; likely loses the allocation for the whole district; punishes 40,000 households for one man's conduct |
| **Re-convene the Gram Sabha with due notice, disclosure and a documented alternative-site search — in parallel, not in sequence** | Cures the consent defect at its source; disclosure neutralises the conflict of interest; keeps the project alive; produces a record that survives audit and litigation | Requires 2–4 weeks; risks the quarterly deadline; requires you to tell the Collector something he does not want to hear |
| Approve subject to a compensation/rehabilitation package for the pastoralists | Fast; addresses distributive harm | Buys consent rather than obtaining it; commons are typically undercompensated because they are unpriced; leaves the procedural defect intact |

**(c) Course of action — reasoned.**

I would **not process the transfer on the present record**, and I would say so to the Collector in writing on the same day, together with a concrete plan to save the project rather than merely to stall it.

Concretely: **(i)** commission, within 72 hours, a **documented alternative-site search** — three of four sites are uncontroversial, which suggests site four is a convenience rather than a necessity, and revenue records will settle that quickly; **(ii)** **re-convene the Gram Sabha** with statutory notice, active outreach to the pastoralist households, and a mandatory written **disclosure of interest** by the *sarpanch*, who should recuse himself from moving or voting on the item; **(iii)** if, after genuine deliberation, the Gram Sabha still approves and no alternative site is viable, **approve the transfer with binding conditions** — a fodder-security guarantee or equivalent land substitution for the affected families, and a feedstock-procurement commitment that channels payments to them; **(iv)** put the **deadline problem on the record with the Collector and the State nodal department in parallel**, requesting a short extension on documented grounds, so that the timeline risk is managed by the system rather than absorbed by 300 families.

**The reasoning.** The Collector's premise — "it is legally valid" — is true and insufficient. The Gram Sabha is not a formality to be satisfied; it is the mechanism by which the least powerful people in the block are supposed to be heard, and a resolution they did not know about does not perform that function. Equally, refusing outright would be self-indulgent: the environmental and livelihood benefits are real, and an officer who protects his conscience by losing his district's allocation has traded 40,000 households for his own comfort. The right response to a **procedural** defect is to **cure the procedure**, quickly and visibly, not to accept it and not to abandon the project.

**On the deadline.** A deadline is a reason to act fast, not a reason to act wrongly. And the practical arithmetic favours doing it properly: a tainted transfer of grazing commons is the single most litigable land action available, and a stay order two years from now will cost the project far more than three weeks costs it today.

**Values demonstrated:** objectivity, empathy for the weakest stakeholder, transparency (disclosure and recusal), courage in dissenting **upward and on the record**, and **practical wisdom** — I neither obey nor resign; I keep the project alive and fix what is broken about it.

## Essay — "The rules that bind the powerful are written when they are not yet powerful"

**Direction.** This is a proposition about **the timing of constraint**, and the essay must resist reading it as a cynical claim. The thesis to develop: *rules acquire legitimacy from being written behind a veil of uncertainty about who will be bound by them — and lose it as soon as their authors know the answer.* The essay's real subject is therefore **institutional design under uncertainty**, and its best move is to show that this is a feature to be engineered, not an accident to be lamented.

**Structure.**

1. **Opening (concrete, not abstract).** A founding moment where people wrote rules they could not be sure would favour them. The Constituent Assembly is the obvious choice; better ones exist — the drafters of the Election Commission's powers, or the framers of the Finance Commission, none of whom knew which party or which State they would be in a decade later.
2. **Section 1 — Why uncertainty produces fairness.** Rawls's veil of ignorance as the formal statement of the intuition. But move quickly to concrete cases: constitutional amendment procedures, judicial independence, term limits, the anti-defection framework, arbitration clauses agreed before a dispute exists. In each, the drafters restrain a future self they cannot identify.
3. **Section 2 — Why the window closes.** Once power crystallises, rule-making becomes rule-shaping. Gerrymandering; the drift of emergency powers; the tendency of every regulator to be captured by those it regulates; the 42nd Amendment as the case where a Parliament rewrote the rules it was itself operating under. The general law: **the incentive to constrain is inversely proportional to the probability that the constraint will bind you.**
4. **Section 3 — Beyond politics.** Keep at least a third of the essay outside the constitutional register, or the examiner will have read it before. Scientific method as a rule written by researchers who do not yet know whether their hypothesis will survive it. Peer review, pre-registration of trials, blind auditions in orchestras. Competition law written before national champions exist. International rules — the Law of the Sea, the NPT, the emerging AI-governance debate — where the timing question is live right now: *the states writing AI rules today are precisely the ones who do not yet know if they will lead.*
5. **Section 4 — Counter-argument, taken seriously.** The proposition is too neat. Some of the best rules were written by the powerful against their own interest — the abolition of the slave trade by the world's leading slave-trading power; Ashoka's edicts; the voluntary surrender of privilege. Conversely, the powerless sometimes write rules that are merely wishful, because the capacity to enforce is itself a form of power. **A rule with no enforcer is a preference.**
6. **Section 5 — Designing for the closing window.** The constructive turn. Sunset clauses. Automatic review triggers. Rules that take effect only after a delay long enough to obscure who benefits — the "veil of time" as a practical substitute for the veil of ignorance. Supermajority requirements for changing the rules of rule-changing. Independent commissions insulated from the government of the day. **Article 368's graded majorities and the basic-structure limit are exactly this technology.**
7. **Conclusion.** The proposition is not a warning about hypocrisy; it is an instruction about *when* to act. A society that recognises the window will spend its uncertain moments writing constraints rather than seizing advantages — and will be governed, decades later, by people who never had the chance to exempt themselves.

**Fodder to have ready:** Rawls's original position; Ulysses and the mast (pre-commitment); Madison in *Federalist* 51 — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"; Ambedkar's warning that constitutional morality is not natural and must be cultivated; the 42nd and 44th Amendments as a rule-writing sequence; Acemoglu–Robinson on inclusive versus extractive institutions; North on institutions as the rules of the game; Elster on constitutional pre-commitment; the Montreal Protocol as rules written before the interests hardened; the Nuremberg principles.

**Traps to avoid:** do not write a polity answer with headings; do not turn it into a catalogue of Indian constitutional provisions; do not let cynicism become the thesis — the essay should end constructive, because the interesting claim is not that the powerful escape rules but that societies can *design for the moment before they do.*
