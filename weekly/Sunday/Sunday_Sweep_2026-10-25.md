### GS Mains — answer skeletons

**M1 — China's TBM export controls and supply-chain resilience (GS2/GS3)**
- **Intro:** from **18 Nov 2026**, China will require export licences for tunnel-boring machines under its dual-use rules. India's metro, rail, highway and hydro tunnelling depends heavily on imported TBMs. The 2025 hold-up of three Herrenknecht machines is the precedent.
- **Implications:** project delays and cost overruns (metros, Himalayan rail and road tunnels, pumped storage); leverage in the India–China normalisation; a pattern after rare-earth and fertiliser curbs (*weaponised interdependence*); risk to strategic border infrastructure.
- **Measures:** diversify suppliers (Germany, Japan, Korea); localise manufacturing (Herrenknecht's India plant; PLI for capital goods; the Make in India capital-goods policy); refurbish and reuse TBMs; standardise specifications across agencies; build component ecosystems (cutters, bearings); strategic stockpiles for critical spares; Quad/IPEF supply-chain cooperation.
- **Conclusion:** resilience comes from *redundancy and domestic capability*, not from decoupling.

**M2 — UDAN at ten (GS3)**
- **Intro:** UDAN (Regional Connectivity Scheme under NCAP 2016) began on **21 Oct 2016**. Operational airports rose from **74 (2014) to 166 (2026)**; **687 routes** and about **1.72 crore passengers** have flown under it.
- **Achievements:** tier-2/3 connectivity, helicopters and seaplanes for hilly and island regions, Krishi UDAN, regional airlines' growth, a demand-led bidding model with VGF.
- **Weaknesses:** many routes closed once VGF ended; weak regional airlines; airports built but under-used; the capacity of AAI; seaplane and helicopter operations stalled.
- **Viksit UDAN (FY27–FY36, ~₹28,840 cr):** **100 airports from unserved airstrips**, **200 helipads**, O&M support for ~441 aerodromes, VGF of up to five years per route. This targets the supply side and running costs, the main gaps.
- **Way forward:** route viability studies, State co-funding, a tie-up with tourism and GatiShakti, stronger regional carriers, and outcome-based VGF.
- **Conclusion:** the first decade built the network; the second must make it last.

### GS4 case — model approach

- **Stakeholders:** town residents (drinking water); farmers with standing crops; plant workers and the owner; the ethanol programme; upstream dam users; your staff; the public's trust in fair allocation.
- **Ethical issues:** **distributive justice under scarcity**; **priority of the right to drinking water** (Art 21; the National Water Policy places drinking water first); **undue influence** (the donor owner); **transparency** (stale data, upstream over-drawing); **public order vs legitimate protest**; the plant's jobs vs farmers' livelihoods.
- **Options:**
  1. *Give the plant an assured allocation* → favouritism under influence; breaks the priority order. Reject.
  2. *Keep canal releases unchanged to avoid unrest* → risks the town's drinking water in six weeks. Reject.
  3. *Cut industry fully* → defensible legally, but ignores 1,500 jobs and possible water reuse at the plant.
  4. **Transparent, priority-based rationing on fresh data** → recommended.
- **Course of action:** (i) Order an **immediate fresh storage and inflow assessment**, and stop the **upstream over-drawing** at once; (ii) **reserve drinking water first** for the full lean season, with a buffer; (iii) set **canal releases by crop stage** (protective irrigation for crops close to harvest; advice and support for others, e.g. crop insurance claims and MGNREGA work); (iv) give the plant **a reduced, metered allocation** conditional on water recycling and on shifting to treated sewage water where possible; (v) **publish the allocation and the data** and hold an all-party meeting with farmer groups before any blockade; (vi) reply to the owner in writing that allocation follows a published priority order; (vii) report to the State for drought declaration and tanker contingency.
- **Values:** impartiality, transparency, compassion, accountability, courage. Close with **"fair rules, openly applied, are the best defence against both pressure and protest."**

### Essay — model approach: "Interdependence is both a shield and a sword."

- **Hook:** in 2025–26, the same countries that traded freely with each other began licensing rare earths, fertilisers and tunnel-boring machines. The pipes that carried goods now carry pressure.
- **Thesis:** interdependence protects (it raises the cost of conflict and spreads prosperity) but it can also be used as a weapon when dependence is lopsided. The answer is not to retreat from the world but to make dependence *mutual and diversified*.
- **Dimensions (pick 5–6):**
  1. *History:* the "liberal peace" argument (Norman Angell, 1910) and its failure in 1914; post-1945 European integration as the shield working.
  2. *Economic:* global value chains; China's export controls; sanctions on Russian oil and their effect on India's energy choices; "friend-shoring".
  3. *Technology:* semiconductors, critical minerals, and the PKI and cloud systems a country does not control (the eMudhra episode as a small domestic example).
  4. *Climate:* the shared atmosphere; carbon border taxes (CBAM) as interdependence turned into a trade weapon; climate finance as the shield.
  5. *Health:* vaccine supply chains in COVID-19; India's "pharmacy of the world" role as soft power.
  6. *Society and federalism:* water-sharing between States (Cauvery) as interdependence at home; it calls for institutions, not force.
  7. *India's strategy:* strategic autonomy, multi-alignment, INSTC and IMEC as diversified corridors, Atmanirbhar Bharat as capacity-building, not autarky.
- **Counter-view:** some argue full self-reliance is safer. Answer: autarky is costly and slows growth; the safer path is redundancy and domestic capability in a few critical nodes.
- **Way forward:** diversify suppliers, build domestic capacity in critical sectors, create rules-based institutions (WTO reform, supply-chain compacts), and keep stockpiles of strategic goods.
- **Conclusion:** the world cannot un-tie its knots. Nations should hold the threads in many hands, including their own.
