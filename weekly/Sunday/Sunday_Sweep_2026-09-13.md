### M1 — GS2 skeleton (local government finance and State Finance Commissions)

**Intro (2–3 lines).** The 73rd and 74th Amendments constitutionalised *elections* to local bodies, not *resources* for them. Part IX and Part IXA mandate that a panchayat exist, be elected and be reconstituted on time; they say almost nothing about what it may spend. The result is a third tier with democratic legitimacy and fiscal dependence.

**Body 1 — the design gap.** The Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules are **lists of subjects, not transfers of function**; devolution of each is left to the State legislature (Art 243G, 243W). **Art 243H and 243X** *permit* the State to authorise taxes, duties, tolls and fees — permissive language, not mandatory. So the amendment created an obligation to hold elections and a discretion to fund.

**Body 2 — the State Finance Commission, and why it underperforms.** **Art 243-I and 243Y** require an SFC every five years to recommend the distribution of State taxes, the assignment of taxes to local bodies, and grants-in-aid, with the report and an **action-taken report laid before the State legislature**. In practice: delayed constitution, delayed reports, non-uniform data, and recommendations accepted in principle and not implemented. The **Central Finance Commission** has repeatedly conditioned local-body grants on SFC compliance — a workaround that concedes the problem.

**Body 3 — what local bodies actually have.** Own revenue (property tax and its low buoyancy, user charges, professional tax, advertisement and licence fees); assigned revenue; CFC and SFC transfers; scheme funds, which are **tied** and therefore not fiscal autonomy; and, for larger municipalities, **municipal bonds**, **pooled finance**, **land-value capture** and **PPP** — instruments that presuppose creditworthiness most cities do not have.

**Body 4 — what would make it credible.** Timely and technically staffed SFCs on a fixed statutory calendar; a floor share of State divisible pool rather than ad hoc grants; property-tax reform (GIS mapping, periodic revision of the assessment base, collection efficiency); untied funds as a rising share of transfers; audited annual accounts as the precondition for market borrowing; and functional devolution *matched* to fiscal devolution, so that funds, functions and functionaries move together.

**Conclusion.** Decentralisation that transfers responsibility without revenue does not empower the local body; it relocates the blame. The credibility of the third tier will be settled in State budget documents, not in constitutional text.

*Use the DPC/MPC point as a supporting example — planning bodies exist on paper in most States and meet rarely, which is the same failure in a different institution.*

### M2 — GS3 skeleton (price subsidies vs income transfers)

**Intro.** A price subsidy alters the signal the market sends; an income transfer alters the budget the household holds. The choice between them is not primarily a choice about generosity — it is a choice about **where the distortion sits**.

**Body 1 — the case for income transfers.** Removes the price distortion (urea underpricing → N:P:K imbalance, soil damage, over-application; free or near-free power → groundwater depletion). Improves targeting via DBT and Aadhaar seeding. Reduces the physical costs of the PDS chain — procurement, storage, transport, and the FCI's carrying cost. Preserves consumer choice; a transfer can be spent on pulses or protein rather than only on cereals. International and Indian precedents: **PM-KISAN**, **PAHAL/DBTL for LPG**, Telangana's **Rythu Bandhu**, and the Jharkhand DBT-in-fertiliser pilot.

**Body 2 — the efficiency objections to making the switch.** Transfers are indexed poorly against food-price inflation, so the real value erodes. Market failures do not disappear: thin and monopsonistic rural markets, no assurance that grain will be available at a reasonable price in remote areas, and the procurement system's second function — **buffer stocks and price stabilisation** — is not replaced by cash. Fertiliser subsidy withdrawal without a working credit market can cause an input-use collapse in the season it happens.

**Body 3 — the equity objections, which are different.** Exclusion error is the mirror of leakage: Aadhaar-linked systems fail for the migrant, the manual labourer with worn fingerprints, the household without a bank branch in reach. Intra-household allocation matters — grain that arrives as grain is more likely to be eaten by women and children than cash that arrives in one account. Land-titling gaps mean a per-acre transfer reaches the owner, not the **tenant or sharecropper** who actually farms — which connects directly to the tenancy-recording failure of India's land reforms.

**Body 4 — a defensible position.** Sequence rather than switch: phase transfers in where markets are deep and banking penetration is high; retain physical distribution where they are not; reform the price signal *within* the existing instrument first (bring urea into NBS, raise the fertiliser price gradually with a compensating per-hectare transfer); fix the tenant-identification problem before making land the basis of transfer; and keep buffer-stock and procurement functions distinct from the welfare function, since only one of them is a subsidy.

**Conclusion.** The efficiency case for transfers is strong and the equity case is conditional — conditional on identification, banking access and market depth. Where those three hold, transfers dominate; where they do not, a price subsidy is a crude instrument doing a job nothing else is currently doing.

### GS4 case study — model approach

**(a) Ethical issues.**

- **Legality versus formal compliance.** A consultation that fails quorum and was conducted in a language the participants do not use for formal business is a consultation in form and not in substance. Certifying it would be **procedurally correct and substantively false** — the precise failure mode that hollows out PESA.
- **Free, prior and informed consent** as a substantive right of a Scheduled Tribe community, not a box in a file. "Prior" and "informed" are doing real work in that phrase; short notice on a market day defeats both.
- **Conflict between hierarchical loyalty and legal duty.** The Collector's instruction is not an illegal order in explicit terms, but complying with it requires you to attest to something you know to be defective.
- **Inducement by benefit.** The residential school is genuinely needed. That is what makes it dangerous: it invites you to treat a public good as compensation for a procedural violation, and to substitute your own judgement of the community's interest for the community's.
- **Development versus consent** — 200 jobs against 40 hectares — and the honest recognition that the trade-off is real, that reasonable people weigh it differently, and that **the decision on how to weigh it is not yours to make; it belongs to the Gram Sabha.**
- **Personal integrity and moral courage** against a low-cost, low-visibility path of least resistance.

**(b) Options.**

1. **Clear the file as directed.** *Merits:* protects the working relationship, keeps the investment and the jobs, may deliver the school. *Demerits:* certifies a defective process; exposes the lease to legal challenge and eventual quashing, which serves nobody including the company; sets a precedent in a district where you will face the same file again; and abdicates the specific statutory protection you exist to administer. **Rejected.**
2. **Reject the application outright.** *Merits:* decisive, unambiguous. *Demerits:* exceeds what the defect warrants — the flaw is in the *process*, not necessarily the *proposal* — and substitutes your judgement for the Gram Sabha's just as surely as option 1 does. **Rejected.**
3. **Return the file for a fresh Gram Sabha conducted properly.** *Merits:* corrects the actual defect; respects the community's authority to say yes *or* no; produces a decision that will survive scrutiny; costs six weeks. *Demerits:* delay; possible loss of the investment; friction with the Collector and the MLA. **Preferred.**
4. **Refer upward for instructions without recommendation.** *Merits:* transfers risk. *Demerits:* evades a decision squarely within your competence; the six weeks pass anyway.

**(c) Course of action.**

I would adopt option 3, and do it on the record. Concretely: put a **written note on the file** setting out the quorum shortfall against the prescribed rule and the substance of the three complaints, and **return it for a fresh Gram Sabha**. Direct that the fresh meeting be convened with **adequate written notice, on a non-market day, at a time fixed in consultation with the hamlets, with proceedings conducted in the language the community actually uses**, with **videography and an independent observer**, and with the **full project information — area, duration, expected employment, environmental effects and rehabilitation terms — circulated in advance** so that "informed" means something.

I would **meet the Collector in person before recording the note**, explain that the defect is one that would void the lease on challenge, and place the reasoning on paper rather than leaving it as an oral disagreement — protecting both of us and, more importantly, the record. I would tell the company that the **school offer must be considered entirely separately** from the lease and cannot form part of the consultation, and that a properly obtained consent is in its own interest, since a challenged lease is worth less than a slower one.

If the fresh Gram Sabha consents, the file proceeds with my recommendation. **If it refuses, that refusal is the outcome the law provides for, and I would forward it as such.** The point of PESA is not to produce consent; it is to make the answer the community's to give.

**Values demonstrated:** integrity, objectivity, courage of conviction, empathy towards the weaker sections, and the distinction between accountability to a superior and responsibility to the law.

### Essay — model approach: *"Power devolved is not power delivered."*

**What the statement is actually claiming.** Devolution is a transfer of *authority*; delivery is the transfer of *capacity, resources and consequence*. The sentence asserts that the first can occur completely while the second does not occur at all — and that this gap is not accidental but structural, because devolving authority is cheap and devolving resources is not.

**Structure.**

**Opening.** Resist the temptation to open with the 73rd Amendment. Open with a concrete image — a sarpanch with a constitutional mandate, a five-year term, a Schedule of twenty-nine subjects, and no untied money to repair a hand pump. Then state the thesis: the Indian experience with decentralisation is not a story of resistance to devolution, but of devolution *without* its three enabling conditions — funds, functions and functionaries.

**Part 1 — Devolution as a democratic idea.** The moral case: subsidiarity; decisions taken closest to those they affect; local knowledge as an information advantage no central planner can replicate; participation as a good in itself, not merely a means. Gandhi's *gram swaraj* against Ambedkar's scepticism about the village as a site of hierarchy — **use the disagreement, do not resolve it**, because both were right about something. The global lineage: the Panchayati Raj amendments, but also the European principle of subsidiarity and the American federal debate.

**Part 2 — Why devolved power fails to deliver.** Fiscal dependence (permissive taxation, weak SFCs, tied scheme funds). Capacity (a panchayat secretary shared across villages; no technical staff; no procurement expertise). Parallel bodies and line departments that retain the functionaries. Elite capture, the *sarpanch pati* phenomenon, and the difference between **representation and substantive participation**. Information asymmetry, which makes accountability formal rather than real. And the political economy point that ought to be stated plainly: **State governments devolve reluctantly for the same reason the Union devolves reluctantly to States** — power surrendered is rarely recovered.

**Part 3 — Where devolution has delivered, and what those cases share.** Kerala's People's Plan and the earmarking of plan funds. Operation Barga's lesson that *recording* a right delivers more than *conferring* it. Community forest rights where the Gram Sabha holds and exercises real authority. Water-user associations and self-help group federations. The common feature in every success: **an untied resource, a recorded entitlement, and a functionary answerable locally.** Not a slogan — a mechanism.

**Part 4 — Beyond local government.** Widen the frame so this does not read as a Polity answer in essay clothing: delegation in an organisation without the authority to spend; national sovereignty without economic capacity; rights conferred by statute without a forum to enforce them; digital access granted without literacy to use it. The pattern is general — **the transfer of a right is the beginning of empowerment, not its completion.**

**Part 5 — What would close the gap.** Fiscal: statutory SFC calendars, a floor share of the divisible pool, untied funds, property-tax reform. Administrative: cadre and technical support at the block level, activity mapping so each Schedule subject has a named owner. Accountability: social audit, RTI at the local level, timely audited accounts. Political: reservation deepened into training and tenure protection so representation becomes participation.

**Conclusion.** Return to the opening image and close it. The measure of decentralisation is not the number of elected representatives — India has around three million, more than any other country on earth — but **whether the citizen knows whom to ask, and whether that person can say yes.** Power devolved is a promise; power delivered is a hand pump that works.
