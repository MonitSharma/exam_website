### 1. Washington arms itself with a 100% tariff switch aimed at buyers of Russian oil — and India is named in the frame — International Relations

**The headline.** On **18 September 2026** the US President signed the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026** (H.R. 5334) into law. Its central instrument is *not* a sanction on Russia at all: it authorises the President to impose tariffs of **up to 100%** on the **five largest importers of Russian crude oil or natural gas** — a description that fits **India and China** before anyone is named.

**The exam angle.** This is the cleanest live case study available on **secondary sanctions** — measures that punish *third countries* for lawful trade with a sanctioned state — and therefore on the limits of **strategic autonomy** in a world where the dominant currency and the dominant market belong to the same power. The second angle is the **discretion structure**: the Act creates *authority*, not automaticity, and preserves a **presidential waiver**. That distinction (enabling statute vs. self-executing sanction) is exactly the sort of thing a Prelims statement will get wrong on purpose. The third is India's standing reply — energy security for 1.4 billion people, sourcing by "evolving market dynamics" — which is the quotable Mains line on the difference between *alignment* and *convergence*.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **Full title:** the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026**; bill number **H.R. 5334**. Named for **Senator Lindsey Graham**, who died in **July 2026**.
- **Passage:** **Senate 86–11** (August 2026); **House 262–159** (**16 September 2026**); **signed 18 September 2026**.
- **The tariff provision:** authorises tariffs of **up to 100%** on goods from the **five largest purchasers of Russian oil or gas**, and on countries facilitating evasion of Russian oil sanctions. It **does not automatically impose** a tariff on any named country; the President retains **waiver authority** under specified conditions.
- **Other limbs:** sanctions on **President Putin**, Russian officials, oligarchs, banks and financial institutions; targets Russia's **"shadow fleet"** of tankers; and at the US President's request, **extends existing sanctions on Iran's energy and weapons sectors for five years**.
- **The pre-existing baseline:** the US already levies **50% tariffs on India**, of which **25 percentage points** were imposed specifically for purchases of Russian energy. This Act is an *additional* lever on top of that.
- **India's response (MEA):** India "remains firmly committed to ensuring **energy security** for its **1.4 billion** people", will continue **diversified sourcing** "on the basis of **evolving market dynamics**", and will "take all necessary measures" to protect its interests. The MEA added that it had raised the legislation's implications — for the bilateral relationship *and* for the international energy market — with US interlocutors at high levels in recent months.
- **The static vocabulary to keep straight:** **primary sanctions** (bar the sanctioning state's own persons from dealing with the target) versus **secondary sanctions** (penalise third-country persons); **extraterritoriality**; **CAATSA** (Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act, **2017**) — under which India's **S-400** purchase drew a waiver debate; and the **price cap** mechanism operated by the **G7 + EU + Australia** on seaborne Russian crude.
- **Why India buys:** Russian crude went from a negligible share of Indian imports pre-2022 to roughly a third of the barrel mix at peak, on discounted **Urals** grades. The counter-argument India has used publicly is that the West's own **pipeline gas and LNG** purchases from Russia continued alongside.

**Destination notebook:** GS2 — International Relations (India–US, India–Russia, effect of policies of developed countries on India's interests); cross-tag GS3 — Economy (energy security, external sector).

**Static anchor:** **your India–US and India–Russia bilateral cards**, plus the **energy security** section of the **Mrunal Economy pillar (external sector / BoP)**. *Nudge:* on the India–US card, open a running sub-head "**Coercive economic statecraft**" and list, in date order, **CAATSA (2017) → 25% Russian-oil tariff → 50% total tariff → Graham Act (18 Sept 2026, up to 100%, waiver retained)**. Cross-link from the **crude oil import basket** page in your Economy notes with one line — "**secondary-sanction risk is now a variable in the sourcing decision, not just price**".

### 2. The FCRA rewrite reaches its committee stage — and the fight is over who keeps the assets — Polity & Governance

**The headline.** The **Joint Committee of Parliament on the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026** held its **first meeting** this week. The provision doing all the work is a new **"designated authority"** empowered to take custody of, manage and dispose of foreign-contribution assets of an organisation whose FCRA registration is **cancelled, surrendered or has lapsed**.

**The exam angle.** Three separate syllabus hooks sit in one bill. (i) **Civil society and the regulatory state** — the classic GS2 theme of NGOs, accountability and the space for dissent. (ii) **Due process** — vesting property in a state authority without a prior hearing raises **Article 300A** (right to property, a constitutional right since the 44th Amendment, no longer a fundamental right) and **Article 14** reasonableness. (iii) **Parliamentary procedure** — a **Joint Committee**, not a Department-related Standing Committee, which is a distinction Prelims tests directly. Keep in mind that the current parent statute is the **FCRA, 2010**, not the 1976 Act it replaced; aggregators still get this wrong.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **The Bill:** the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026**; introduced in the **Lok Sabha on 25 March 2026**; referred to a **Joint Parliamentary Committee on 12 August 2026** amid Opposition protest.
- **Committee composition:** **21 members nominated by the Lok Sabha Speaker** and **10 by the Rajya Sabha Chairman** — a **31-member** committee. Report due in the **first week of the Winter Session**.
- **The core amendment:** creation of a **"Designated Authority"** to take **provisional custody** of foreign-contribution assets when FCRA registration ends. Assets are **returned if registration is restored**; if it is not restored within the prescribed period, the vesting can become **permanent**, and the authority may **transfer the assets to a Ministry, department, agency or local authority, or dispose of them**.
- **The gap it claims to fill:** **Section 15 of the FCRA, 2010** provides that assets vest in a prescribed authority on cancellation, but sets out no procedure — no rules for taking possession, maintaining inventories, or **separating foreign-funded assets from domestically funded ones**.
- **The objections raised in committee:** the designated authority would wield **wide, largely unchecked powers**; there is **no express provision for a prior hearing or judicial review**; and mixed-funding assets are hard to disentangle. Ruling-party members pressed instead on the **utilisation** of foreign contributions; the **Home Ministry** defended the amendments on grounds of **national security, transparency and accountability**.
- **Statutory lineage:** **FCRA, 1976**, enacted amid Cold War anxieties about foreign influence on Indian politics → repealed and replaced by the **FCRA, 2010** → tightened by the **FCRA (Amendment) Act, 2020** (mandatory **SBI New Delhi Main Branch** FCRA account; **Aadhaar** for office-bearers; **sub-granting to other FCRA entities prohibited**; administrative-expense cap cut from **50% to 20%**).
- **Who administers it:** the **Ministry of Home Affairs** — not the Finance Ministry. Registration is for **five years**, renewable.
- **The constitutional frame for an answer:** **Article 19(1)(c)** (freedom to form associations), read with the **reasonable restrictions** in **Article 19(4)** on grounds including sovereignty, integrity and public order; and **Article 300A** on deprivation of property only by authority of law.

**Destination notebook:** GS2 — Polity & Governance (statutory bodies, role of civil society/NGOs, government policies and interventions).

**Static anchor:** **Laxmikanth — "Parliamentary Committees" chapter** and the **Pressure Groups / NGOs** material in the Governance section. *Nudge:* in the Parliamentary Committees chapter, add a live example line against **Joint Committee on a Bill** — "**FCRA (Amendment) Bill, 2026 — JPC of 31 (21 LS + 10 RS), referred 12 Aug 2026**" — and keep a separate running **FCRA timeline card** (1976 → 2010 → 2020 amendment → 2026 Bill) so you can answer either the statute question or the civil-society question from one page.

### 3. Eco-Sensitive Zones: notified, filed, and then forgotten — Environment

**The headline.** A *Down To Earth* analysis makes an argument that is uncomfortable precisely because it is administrative rather than dramatic: for most protected areas the ESZ story ends at the **gazette notification**, because the **Zonal Management Plan** that was supposed to give the zone operational content was never written — and the **Zonal Management Committee** meant to supervise it exists, where it exists at all, without budget, secretariat or authority.

**The exam angle.** ESZs are a **repeat-tested Prelims topic** (see the PYQ mirror below) and the examiner's favourite trap is the legal basis — **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, *not* the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972. For Mains this is a first-rate **implementation-gap** case: the notification *categorises* activities but sets no limits on **scale or intensity**, no **design standards**, no **mitigation requirements**, and no mechanism to assess **cumulative impact**. The forest officer who nominally chairs the committee has jurisdiction only up to the park boundary; the land outside is governed by revenue and town-planning law that does not mention the ESZ at all. That is the sentence to carry into an answer.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **What an ESZ is:** an area **immediately surrounding** a national park or wildlife sanctuary, managed as a **protective buffer / shock absorber** against incompatible development. It **regulates** the character, scale and design of activity — it does **not prohibit** activity as such.
- **Legal basis:** notified by the **Union Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change** under the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**. Conceptual origin in the **National Wildlife Action Plan (2002–2016)**; detailed **MoEFCC guidelines, 2011**.
- **Activity classification:** every notification sorts activities into **prohibited, regulated and permitted**. Typically prohibited: commercial mining, polluting industries, major hydro projects, use of wood for commercial purposes.
- **The missing instrument:** the **Zonal Management Plan (Zonal Master Plan)**, to be prepared *after* notification — specifying extraction limits, setbacks, design standards, wildlife-crossing structures, lighting norms and mitigation requirements.
- **Why a buffer is ecologically necessary:** a park boundary is an **administrative line, not an ecological one**. South Indian elephants range over **300–600 sq km**; few parks approach that. Every park sits in a **watershed** whose streams originate outside it, so paving or draining the catchment degrades the park's hydrology **without a single tree inside being felled**.
- **The three illustrations used (all in Hyderabad):** **KBR National Park** (~**390 ha** of Deccan scrub; lost land to a ring road *before* the ESZ was contemplated, and the ESZ was then drawn to the road's geometry rather than to ecology); **Mrugavani National Park** (physically divided by the **Outer Ring Road**; ESZ deferred indefinitely); **Mahavir Harina Vanasthali Wildlife Sanctuary** (a municipal solid-waste dump alongside it drew **stray dogs** that entered and preyed on **spotted deer**).
- **The invisible stressors worth citing:** urban stormwater and sewage entering park channels, driving **eutrophication** and pathogen load; chronic traffic and construction **noise**, which suppresses bird species richness and shifts composition toward **noise-tolerant generalists**. There is essentially **no water-quality monitoring at drainage entry points** of urban protected areas and **no noise monitoring inside ESZs**.
- **The reforms proposed:** **evidence before boundary** (ecological baseline, hydrological mapping, water-quality audit of incoming drainage, *then* the line); a **statutory two-year deadline** for the Zonal Management Plan; real committees with secretariats, budgets and decision-making powers; and amendment of **State Town and Country Planning Acts** to make ESZ restrictions **mandatory overlays in Master Plans**.
- **The quotable line:** *a managed transition zone without management is simply land that has been labelled and then abandoned.*
- **The judicial backdrop you should already have:** the Supreme Court's evolving position on a **default minimum ESZ** around protected areas — a **1 km** ESZ directed in **2022**, then **modified in 2023** to exempt areas with approved ESZ notifications and to accommodate human settlements and infrastructure.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Environment (conservation, protected-area network); cross-tag GS2 — Governance (implementation gap, centre–state–local coordination).

**Static anchor:** **Shankar IAS — "Protected Area Network" chapter**, the Eco-Sensitive Zone section. *Nudge:* under the ESZ entry, write three lines you can reproduce under pressure — "**EPA 1986, not WLPA 1972**"; "**regulates, does not prohibit**"; "**Zonal Master Plan is the operative instrument — and is usually missing**" — and cross-link to the **EIA Notification, 2006** page, since an ESZ overlay is what an EIA in that landscape *ought* to be screened against.

### 4. FAO's 'Forests for Food' asks the food ministry's question of the forest ministry — Reports & Indices

**The headline.** *Forests for food: Nourishing people, sustaining the planet*, released on **15 September 2026** by the **Food and Agriculture Organization** with the **Landscape Alliance**, argues that forests' contribution to feeding people is almost entirely missing from national statistics and policy frameworks — and that forests should be counted **inside** the agrifood system rather than filed under "environment".

**The exam angle.** Report questions in Prelims turn on **who publishes what**, so pair this immediately with FAO's other titles so you cannot be confused: the biennial **State of the World's Forests (SOFO)**, the **Global Forest Resources Assessment (FRA)**, and — on the food side — **The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI)**. For Mains the report hands you a ready bridge from **nutrition security** to **forest rights**: India's statutory analogue of "forest food" is **Minor Forest Produce** under the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**, which makes this a single answer covering GS3 environment, GS2 tribal rights and GS3 food security.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **The report:** *Forests for food: Nourishing people, sustaining the planet*, **FAO** with the **Landscape Alliance**, **15 September 2026**. **FAO** — a UN specialised agency, founded **1945**, headquartered in **Rome**.
- **More than 5 billion people** worldwide depend to some extent on forests and trees for livelihoods.
- **Over 50,000 wild species** are used for food and medicine, of which **at least 10,000 are consumed directly as food**.
- Forest resources supply as much as **20% of rural incomes** in some areas; **more than 1 billion** people rely on medicinal plants for healthcare; **woodfuel** remains essential for cooking or water sterilisation for about **a quarter** of the global population.
- **Forested watersheds supply freshwater to more than 85% of major cities.**
- **Regional figures worth one line each:** parts of **Africa** where communities obtain up to **80% of daily protein** from wild meat; the **Amazon**, where fish are **44%** of total food consumed by weight; **Europe**, where **90%** of respondents across 28 countries eat at least one forest food a year.
- **The Indian evidence cited:** children in areas of greater forest cover show **more diverse diets and lower stunting**, and a study in eastern India linked wild-food consumption to higher dietary diversity among women, **particularly in the lean season**.
- **The pressure:** an estimated **10.9 million hectares** of forest deforested **annually in the decade to 2025**; around **645 million** people experienced chronic hunger in **2025**.
- **FAO's asks:** sustainable forest management, **agroforestry**, better land-use planning, **secure land rights**, stronger data systems, integrated agriculture-and-forestry policy, and participation of forest-dependent communities.
- **The Indian instruments to attach:** **Minor Forest Produce** — defined in **Section 2(i) of the Forest Rights Act, 2006** as all **non-timber forest produce of plant origin**, including bamboo, brushwood, stumps, cane, tussar, cocoons, honey and wax — with **ownership, access, use and disposal** rights under **Section 3(1)(c)**; the **MSP for MFP** scheme; **Van Dhan Vikas Kendras** under **TRIFED** (Ministry of Tribal Affairs); and the **National Agroforestry Policy, 2014**, India being the first country to adopt one.
- **The Indian data counterpart:** the biennial **India State of Forest Report** by the **Forest Survey of India** (Dehradun) — the domestic mirror of FAO's FRA.
- **The analytical caution to include:** counting forest foods in nutrition policy also imports their limits — **extraction sustainability** and **zoonotic risk** from wild meat — which is why the report couples the claim with sustainable management rather than simple promotion.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Environment & Food Security; cross-tag GS2 — Social Justice (tribal rights, FRA) and GS3 — Agriculture (agroforestry).

**Static anchor:** **Shankar IAS — "Forests / Biodiversity" chapters**, and your **Forest Rights Act, 2006** card. *Nudge:* start a single page titled "**Forest foods & MFP**" that carries the FAO headline numbers on one side and the Indian statutory stack (**FRA 2006 §2(i) and §3(1)(c) → MSP for MFP → Van Dhan → National Agroforestry Policy 2014**) on the other; and on your **FAO publications** card add *Forests for Food (2026)* beneath **SOFO**, **FRA** and **SOFI** so the four never blur.

### 5. The Fed raises rates for the first time since 2023 — and the transmission runs straight through India's external account — Economy

**The headline.** At its **September 2026** meeting the **Federal Open Market Committee** voted **12–0** to raise the **federal funds rate** by **25 basis points** to a target range of **3.75%–4%** — the first increase in more than three years — with Chair **Kevin Warsh** citing inflation still above the **2%** target and promising a "timelier return" to it. The median FOMC member expects **one further hike** this year.

**The exam angle.** Two things make this examinable. First, the **comparative mandate**: the Fed has a **dual mandate** — price stability *and* maximum employment — while the RBI operates a **flexible inflation-targeting** framework with a statutory **4% CPI target and a ±2% band**. Prelims has tested the Indian side of that repeatedly and the contrast is a natural statement-pair. Second, the **transmission channels**: a US rate rise narrows the interest-rate differential, pulls portfolio capital toward US assets, pressures the rupee, raises the rupee cost of imported crude and fertiliser, and lifts the benchmark against which Indian external borrowing is priced — the **impossible trinity** in operation. Running underneath both is the live theme of **central bank independence**, since the hike came against explicit and public political pressure for cuts.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **The decision:** **+25 bp**, to **3.75%–4.00%**; **unanimous, 12–0**; the **first hike since 2023**. Chair: **Kevin Warsh**; his predecessor was **Jerome Powell**.
- **Basis point:** one hundredth of a percentage point — **25 bp = 0.25%**.
- **Federal funds rate:** the rate at which US banks lend to one another **overnight** — the American functional analogue of India's **repo rate** (the rate at which the RBI lends to banks against government securities).
- **The Fed's dual mandate:** **price stability** (numerical target **2%**) and **maximum employment** (no numerical target). **FOMC** — the **12-member** rate-setting body; it publishes a **Summary of Economic Projections (SEP)** four times a year, including the "dot plot" of members' expected policy rates.
- **India's contrast:** the **Monetary Policy Committee**, created by the **Finance Act, 2016** amending the **RBI Act, 1934** — **six members** (three RBI, three nominated by the Centre), Governor has the **casting vote**, quorum four, meets at least **four times a year**. Target: **4% CPI inflation, band ±2%**, notified for a five-year term; failure to meet it for **three consecutive quarters** triggers a report to the Government.
- **Hawkish vs dovish:** *hawkish* prioritises holding inflation down (higher rates); *dovish* prioritises growth and employment (lower rates). The interest in this decision is that it can be read either way — a first hike in three years, but only **one more** pencilled in when markets expected several.
- **The channels into India (learn these as a list):** (i) **capital flows** — a narrower interest-rate differential pulls FPI money out of emerging markets; (ii) **exchange rate** — outflows plus a stronger dollar weaken the rupee, raising the rupee cost of **crude and fertiliser imports**, an imported-inflation channel; (iii) **external borrowing costs** — higher US Treasury yields reprice **ECBs** and sovereign/corporate issuance abroad; (iv) **policy constraint** — the MPC sets rates for domestic conditions but cannot fully ignore the external account.
- **The theoretical frame:** the **impossible trinity (Mundell–Fleming trilemma)** — a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and independent monetary policy: any two, never all three. India's managed float plus partial capital-account convertibility is the practical compromise, with **forex reserves** as the shock absorber.
- **The governance theme:** open political pressure on a central bank, and the institutional design questions that follow — fixed terms, removal procedures, statutory targets — which apply equally to any inflation-targeting central bank, India's included.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Economy (monetary policy, external sector, capital flows).

**Static anchor:** **Mrunal Economy — the Monetary Policy pillar** (BoP and external sector section alongside). *Nudge:* on the MPC page, add a two-column comparison you can reproduce in thirty seconds — **Fed: dual mandate, 2% target, FOMC-12, SEP/dot plot** against **RBI: flexible inflation targeting, 4% ±2%, MPC-6, casting vote with Governor** — and on the external-sector page write the four transmission channels as a numbered list with the **impossible trinity** as the closing line.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS2 — International Relations, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"Secondary sanctions convert a bilateral dispute into a test of third-country autonomy."** In the light of recent United States legislation authorising punitive tariffs on the largest purchasers of Russian energy, **examine** the constraints this places on India's pursuit of strategic autonomy, and **discuss** the options available to India in managing its energy security.

**Model-answer skeleton (four points):**

1. **Frame the instrument, not the politics.** Define secondary sanctions — measures penalising *third-country* persons for lawful trade with a sanctioned state — and distinguish *authority* from *automaticity*: the 2026 Act creates a presidential power (up to 100% tariffs on the five largest buyers of Russian oil and gas) with a retained **waiver**, layered on an existing **50%** tariff of which **25 points** already relate to Russian energy. Note the precedent of **CAATSA (2017)** and the S-400 waiver debate.
2. **State the constraint honestly.** Energy is the binding one: India imports the overwhelming majority of its crude, so sourcing is a *price and volume* decision before it is a *political* one; the MEA's own formulation — energy security for **1.4 billion** people, sourcing by "**evolving market dynamics**" — is a defence of policy space, not of alignment. Add the second-order constraints: dollar-clearing and shipping insurance, a widening trade exposure to a single market, and the signalling cost to the India–Russia relationship of any visible retreat.
3. **Set out the options, with trade-offs.** (i) **Diversification** of the crude basket — West Asia, West Africa, the US itself — at a landed-cost penalty; (ii) **negotiation** through the trade track, seeking waiver or carve-out, which trades autonomy rhetoric for tariff relief; (iii) **de-risking the settlement layer** — rupee-denominated trade, non-dollar clearing, indigenous insurance and tonnage — slow, partial, but structural; (iv) **demand-side insulation** — strategic petroleum reserves, biofuel and ethanol blending, electrification of transport, and the renewable build-out, which is the only option that shrinks the exposure itself rather than relocating it.
4. **Conclude on the doctrine.** Strategic autonomy in 2026 is not non-alignment revived; it is the capacity to absorb cost without changing position. That capacity is built in the **energy mix and the payments architecture**, not in declaratory diplomacy — which is why the durable Indian answer to secondary sanctions is a smaller import bill, not a better argument.

| **2** | **(c)** | Statements 2 and 3 are correct — the Bill creates a **"designated authority"** over foreign-contribution assets on cancellation, surrender or lapse of registration, and was referred to a **31-member Joint Committee** (21 LS + 10 RS) on 12 August 2026. Statement 1 is wrong: the statute in force is the **FCRA, 2010**, which repealed and replaced the 1976 Act. |
| **3** | **(a)** | Statements 1 and 2 are correct — ESZs are notified by MoEFCC under the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, and the **Zonal Master Plan** is the instrument meant to operationalise them. Statement 3 is wrong: an ESZ **regulates rather than prohibits**; notifications classify activities as prohibited, **regulated** or **permitted**, and agriculture, horticulture and local livelihoods generally continue. |
| **4** | **(d)** | *Forests for food: Nourishing people, sustaining the planet* was released on **15 September 2026** by the **FAO** with the **Landscape Alliance**, and reports that **more than 5 billion** people depend to some extent on forests and trees for livelihoods. (c) describes the **India State of Forest Report**; (a) and (b) are fabricated attributions. |

| **6** | **(c)** | All three are correct. A Joint Committee on a Bill is set up by a **motion in one House concurred in by the other**; **DRSCs are standing (permanent, periodically reconstituted)** while a Joint Committee on a Bill is **ad hoc** and dissolves on submitting its report; and committee recommendations are **advisory**, not binding. |
| **7** | **(b)** | Statements 1 and 2 are correct — **Conservation Reserves** and **Community Reserves** were added as categories by the **Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2002**, the former over **Government-owned** land (typically adjacent to, or linking, existing protected areas) and the latter over **private or community** land voluntarily offered for conservation.
