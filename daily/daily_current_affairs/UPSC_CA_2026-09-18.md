## Part A — Briefing

### 1. SEMICON India 2026 opens at Yashobhoomi — and Semicon 2.0 moves the mission from packaging to fabrication — Science & Technology

**Headline.** The Prime Minister inaugurated the **fifth edition of SEMICON India** at **Yashobhoomi, Dwarka, New Delhi on 17 September 2026** (running 17–19 September), and used the platform to set out **Semicon 2.0** — the second phase of the India Semiconductor Mission, with an outlay of **₹1,27,500 crore (~US$13.5 billion)**, approved by the Union Cabinet in **July 2026**. Over **600 companies** and participants from **52 countries** are present, with country pavilions from Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, the Netherlands, Singapore and Sweden.

**Exam angle.** The examinable shift is *structural*, not financial. **Semicon 1.0 (₹76,000 crore, 2021)** was a capital-subsidy scheme aimed mainly at getting units on the ground — and what actually came up was overwhelmingly **ATMP/OSAT** (assembly, testing, marking, packaging), the low-value end of the chain. **Semicon 2.0's six pillars** — chip **design**, **machines and materials**, new **fabs**, strengthening **ATMP/OSAT**, **R&D**, and **talent** — read as an admission that a packaging-led entry does not by itself buy technological sovereignty; the binding constraints are **equipment, materials, EDA tools and engineers**, all of which sit upstream. Second hook: semiconductors are the clearest live case of **industrial policy in a globally concentrated, export-control-shadowed supply chain** — useful for any question on strategic autonomy, friend-shoring, or China+1. Third: the **fiscal-federal** angle — the approved units are clustered in **Gujarat, Assam, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Odisha and Andhra Pradesh**, with States topping up Centre incentives, which is a good example of competitive federalism in industrial location.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **SEMICON India 2026** — 5th edition, **Yashobhoomi (India International Convention & Expo Centre), Dwarka**, **17–19 September 2026**; ~600 companies, 52 countries.
- **Semicon 2.0**: outlay **₹1,27,500 crore (~US$13.5 bn)**, Cabinet approval **July 2026**; **six pillars** — chip design; machines & materials; fabs; ATMP/OSAT; R&D; talent.
- **Semicon 1.0 / India Semiconductor Mission (ISM)**: launched **2021** with **₹76,000 crore**; ISM sits under the **Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY)** as an independent business division within **Digital India Corporation**.
- **Approved portfolio:** **12 units**, cumulative investment **over ₹1.64 lakh crore** — **1 semiconductor fab**, **2 compound-semiconductor fabs**, **9 packaging/ATMP units**; **three are operational**.
- **Talent:** EDA (Electronic Design Automation) tool access extended to **400+ academic institutions** and **100+ start-ups** under the Chips-to-Startup/DLI track; stated target of **~1 lakh semiconductor engineers**.
- **Vocabulary UPSC can test:** **ATMP** (Assembly, Testing, Marking, Packaging) vs **OSAT** (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test) vs **fab** (front-end wafer fabrication); **compound semiconductors** (GaN, SiC) used in power and RF devices, distinct from silicon CMOS logic.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Science & Technology (indigenisation, achievements in IT & computers) and GS3 — Economy (industrial policy, manufacturing, PLI-type incentives).

**Static anchor.** **Mrunal Pillar 4B — Infrastructure / Industry & Manufacturing**, under "PLI and sectoral schemes". *Update:* add a two-line box distinguishing **ISM 1.0 vs Semicon 2.0** (outlay, pillars, what changed), and cross-link it to the **critical minerals** note — gallium, germanium and rare earths are the input-side vulnerability that makes the "machines and materials" pillar necessary, and the two topics are increasingly asked together.

### 2. India rejects the new Pakistan–China "Boundary Joint Commission" — and the 1963 cession returns to the news — International Relations

**Headline.** After the **first meeting of a newly constituted Pakistan–China Boundary Joint Commission** was held in **Islamabad**, the **Ministry of External Affairs on 16 September 2026** rejected the body outright, stating that there is **no legally recognised boundary between Pakistan and China** in the area concerned because the territory involved is Indian territory under **illegal Pakistani occupation**. The MEA said India "resolutely opposes and rejects any attempts to alter the status of these occupied territories or legitimise illegal occupation".

**Exam angle.** This is a **Places-in-News plus treaty-law** item, and it is unusually clean. The legal spine is the **Sino-Pakistan Boundary Agreement of 2 March 1963**, under which Pakistan ceded about **5,180 sq km of the Shaksgam Valley (the Trans-Karakoram Tract)** to China. India's objection rests on two propositions the examiner can test: (a) *nemo dat quod non habet* — Pakistan could not transfer title it did not hold; and (b) the agreement's **own Article 6**, which provides that after the Kashmir dispute is settled, the **sovereign authority concerned** will re-open negotiations with China to sign a boundary treaty replacing the 1963 agreement — i.e. the agreement is **provisional by its own terms**. A permanent "boundary commission" is therefore inconsistent with the instrument it claims to implement. Second hook: **geography**. The Shaksgam Valley lies **north of the Siachen Glacier**, across the **Karakoram** range, adjoining **Aksai Chin** and abutting the **Xinjiang** region — the whole northern arc of the Kashmir question in one map. Third: this is the India–China–Pakistan **triangular dynamic**, and pairs naturally with CPEC (which India opposes on the same sovereignty ground, since it runs through Gilgit-Baltistan).

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Sino-Pakistan Boundary Agreement, 1963** (signed **2 March 1963**): Pakistan ceded **~5,180 sq km** of the **Shaksgam Valley / Trans-Karakoram Tract** to China. India has never recognised it.
- **Article 6** — the settlement is **provisional**; after the Kashmir dispute is resolved, the sovereign authority concerned reopens negotiations with China for a replacement boundary treaty.
- **Shaksgam Valley:** north of the **Siachen Glacier**, beyond the **Karakoram** watershed; drained by the **Shaksgam river**, a tributary of the **Yarkand**; administered by China as part of **Xinjiang (Kashgar/Taxkorgan area)**.
- **India's standard formulation:** the **entire Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh** are an integral part of India; **PoK/PoJK** and the ceded tract are under illegal occupation.
- **Related:** the **Karakoram Pass** and **Karakoram Highway** (China–Pakistan, via the **Khunjerab Pass**, 4,693 m) — the Khunjerab is the operative China–Pakistan road crossing, *not* the Karakoram Pass, a distinction UPSC has exploited before.
- **Adjacent flashpoint:** **Aksai Chin**, held by China since 1962, is a separate dispute from the 1963 cession — do not conflate the two.

**Notebook.** GS2 — International Relations (India and its neighbourhood; bilateral agreements affecting India's interests) and GS1 — Geography (India's physical features, frontier regions).

**Static anchor.** **Laxmikanth is not the home here** — this belongs in the **NCERT Class 11 *India: Physical Environment*, Ch. 2 (Structure and Physiography, Karakoram/Trans-Himalaya)** note, plus your IR file on **India–China border management**. *Update:* draw or paste one sketch map carrying **Siachen, Shaksgam/Trans-Karakoram Tract, Aksai Chin, Gilgit-Baltistan, Khunjerab Pass, Karakoram Pass** together and label which is disputed with whom — a single diagram that answers half a dozen possible Prelims questions.

### 3. RBI refuses Tata Sons' exit from NBFC registration — and the Upper Layer listing mandate becomes real — Economy

**Headline.** The **RBI on 11 September 2026 rejected Tata Sons' application to voluntarily surrender its registration as a Core Investment Company**, directing the holding company to comply with the obligations attaching to an **NBFC-Upper Layer**, which include **mandatory listing on stock exchanges**. Tata Sons was classified **NBFC-UL on 30 September 2022**, which set a three-year listing deadline. On **17 September 2026** the Tata Sons board separately **reappointed N. Chandrasekaran as Executive Chairman for five years** (his current term runs to **20 February 2027**) and approved moving ahead on the listing — a decision **Tata Trusts**, the majority shareholder, has publicly disputed.

**Exam angle.** Ignore the boardroom drama; the examinable content is the **Scale-Based Regulation (SBR) framework for NBFCs**, in force since **1 October 2022**, which UPSC has not yet asked directly and which is squarely due. SBR replaced the old activity-based classification with a **four-layer pyramid** keyed to **size, activity and perceived riskiness** — a textbook illustration of **proportionate, risk-based regulation**. The Tata Sons case also gives you the concept of a **Core Investment Company (CIC)**: an NBFC that holds **not less than 90% of its net assets** in group investments and does not trade in them — i.e. a pure holding vehicle, regulated because group-level leverage is a **systemic-contagion** channel. Third hook, and the best Mains line: **why force a private holding company to list?** The regulator's answer is **market discipline** — continuous disclosure, price signals and public scrutiny as a supplement to supervision at entities too large to be left opaque. The counter-argument (dilution of promoter control, forced valuation of an unlisted strategic holding) is the other half of a balanced answer.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Scale-Based Regulation (SBR)** for NBFCs — announced **October 2021**, effective **1 October 2022**; four layers: **Base (NBFC-BL)**, **Middle (NBFC-ML)**, **Upper (NBFC-UL)**, **Top (NBFC-TL)**; criteria — **size, activity, perceived riskiness**.
- **Base Layer:** non-deposit-taking NBFCs with assets **below ₹1,000 crore**. **Middle Layer:** **all deposit-taking NBFCs** irrespective of size, plus non-deposit-taking NBFCs with assets **₹1,000 crore and above**.
- **Upper Layer:** NBFCs specifically identified by RBI on a **parametric scoring** of quantitative and qualitative factors; subject to enhanced regulation for **at least five years**, including **mandatory listing within three years** of identification. **Top Layer** is meant to remain **empty** unless RBI sees substantial systemic risk in a UL entity.
- **RBI publishes the NBFC-UL list annually** — **17 NBFCs** in the list for the latest cycle (up from 15 in an earlier list).
- **Core Investment Company (CIC):** an NBFC with asset size of **₹100 crore or more** that holds **≥90% of its net assets** in equity/preference shares, bonds, debentures, debt or loans of **group companies**, of which **≥60% of net assets** must be in **equity shares** of group companies; must **not trade** in those investments except for **block sale for dilution or disinvestment** (governed by the **Core Investment Companies (Reserve Bank) Directions, 2016**).
- **Tata Sons timeline:** classified **NBFC-UL 30 September 2022** → three-year listing horizon → application to surrender CIC registration → **rejected 11 September 2026** → board moves on listing, **17 September 2026**.
- **Legal basis:** RBI's powers over NBFCs flow from **Chapter III-B of the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Economy (Indian financial system; banking and non-banking intermediation; regulatory bodies).

**Static anchor.** **Mrunal Pillar 1B/1C — Banking and Financial Intermediaries (NBFCs, SHGs, MFIs)**. *Update:* replace any pre-2022 "NBFC-D / NBFC-ND-SI" classification note with the **four-layer SBR pyramid**, and add **CIC** as a labelled sub-type; cross-link to the **IL&FS (2018) crisis** note — SBR is the direct policy consequence of that episode, and a question on either is really a question on both.

### 4. Global Gender Gap Report 2026: India 131st of 145, and the sub-index split is the whole story — Reports & Indices

**Headline.** The **World Economic Forum released the 20th edition of its Global Gender Gap Report on 16 September 2026**. India **retained 131st position out of 145 economies**, with **64.5% of its overall gender gap closed** — below the global average. Globally, **69.2%** of the gap is closed, and at the 20-year rate of progress full parity is **120 years away** (an improvement on the 123 years projected in 2025). **Iceland ranks first for the 17th consecutive year**, the only economy above 90% parity (**93%**).

**Exam angle.** UPSC has asked *who publishes this index* (2017) and will not ask it twice — the value now is in the **methodology**, which is genuinely counter-intuitive and therefore very testable. The index measures **gaps, not levels**: a poor country where men and women are equally badly off scores better than a rich one with a wide gap. It benchmarks **four sub-indices** — **Economic Participation and Opportunity, Educational Attainment, Health and Survival, Political Empowerment** — and India's profile is the classic "**scissors**" shape: near-parity where outcomes are measured by attainment (**Educational Attainment 96.6% closed**, **Health and Survival 95.6% closed**), collapse where outcomes are measured by power and money (**Economic Participation 143rd, 41.2% closed**). Note carefully the distinction the examiner loves: **Political Empowerment is India's best *rank* (67th) but its worst *score* (24.5% closed)** — because the global distribution on political empowerment is itself terrible, so a bad absolute score still buys a good relative position. Third hook: within economic participation, **labour-force participation parity 44.1%** and **estimated earned-income parity 35.1%** are the two numbers that carry a Mains answer on the **feminisation of unpaid work** and the **U-shaped female labour-force participation curve**.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Global Gender Gap Report 2026** — **World Economic Forum (WEF)**, released **16 September 2026**; **20th edition** (first published **2006**); covers **145 economies**, with a constant longitudinal sample of **97 economies** present in every edition.
- **India: rank 131/145**, score **64.5%** (rank unchanged from 2025).
- **India's sub-indices:** Educational Attainment **96.6%** closed; Health and Survival **95.6%** closed; Political Empowerment **24.5%** closed, **rank 67**; Economic Participation and Opportunity **41.2%** closed, **rank 143**.
- Within economic participation: **labour-force participation parity 44.1%**; **estimated earned income parity 35.1%**.
- **Global:** **69.2%** of the gap closed; **120 years** to parity at current pace; **Iceland 1st** for the **17th** straight year at **93%**.
- **Method:** measures **gaps between women and men**, **not levels of development**; scores are on a **0 (imparity) to 1 (parity)** scale; values are **capped at parity**, so no country is rewarded for women out-performing men.
- **Do not confuse with:** **Gender Inequality Index (GII)** and **Gender Development Index (GDI)** — both **UNDP**, in the *Human Development Report*; and the **Global Gender Gap Index — WEF**.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Society (role of women and women's organisations; social empowerment) and GS2 — Governance (welfare schemes, indices); GS3 — Economy for the labour-force participation strand.

**Static anchor.** Your **Reports & Indices tracker**, plus the **Society — Women** note. *Update:* add a single comparison row — **Global Gender Gap Index (WEF)** vs **GII (UNDP)** vs **GDI (UNDP)** vs **Women, Business and the Law (World Bank)** — with publisher, dimensions and India's latest rank, because Prelims questions on indices are almost always **publisher-matching** questions. Cross-link to the **PLFS female LFPR** figure so the index number and the domestic survey number sit on the same page.

### 5. Supreme Court asks the Centre to defer the mandatory third language for Class 6 to 2027 — Society

**Headline.** On **17 September 2026**, a three-judge Bench headed by **Chief Justice of India Surya Kant**, hearing petitions challenging **CBSE circulars** that advanced the mandatory **three-language requirement for Class 6 to the 2026-27 session**, urged the Centre and the Board to **defer implementation by a year to the 2027 academic year**, observing that enforcing the requirement **mid-term** imposes avoidable logistical strain on young children and their families. The Court did not strike the policy down; it pressed for a **transitional** approach, including making the requirement optional for the current year.

**Exam angle.** Three clean hooks. First, the **three-language formula** itself — it originates in the **National Education Policy of 1968** (drawing on the **Kothari Commission, 1964-66**), is restated in **NEP 1986** and in **NEP 2020**, and under NEP 2020 carries the explicit rider that **no language will be imposed on any State** and that at least two of the three should be **native to India**. Second, the **constitutional-federal** layer: **education is in the Concurrent List** (moved there by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**, Entry 25), so a national policy binds only through adoption and through central boards like CBSE — which is precisely why CBSE circulars, not a statute, are what is being litigated. Third, and this is the judicially interesting part: the Court is not adjudicating the **validity** of the policy but the **reasonableness of its transition** — a **legitimate-expectation / proportionality-in-implementation** argument rather than a substantive challenge. That distinction is worth a sentence in any Mains answer on judicial review of policy.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Date and forum:** **17 September 2026**; Supreme Court, three-judge Bench headed by **CJI Surya Kant**; challenge to **CBSE** circulars advancing the Class 6 three-language mandate to **2026-27**; Court suggested deferral to **2027**.
- **Three-language formula:** first formalised in the **National Policy on Education, 1968**, following the **Kothari Commission (1964-66)**; reaffirmed in **NEP 1986** and **NEP 2020**.
- **NEP 2020 language provisions:** mother tongue/local language as medium of instruction **at least till Grade 5, preferably till Grade 8**; **three-language formula continues**, with **no language imposed on any State**, and **at least two of the three native to India**.
- **Constitutional frame:** **Education — Concurrent List, Entry 25**, shifted from the State List by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**. **Article 350A** — facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for linguistic minorities. **Article 351** — duty of the Union to promote the spread of Hindi. **Article 343** — Hindi in Devanagari as the official language of the Union; **Official Languages Act, 1963** for continued use of English.
- **Eighth Schedule** currently lists **22 languages** (14 at commencement; **Sindhi** added by the **21st Amendment, 1967**; **Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali** by the **71st Amendment, 1992**; **Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali** by the **92nd Amendment, 2003**).
- **CBSE** is a **board of the Ministry of Education**, not a statutory body created by an Act of Parliament — relevant to why the challenge is by writ against circulars.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Society (social empowerment, education, linguistic diversity) and GS2 — Governance (issues relating to development and management of the education sector; Centre-State relations).

**Static anchor.** Your **NEP 2020 note** plus **Laxmikanth, Chapter on Official Language (Part XVII, Articles 343-351)**. *Update:* add a three-line history of the **three-language formula (1968 → 1986 → 2020)** to the NEP note, and cross-link it to the **Official Language chapter** and to the **Concurrent List / 42nd Amendment** entry — the language question is simultaneously a policy, a federalism and a fundamental-rights topic, and UPSC has approached it from all three.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS3 — Science & Technology / Economy, 250 words, 15 marks)

**"India's semiconductor strategy has so far bought capacity at the low-value end of the chain; the harder task is to move upstream."** In this light, examine the shift in approach represented by the second phase of the India Semiconductor Mission, and discuss the constraints that will determine whether it succeeds.

**Model-answer skeleton.**

1. **Set the frame.** Semiconductors as a strategic, not merely commercial, sector — concentration of fabrication in a handful of geographies, export controls as an instrument of statecraft, and India's import dependence. State the pivot in one line: Phase 1 (₹76,000 crore, 2021) was capital-subsidy-led and delivered mainly ATMP/OSAT; Phase 2 (₹1,27,500 crore) adds design, equipment and materials, R&D and talent as distinct pillars.
2. **What the shift gets right.** Value capture sits upstream (design and fabs), not in packaging; equipment and materials are the true chokepoints; EDA access for institutions and start-ups and a large engineer-training target attack the human-capital constraint; a 12-project, ₹1.64 lakh crore base with three units operational gives the programme an actual industrial anchor rather than a paper target.
3. **The constraints.** (i) **Capital and cycle risk** — fabs are multi-billion-dollar, technology-generation-limited assets in a cyclical industry; (ii) **inputs** — ultra-pure water, uninterrupted power, high-purity chemicals and gases, and critical minerals (gallium, germanium, rare earths) where supply is concentrated and export-controlled; (iii) **talent depth** — India supplies design engineers to the world but has thin process-engineering and fab-operations experience; (iv) **market pull** — without domestic demand anchors in automotive, telecom and defence electronics, subsidised capacity risks idling; (v) **fiscal-federal coordination** — State top-ups create location competition but also incentive duplication.
4. **Conclude with the judgement.** Success should be measured not by announced investment but by **process nodes actually running, design IP owned domestically, and equipment/materials localisation** — and by whether the R&D and talent pillars are funded through the cycle rather than only in the announcement year.

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**1. (a)** — Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Semicon 2.0's outlay of **₹1,27,500 crore** exceeds the **₹76,000 crore** of the 2021 phase, and ISM operates as an independent business division within **Digital India Corporation** under **MeitY**. Statement 3 is wrong: of the 12 approved units, **nine are packaging/ATMP units** against one silicon fab and two compound-semiconductor fabs — packaging, not fabrication, dominates.

**2. (b)** — The Shaksgam Valley (Trans-Karakoram Tract) lies **north of the Siachen Glacier beyond the Karakoram**, and was ceded by Pakistan under the **1963 Sino-Pakistan Boundary Agreement**, whose **Article 6** makes the arrangement provisional pending settlement of the Kashmir dispute. (a) misplaces it (Zoji La is in the Kashmir–Ladakh transition, far to the south-west); (c) describes **Aksai Chin**, a separate matter; (d) describes the CPEC alignment through **Gilgit-Baltistan**, which is distinct territory.

**3. (d)** — All three are correct. The RBI rejected the surrender application on **11 September 2026**; **NBFC-UL** entities must list within **three years** of identification under SBR; and a **CIC** must hold **≥90% of net assets** in group-company investments (with **≥60% in equity**) and must not trade in them except for block sale or dilution.

**4. (b)** — Statements 2 and 3 are correct. India ranked **67th on Political Empowerment** against **143rd on Economic Participation and Opportunity**; and **96.6%** of the Educational Attainment gap was closed against only **24.5%** of the Political Empowerment gap — the sub-index where India ranks best is the one where it scores worst, because the global distribution on political empowerment is itself very unequal. Statement 1 is wrong: the Global Gender Gap Report is published by the **World Economic Forum**; the UNDP publishes the **Gender Inequality Index** and **Gender Development Index** in the Human Development Report.

**5. (b)** — The petitions challenged **CBSE circulars** that advanced the mandatory third language for Class 6 to the 2026-27 session; the Court urged deferral to 2027 and did not rule on the validity of NEP 2020 itself.

**6. (a)** — Statements 1 and 2 are correct. **All deposit-taking NBFCs** sit in the Middle Layer or above regardless of asset size, and the **Top Layer** is designed to remain empty unless RBI sees a substantial rise in systemic risk from a UL entity. Statement 3 is wrong: SBR uses **size, activity and perceived riskiness** — not size alone.

**7. (c)** — All three pairs are correctly matched. **Article 343** — Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union; **Article 350A** (inserted by the **7th Amendment, 1956**) — facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for children of linguistic minority groups; **Article 351** — directive to the Union to promote the spread and development of Hindi.
