### 1. The UNGA General Debate opens today — and India's two asks at the UN are now distinct — International Relations

**Headline.** The General Debate of the 81st UN General Assembly session opens at UN Headquarters today (22 September), running to 28 September; EAM S. Jaishankar will lead the Indian delegation and deliver India's national statement on 26 September.

**Exam angle.** This is the cleanest annual hook for the UN's institutional architecture, and this year India is running two *separate* tracks that aspirants routinely conflate. Track one is **structural reform** — expanding the Security Council in both permanent and non-permanent categories, which requires a Charter amendment under Articles 108–109 and is stuck in the Intergovernmental Negotiations (IGN), rolled over yet again into this 81st session. Track two is **an ordinary election** — India's candidature for a non-permanent seat for the 2028–29 term, decided by a simple vote in June 2027. The first needs treaty change; the second needs 129 ballots. A question that tests whether you know the difference is very much UPSC's style.

**Key facts to memorise.**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Session | 81st session of the UNGA, opened 8 September 2026 |
| President of the General Assembly | **Dr Khalilur Rahman (Bangladesh)** — elected 2 June 2026 by secret ballot, 99 votes to 91, from the Asia-Pacific Group |
| Session theme | "Restoring trust, managing transformation: A United Nations that delivers for all" |
| General Debate | 22–28 September 2026; India's statement on **26 September** |
| UNSC reform | IGN process **rolled over** to the 81st session; India's demand is **text-based negotiations with defined timelines and milestones**, and expansion in *both* categories |
| India's UNSC bid | Non-permanent seat, **2028–29 term**; campaign theme **"#India4UNSC: Peace, Planet, Progress"**, vision acronym **SHANTI** |
| The contest | Election at the UNGA in **June 2027**; **India vs Tajikistan** for the single **Asia-Pacific Group** seat |
| Winning threshold | **Two-thirds of members present and voting** (~129 of 193) |
| India's record | **8 previous terms** on the Council; Tajikistan has never served |
| Complication | Tajikistan has carried **OIC** backing since 2023 (56 GA votes) |

**Notebook:** GS2 — International Relations (Important international institutions).

**Static anchor:** Your GS2 IR file on **the United Nations system** — principal organs, the UNGA's one-state-one-vote rule, and the non-permanent-member election rules (Article 23: 10 non-permanent members, two-year terms, no immediate re-election, elected by two-thirds of those present and voting, with the regional distribution fixed by GA Resolution 1991A of 1963). *Update nudge:* add a two-column box — "Charter amendment route (Art. 108/109, needs 2/3 GA + ratification by 2/3 members **including all five permanent members**)" versus "ordinary election route" — this single distinction defuses most UNSC-reform MCQs.

### 2. GOBARdhan becomes operational — a guaranteed buyer and a ten-year price for compressed biogas — Economy

**Headline.** The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas has issued the operational guidelines (dated 15 September, rolled out this week) for the ₹23,731-crore GOBARdhan scheme, giving compressed biogas producers assured offtake of up to 100% of output and a government-administered price locked in for at least a decade.

**Exam angle.** Read this as a **market-design** story, not a subsidy story — that framing is what earns Mains marks. CBG's problem was never the technology; it was that a plant had no certain buyer and no certain price, which made it unbankable. The scheme fixes both ends: a **mandated purchase obligation** on City Gas Distribution entities manufactures the demand, and an **administered price fixed to 2036** removes price risk. It is the same instrument logic as a power-sector PPA or an MSP, applied to waste-to-energy. It also sits at the junction of three syllabus areas at once — energy security and import substitution (GS3 Economy), crop-residue and waste management (GS3 Environment), and rural incomes (GS3 Agriculture).

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **GOBARdhan** = **G**alvanizing **O**rganic **B**io-**A**gro **R**esources **Dhan**; now the National Unified Scheme for Compressed Biogas, administered by the **Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas**.
- Outlay **₹23,731 crore**; period **FY 2026-27 to FY 2035-36**; Union Cabinet approval **6 August 2026**.
- **Six components:** (i) CBG Offtake Assurance Mechanism, (ii) CBG Pricing Framework, (iii) Capital Assistance, (iv) Development of Pipeline Infrastructure, (v) Credit Guarantee Support, (vi) CBG Ecosystem Challenge Fund.
- **Administered CBG price: ₹2,110 per MMBTU** — roughly **₹98/kg** at 95% methane content — fixed until **31 March 2036** unless revised.
- **CBG Obligation trajectory** on CGD entities in the **CNG (Transport)** and **PNG (Domestic)** segments: **3% in FY 2026-27 → 4% in FY 2027-28 → 5% from FY 2028-29**.
- **Capital assistance up to ₹2 crore per TPD** (tonne per day) for eligible greenfield projects; brownfield capacity expansion also eligible.
- Offtake assurance of **up to 100%** of a plant's output; price support backed for a **minimum 10-year horizon**.
- Predecessor to remember: **SATAT** (Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation), launched **1 October 2018** by the oil marketing companies.

**Notebook:** GS3 — Economy (Infrastructure: Energy) with a cross-entry in GS3 Environment (waste management).

**Static anchor:** **Mrunal Pillar 5 — Infrastructure (Energy)**, and **Shankar IAS, "Waste Management" / renewable energy topic**. *Update nudge:* build one "assured-offtake instruments" comparison row — MSP (agriculture), PPA (power), VGF (infrastructure), CBG Obligation (bioenergy) — so you can deploy the same analytical frame across three GS3 papers; cross-link SATAT, the National Bioenergy Programme and the Ethanol Blended Petrol Programme.

### 3. India International Water Week opens with climate resilience as the organising theme — Environment

### 4. Tulu becomes a second additional official language in two Karnataka districts — Art & Culture

**Headline.** The Karnataka Cabinet, meeting at Mangaluru on 18 September, approved Tulu as a **second additional official language for administrative purposes in Dakshina Kannada and Udupi districts** — a sub-State-level language recognition, distinct from both Eighth Schedule inclusion and classical-language status.

**Exam angle.** This is an unusually clean vehicle for testing the **three separate ladders of language recognition** in India, which candidates mix up constantly: (i) **State official language**, decided by a State legislature under **Article 345**; (ii) **Eighth Schedule inclusion**, which requires a **constitutional amendment**; and (iii) **classical language status**, a purely **executive** decision by the Ministry of Culture on antiquity criteria. Tulu has now climbed only the first, and only at district level. The trap to internalise: **Pali and Prakrit are classical languages but are *not* in the Eighth Schedule** — so the two lists do not nest.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- Decision by the **Karnataka Cabinet, 18 September 2026**, at Mangaluru, chaired by **CM D. K. Shivakumar** (in office since 3 June 2026); the same meeting cleared projects worth **₹32,611 crore**.
- Applies to **Dakshina Kannada and Udupi** districts; **₹82 lakh** annual allocation for translation and staff training. Karnataka's official language remains **Kannada** (Karnataka Official Language Act, 1963).
- **Tulu** is a **South Dravidian** language of the Tulu Nadu region; **Census 2011: 18,46,427 speakers** (community estimates run far higher, as migrants are often enumerated as Kannada speakers).
- Historically written in the **Tigalari script**, which developed from **Grantha** around the 12th century; inscriptions survive around **Barkur and Kundapura**. Today Tulu is usually written in the **Kannada script**.
- **Not in the Eighth Schedule** — which has **22 languages**; the last four (**Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali**) were added by the **92nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003**. There are **no fixed criteria** for inclusion.
- **Classical languages now number 11**: Tamil (2004), Sanskrit (2005), Kannada and Telugu (2008), Malayalam (2013), Odia (2014), and **Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali** (added October 2024).
- Cultural associations to file alongside: **Yakshagana**, **Bhuta Kola** (spirit worship), **Kambala** (buffalo race), and the **Karnataka Tulu Sahitya Academy** (established 1994).

**Notebook:** GS1 — Art & Culture (Languages), with a GS2 Polity cross-entry.

**Static anchor:** **Laxmikanth — "Official Language" (Part XVII, Articles 343–351)** and the **Eighth Schedule**; **Nitin Singhania — "Languages" chapter**. *Update nudge:* draw a three-rung ladder on that page — Article 345 (State decides) → Eighth Schedule (constitutional amendment) → Classical status (executive, Ministry of Culture) — and pencil in Tulu, Kodava and Bhojpuri as the standing claimants.

### 5. Thirty lakh village-level workers: the Centre puts a number on India's community cadre — Society

**Headline.** A PIB backgrounder released on 20 September consolidates, for the first time across four ministries, the scale of India's frontline "community cadre" — **over 30 lakh trained village-level workers**, mostly women drawn from Self-Help Groups and paid through activity-linked incentives rather than salaries.

**Exam angle.** The interesting question is not the headcount but the **employment model**. India's last-mile welfare delivery rests on a workforce that is neither civil servant nor contract employee: incentive-paid community volunteers, overwhelmingly women. That produces genuine gains — local trust, low fiscal cost, female workforce participation, convergence across schemes — and a genuine structural problem: no minimum wage floor, no social security, and recurring agitations over honoraria. This is a ready-made GS2 "governance and service delivery" answer with both sides built in, and the numbers below are the sort of granular data that lifts an answer out of the generic pile.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS3 — Economy / Environment, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"India's compressed biogas policy has shifted from subsidising supply to guaranteeing demand. Examine how assured offtake and administered pricing address the bankability of waste-to-energy projects, and evaluate the risks this approach carries."**

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Frame the problem correctly.** CBG's constraint was not technology or feedstock availability but **bankability** — a plant faced both offtake risk (no certain buyer) and price risk (a buyer's-market negotiation against OMCs). SATAT (2018) offered a price but no enforceable obligation, so capacity built slowly. Establish this before describing the new scheme.

2. **How the instrument works.** A **purchase obligation** on CGD entities (3% → 4% → 5% across FY27–FY29 in CNG-Transport and PNG-Domestic) converts a policy aspiration into contractual demand; an **administered price of ₹2,110/MMBTU fixed to March 2036** converts a variable revenue stream into a predictable one; **capital assistance of up to ₹2 crore per TPD**, credit guarantee support and pipeline connectivity address the financing, collateral and evacuation legs respectively. Together these make the project appraisable by a lender — the same logic as a power-purchase agreement.

3. **Co-benefits worth naming.** Import substitution in natural gas; a monetised outlet for **crop residue** that competes with stubble burning; **fermented organic manure** as a by-product supporting soil health and reducing fertiliser subsidy pressure; rural feedstock incomes; and circular-economy alignment with the waste hierarchy.

4. **Risks, evaluated.** (i) **Cost pass-through** — the obligation is an implicit levy on CNG and piped-gas consumers, raising equity questions. (ii) **Administered-price rigidity** — a decade-long fixed price can drift far from marginal cost in either direction, creating windfalls or stranded assets. (iii) **Feedstock aggregation** remains the binding constraint; demand guarantees do nothing for dispersed, seasonal biomass supply chains. (iv) **Enforcement capacity** — an obligation is only as strong as the penalty regime, as the RPO experience in the power sector shows. *Conclude:* demand guarantees are necessary but not sufficient; the residual work is in feedstock logistics and credible enforcement.
