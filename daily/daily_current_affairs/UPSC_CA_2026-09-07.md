### 1. Anak Krakatau erupts again — eight airports shut, ash to 50,000 feet — Geography

**Headline:** Mount Anak Krakatau in Indonesia's Sunda Strait entered a strong eruptive phase from late 4 September, with a major explosion at 03:53 local time on 6 September 2026 sending an ash column to roughly 50,000 feet, closing eight airports in western Indonesia and disrupting about 1,558 flights.

**Exam angle.** This is the single best "Places in News" hook of the week and it doubles as a full physical-geography revision node — subduction volcanism, calderas, volcanic explosivity, and *volcanogenic* tsunamis (which are generated by flank collapse and pyroclastic flows, not by a fault rupture). Prelims has repeatedly tested Indian and Indian-Ocean volcanism (Barren Island, 2018); a live eruption on the Sunda Arc is exactly the kind of trigger UPSC converts into a statement-based question on location and mechanism. The aviation-disruption angle also connects to the ash-cloud hazard that shows up in disaster-management questions.

**Key facts / figures to memorise.**

- **Location:** Sunda Strait, between **Java and Sumatra**, Indonesia. Part of the **Sunda Arc**, where the **Indo-Australian plate subducts beneath the Sunda (Eurasian) plate** — the western limb of the Pacific "Ring of Fire".
- **Current event:** eruptive episode from late **4 September 2026**; major explosion **6 September 2026, 03:53 local**; ash plume ~**50,000 ft** on the Sumatra side; lava fountaining.
- **Alert:** **Level III** on Indonesia's **four-level** scale (i.e. the *second-highest*), declared by **PVMBG** (Center for Volcanology and Geological Hazard Mitigation). **3 km exclusion zone.** Authorities reported **no immediate tsunami threat**.
- **Impact:** **8 airports** temporarily closed on 6 September; **1,558 flights** delayed; **~1.7 lakh passengers** affected.
- **The 1883 parent eruption:** Krakatau ejected an estimated **12–20 km³** of material, destroyed roughly **two-thirds of the island**, generated a tsunami with run-ups near **40 m**, and killed about **36,000** people. It was the deadliest volcanogenic tsunami on record until 2018.
- **"Anak" = "child of":** the present cone **emerged from the sea in 1929** on the rim of the 1883 caldera and had grown to about **328 m** by 2018.
- **22 December 2018:** a **lateral (flank) collapse** during eruption pushed volcaniclastic material into the caldera and generated a tsunami with run-ups up to **13 m** on Java and Sumatra coasts; **at least 437 dead**. No seismic precursor — hence no warning. This is the textbook case for why tsunami warning systems keyed to earthquakes fail for volcanic sources.
- **Why it collapsed:** the cone sat on the margin of a caldera floor **200–300 m below sea level**, leaving the south-west flank **unbuttressed**.
- **India link for context:** India's only confirmed active volcano is **Barren Island** in the **Andaman Sea** (Andaman & Nicobar Islands), on the same broad Sunda–Andaman subduction system; **Narcondam** is classified dormant.

**STATIC ANCHOR:** *NCERT Class 11, Fundamentals of Physical Geography* — Ch. 3 (Interior of the Earth) and the volcanism sections; **G.C. Leong, Ch. 2 "The Earth's Crust"** (volcanoes and volcanic landforms).

### 2. Supreme Court closes the NSE co-location saga after a ₹1,491.21 crore settlement — Economy

**Headline:** A Supreme Court Bench of **Justices J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran** disposed of SEBI's appeals against the National Stock Exchange in the **co-location and dark-fibre** matters, taking on record the regulator's approval of a settlement of about **₹1,491.21 crore** — ending a dispute running since 2015 and clearing the runway for the NSE's long-delayed IPO.

**Exam angle.** Three separate syllabus threads meet here. First, **market microstructure** — what "co-location" and "algorithmic/high-frequency trading" actually are, and why *preferential latency* is a regulatory problem rather than a technical curiosity. Second, the **SEBI settlement (consent) mechanism**, a statutory route that lets a regulator monetise closure "without admission or denial of guilt" — a favourite for questions on regulatory design and on whether such settlements deter misconduct. Third, the **appellate architecture** of Indian securities regulation (SEBI → SAT → Supreme Court), which is standard Polity-meets-Economy material.

**Key facts / figures to memorise.**

- **The allegation:** between roughly 2012 and 2014, some brokers using NSE's **co-location facility** got preferential access to the exchange's **tick-by-tick (TBT) data feed** — the "first-connect-first-served" dissemination architecture meant early logins received price data microseconds ahead of others. The **dark fibre** limb concerned unauthorised leased-line connectivity giving further latency advantage.
- **Co-location:** brokers place their servers physically inside the exchange's data centre, cutting the distance signals travel. Legal in itself and offered globally — the violation lies in **unequal access**, not in co-location as such.
- **Settlement value:** **₹1,491.21 crore**. NSE's earlier offer was about **₹1,388 crore** (June 2025); SEBI agreed **in principle in January 2026**; NSE paid **₹714.74 crore** in July 2026 after in-principle approval.
- **Statutory basis of settlement:** **Section 15JB, SEBI Act, 1992**, inserted by the **Securities Laws (Amendment) Act, 2014**, read with the **SEBI (Settlement Proceedings) Regulations, 2018**. Settlement is *without admission or denial of guilt*; SEBI may refuse settlement in serious cases (e.g. those involving fraud affecting market integrity).
- **Appeal route:** SEBI order → **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)** under **Section 15T** → **Supreme Court** on a **question of law** under **Section 15Z**. SAT is a statutory tribunal with a presiding officer who is a sitting/retired SC judge or HC Chief Justice.
- **SEBI itself:** established 1988, **statutory from 1992** under the SEBI Act; headquarters **Mumbai**; chairman as of 2026 is **Tuhin Kanta Pandey**. NSE is a **Market Infrastructure Institution (MII)** — the same category regulated under the ITRI resilience framework.
- **Why it matters commercially:** regulatory overhang was the stated obstacle to NSE's IPO; NSE had also set aside over **₹4,000 crore** of co-location revenue in an escrow-type arrangement in earlier years.

### 3. Centre notifies Chief Justices for eight High Courts — and every High Court now has a regular Chief Justice — Polity & Governance

**Headline:** The **Ministry of Law and Justice** on **5 September 2026** notified the appointment of **Chief Justices to eight High Courts**, following two sets of Supreme Court Collegium recommendations made in August 2026 — with the effect that all High Courts now have full-time (rather than acting) Chief Justices.

**Exam angle.** Prelims tests the **articles** — who is consulted, who appoints, what happens when the office is vacant. Mains tests the **collegium debate** — opacity, delay, the government's "segregation and sitting on files" practice, and whether the post-NJAC settlement is stable. Note the structural point candidates routinely get wrong: for **High Court** appointments the collegium is the **CJI plus the two senior-most Supreme Court judges**, whereas for **Supreme Court** appointments it is the **CJI plus the four senior-most judges**.

**Key facts / figures to memorise.**

- **The eight appointments (5 September 2026):** Justice **V. Kameswar Rao** (Delhi HC) → CJ, **Patna**; Justice **Ravindra V. Ghuge** (Bombay HC) → CJ, **Calcutta**; Justice **Mahesh Chandra Tripathi** (Allahabad HC) → CJ, **Bombay**; Justice **Sanjay Kumar Agrawal** (Chhattisgarh HC) → CJ, **Rajasthan** (its **44th** regular Chief Justice); Justice **Ashwani Kumar Mishra** → CJ, **Punjab & Haryana**; Justice **Krushna Ram Mohapatra** (Orissa HC) → CJ, **Chhattisgarh**; Justice **Alpesh Y. Kogje** (Gujarat HC) → CJ, **Madhya Pradesh**; Justice **Pushpendra Singh Bhati** (Rajasthan HC) → CJ, **Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh**.
- **Article 217(1):** a High Court judge is appointed by the **President** by warrant under hand and seal, after consultation with the **CJI**, the **Governor of the State**, and — for a judge *other than* the Chief Justice — the **Chief Justice of that High Court**. Note the asymmetry: the HC Chief Justice is **not** consulted for his or her own successor.
- **Related articles:** **214** (HC for each State) · **216** (composition) · **217(2)** qualifications — citizen of India, plus **10 years** as judicial officer **or** 10 years as an advocate of a High Court · **217(1) proviso** retirement at **62** · **222** transfer of a judge by the President after consultation with the CJI · **223** appointment of an **acting** Chief Justice · **224** additional and acting judges · **224A** **ad hoc judges** (retired judges, with the President's previous consent) · **231** common High Court for two or more States.
- **The Judges Cases:** **First Judges Case — S.P. Gupta (1981)** (executive primacy; "consultation" ≠ concurrence) → **Second Judges Case — SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. UoI (1993)** (collegium created; CJI's opinion has primacy) → **Third Judges Case (1998)**, on a **Presidential Reference under Article 143** (collegium expanded and formalised).
- **NJAC:** the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** and the **NJAC Act, 2014** were struck down **4:1** in 2015 for violating **judicial independence** as part of the **basic structure**. Justice Chelameswar dissented.
- **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP):** the administrative document governing the process; a revised MoP has remained unfinalised since 2015 — the standing friction point between the executive and the collegium.
- **Recent context (do not confuse with today's item):** in **February 2026** the **Surya Kant-led** Supreme Court Collegium approved an **early-transfer policy for High Court Chief Justices** — the administrative backdrop to this round of postings.
- **Scale:** India has **25 High Courts**. Judicial vacancies in the High Courts have hovered around a quarter to a third of sanctioned strength through the decade — the standard data point for a Mains introduction.

**Destination notebook:** GS2 — Polity (Judiciary).

### 4. DRDO develops indigenous 500-watt pulsed laser diode stacks for missile proximity fuzes — Science & Technology

**Headline:** DRDO's **Solid State Physics Laboratory (SSPL)** has developed **500-watt pulsed laser diode stacks operating at 905 nanometres**, with an initial batch of **50 prototype stacks** delivered to the **Instruments Research and Development Establishment (IRDE), Dehradun** for trials — the critical emitter for **laser proximity fuzes** on systems such as **QRSAM** and the **NGARM**.

*Sourcing note: this is reported through defence-industry trade press rather than a PIB release; treat the 905 nm / 500 W / 50-unit figures as reported values and revise the underlying physics, which is what Prelims actually tests.*

**Exam angle.** UPSC's Sci-Tech questions rarely ask "which lab made what" — they ask **how the physics works** and **what the strategic point is**. Here the exam-relevant core is: (a) how a proximity fuze differs from impact and time fuzes; (b) why **905 nm near-infrared** is chosen (silicon-detector sensitivity, atmospheric window, low cost, eye-safety class trade-off versus the 1550 nm alternative); and (c) **import substitution in critical components** — the recurring Atmanirbharta theme where India makes the platform but historically imported the seeker, the fuze, and the emitter.

**Key facts / figures to memorise.**

- **What it is:** a **pulsed laser diode stack** — multiple laser diode bars stacked to multiply peak optical power. Rated **500 W peak**, wavelength **905 nm** (near-infrared).
- **Developer:** **SSPL, Delhi** — DRDO's laboratory for semiconductor materials and solid-state devices (part of the Micro Electronic Devices & Computational Systems cluster). **IRDE, Dehradun** is DRDO's electro-optics lab and is doing the evaluation.
- **Laser proximity fuze:** emits pulses, times the return from the target, and detonates the warhead at the **optimum standoff distance** rather than on contact — maximising fragment lethality against fast, small, or manoeuvring targets. Compare: **impact fuze** (contact), **time fuze** (preset delay), **radio/RF proximity fuze** (jammable, wider beam), **laser proximity fuze** (narrow beam, far harder to jam, better in clutter).
- **Why 905 nm:** it lies in an atmospheric transmission window, silicon photodiodes are highly responsive there, and GaAs diode lasers at that wavelength are mature and cheap. The trade-off is that 905 nm is **retinal-hazard region** radiation, whereas 1550 nm is far more eye-safe but needs costlier InGaAs detectors.
- **Host systems:** **QRSAM** — Quick Reaction Surface-to-Air Missile, DRDO's canisterised, all-weather, short-range air-defence system for armoured columns on the move, with an AESA radar and active RF seeker. **NGARM / Rudram-1** — New Generation Anti-Radiation Missile, India's first indigenous air-launched anti-radiation missile for **SEAD** (Suppression of Enemy Air Defences), which homes onto hostile radar emissions with dual INS-GPS plus passive homing.
- **Strategic point:** critical sub-components — emitters, seekers, focal-plane arrays — have been the chokepoint in India's missile programme even where the airframe and propulsion are indigenous. Read alongside the August 2026 decision to transfer **all conventional missile technologies** of DRDO to Indian industry.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Science & Technology (Defence Technology) / Internal Security.

**STATIC ANCHOR:** Sci-Tech notes on **DRDO's missile programme (IGMDP and successors)**; **Internal Security (Ashok Kumar), chapter on defence technology and indigenisation**.
*Update / cross-link nudge:* Add a four-row **fuze comparison table** (impact / time / RF proximity / laser proximity — with "jammable?" and "typical use" columns) to your missiles page, and tag QRSAM and Rudram-1 with their roles so you stop confusing NGARM with the air-to-air family. ### 5. CBDT's Time Series Data: direct taxes at ₹22.26 lakh crore, buoyancy above 1 for a third year — Reports & Indices

**Headline:** The **Central Board of Direct Taxes** has released its **Time Series Data for FY 2000-01 to FY 2024-25**, showing net direct tax collections of a record **₹22.26 lakh crore** in FY 2024-25, a direct-tax-to-GDP ratio of **6.73%**, a taxpayer base above **12.13 crore**, and **tax buoyancy of 1.39** — above 1 for the third consecutive financial year.

**Exam angle.** Two things are being tested here and they are different. The **concept**: tax buoyancy is a ratio, not a rate, and it is *not* the same as tax elasticity — buoyancy captures the combined effect of growth *and* discretionary policy changes, while elasticity holds the rate structure and base constant. The **structural story**: **non-corporate (largely personal) income tax has now overtaken corporate tax**, which reverses the long-standing composition of India's direct taxes and gives you a ready-made Mains argument about the incidence of the tax burden and the effect of the 2019 corporate-rate cut.

**Key facts / figures to memorise.**

- **Net direct tax collection FY 2024-25: ₹22.26 lakh crore**, up from **₹19.60 lakh crore** in FY 2023-24.
- **Composition:** corporate tax **₹9.86 lakh crore**; non-corporate tax **₹12.35 lakh crore**. Non-corporate now exceeds corporate.
- **Direct tax-to-GDP ratio: 6.73%.**
- **Cost of collection: 0.41%** — a historic low, and one of the lowest in the world (i.e. ₹41 spent to collect ₹10,000).
- **Taxpayer base (AY 2024-25): 12.13 crore** total, of which **11.61 crore** individuals.
- **Direct tax buoyancy: 1.39 in 2024-25** — above 1 for the **third consecutive year**.
- **The formula:** **Tax buoyancy = (% change in tax revenue) ÷ (% change in nominal GDP)**. Buoyancy **> 1** means tax revenue is growing faster than the economy. Compare **tax elasticity**, which measures the automatic response of revenue to income *at unchanged rates and base*.
- **Reference points for a comparison line:** buoyancy was about **2.52 in FY22** (a post-Covid base effect) and slipped **below 2 in FY23** — so 1.39 is healthy but not exceptional, and you should say so rather than call it a record.
- **Who publishes:** the **CBDT**, a statutory body under the **Central Board of Revenue Act, 1963**, functioning within the **Department of Revenue, Ministry of Finance**. Its indirect-tax counterpart is the **CBIC**.
- **Direct vs indirect:** direct taxes cannot be shifted (incidence and impact fall on the same person) and are **progressive**; indirect taxes are shiftable and **regressive** in incidence. A rising direct-tax share is generally read as an improvement in equity, so the corporate/non-corporate crossover cuts against that reading.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Economy (Public Finance / Taxation).

**STATIC ANCHOR:** **Mrunal Pillar 2 — Budget & Taxation**; **Ramesh Singh, "Tax Structure in India"** chapter.
*Update / cross-link nudge:* Write the buoyancy formula and the **buoyancy vs. elasticity** distinction in one box at the top of your taxation page — this is the exact place people lose a Prelims mark. Then add a small four-row series (FY22, FY23, FY24, FY25) so you can quote a **trend** rather than a single number in Mains. ### C. Mains-Style Question (GS2 — Polity & Governance, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"The collegium system replaced executive primacy in judicial appointments with judicial primacy, but it did not replace one form of opacity with transparency."** Critically examine this proposition in the light of the constitutional scheme for appointing judges of the higher judiciary and developments since 2015.

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Establish the constitutional baseline, then the drift.** Articles 124 and 217 speak only of "consultation" — the Constitution contemplated executive appointment with judicial input. *S.P. Gupta* (1981) read that literally; the Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges Cases inverted it, creating a collegium the text does not mention. State plainly that judicial primacy is a judicial construct, not a constitutional command — this framing earns marks with either examiner.

2. **Concede the strongest case for the collegium.** The NJAC judgment (2015, 4:1) struck down the 99th Amendment because executive presence in the appointing body, combined with the veto structure, threatened judicial independence — a basic-structure feature. Independence is not a perk of judges; it is a guarantee to litigants against the State, which is the largest litigant in India. The post-1993 arrangement has, on the record, insulated appointments from overt political capture.

3. **Then press the criticism, with specifics.** No published criteria, no reasoned rejections until recently, no secretariat or evaluation infrastructure, and a Memorandum of Procedure unfinalised since 2015. Add the executive's counter-lever: selective clearing and indefinite sitting on reiterated names, which produces prolonged vacancies and acting Chief Justices — the very condition the September 2026 round of eight appointments has just corrected. Note the persistent under-representation of women, SC/ST and OBC judges, and the perception of a self-perpetuating pool.

4. **Close on reform, not lament.** Argue for the achievable middle: a permanent, professionally staffed **judicial appointments secretariat**; published objective criteria and a written record of reasons; statutory timelines binding on the executive after reiteration; and a transparent, diverse zone of consideration. Conclude that independence and accountability are not a zero-sum pair — the legitimacy of the judiciary rests on both, and the reform question is procedural design, not a return to executive primacy.

### D. PYQ Mirror — how UPSC has asked these themes before

**Theme: volcanoes and Indian/Indian Ocean volcanism — *verified*.**
**UPSC Prelims 2018 (Q85):** *"Consider the following statements: 1. The Barren Island volcano is an active volcano located in the Indian territory. 2. Barren Island lies about 140 km east of Great Nicobar. 3. The last time the Barren Island volcano erupted was in 1991 and it has remained inactive since then. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?"* — **Answer: 1 only.** Statement 2 is wrong (Barren Island lies roughly 140 km **north-east of Port Blair**, not east of Great Nicobar), and statement 3 is wrong (further eruptions occurred in 1994-95, 2005-07, 2008-09 and 2017).
*Lesson:* the examiner's trap in volcano questions is almost always **location** and **currency of activity**, not the geology. Apply the same discipline to Anak Krakatau: fix that it is in the **Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra**, and that the 2018 tsunami came from a **flank collapse**.
