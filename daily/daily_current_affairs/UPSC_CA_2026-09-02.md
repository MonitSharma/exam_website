### 1. Supreme Court uses Article 142 to wipe out every FIR against the NEET-UG protesters — Polity & Governance

**Headline.** On **1 September 2026**, a Bench of **CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana** invoked **Article 142** to quash **all FIRs registered anywhere in India between 20 and 25 July 2026** arising out of the NEET-UG paper-leak demonstrations — including FIRs not before the Court, in States and UTs that were not parties — and **barred the registration of fresh FIRs** on the same set of events. The Court also directed the Centre to **notify a pan-India compensation framework within three months** for families of students who died by suicide in connection with the 2026 NEET examination.

**Exam angle.** This is the cleanest Article 142 illustration you will get, and it is examinable from two opposite directions. On the **enabling** side: Article 142(1) lets the Supreme Court pass "such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice", and *Union Carbide v. Union of India* (1991) and *Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat* (1991) hold that ordinary statutory limits cannot fetter it — which is how the Court can close criminal proceedings in States that never appeared before it. On the **limiting** side: *Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner* (1963) holds that Article 142 cannot be exercised in a way inconsistent with fundamental rights or with substantive statutory provisions, and *Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India* (1998) holds it cannot be used to supplant substantive law. The order is therefore a live judicial-overreach-vs-complete-justice prompt: quashing FIRs of non-parties and *prospectively barring* police from registering FIRs edges into what is normally executive and legislative space. Second angle — link it to the **right to protest**: peaceful assembly under **Article 19(1)(b)** read with the reasonable-restrictions clause **19(3)**, and *Anita Thakur v. State of J&K* (2016) and *Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary* (2012) on excessive force against demonstrators. Third angle — the compensation direction is a **continuing mandamus**, the same device used in *Vishaka* and the Taj Trapezium line of orders.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Order date **1 September 2026**; Bench **CJI Surya Kant, J. Joymalya Bagchi, J. V. Mohana**; power invoked **Article 142**.
- Scope: **all FIRs registered 20–25 July 2026** on the NEET-UG protests, across all States/UTs, whether or not before the Court; **future FIRs on the same events barred**; cases "closed for all practical purposes".
- Reasoning line worth quoting: **mere participation in a peaceful demonstration is not a penal offence.**
- Additional direction: Centre to **frame and notify a pan-India compensation policy in three months** for families of 2026 NEET-linked student suicides.
- **Article 142(1)** — decrees/orders for complete justice, enforceable throughout India in the manner prescribed by Parliament or Presidential order. **Article 142(2)** — power to secure attendance of witnesses, discovery/production of documents, and to punish contempt.
- **Only the Supreme Court** has this power; High Courts cannot import it into Article 226 (*Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain*, 2009). Article 226 and **Section 528 BNSS, 2023** (successor to Section 482 CrPC) are the High Court's quashing routes.
- Boundary cases: **Prem Chand Garg (1963)** — cannot override fundamental rights or substantive law; **SCBA v. UoI (1998)** — cannot supplant substantive statutory law; **Union Carbide (1991)** and **Delhi Judicial Service Association (1991)** — statutory limits do not curtail it.
- Recent high-profile Article 142 uses to keep on the same page: dissolution of marriage on irretrievable breakdown (*Shilpa Sailesh*, 2023), the 2G and coal-block cancellations, and the Court's 2025 caution that Article 142 is not a "carte blanche".

**Notebook:** GS2 — Polity (Judiciary: powers and jurisdiction of the Supreme Court; separation of powers) with a GS2 Governance cross-tag on the right to protest.

**Static anchor:** **Laxmikanth — Chapter on the Supreme Court** (jurisdiction and powers), plus the "Judicial Activism / Judicial Review" chapter. *Nudge: on your Supreme Court jurisdiction page, the Article 142 entry is probably one line. Turn it into a four-row box — text of 142(1) and (2) | enabling case law (Union Carbide, Delhi Judicial Service Association) | limiting case law (Prem Chand Garg, SCBA v. UoI) | 2023–26 applications (Shilpa Sailesh, NEET FIRs 2026) — and cross-link to your Article 19(1)(b) page and to the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 note you made on 28 August.*

### 2. GCRMN's global coral report: the recovery window between heatwaves has closed — Environment

**Headline.** The **Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network (GCRMN)** released *Status of Coral Reefs of the World* on **31 August 2026** — the largest reef assessment ever attempted, built on **21.1 million observations from 36,886 reef sites in 124 countries**. Global hard-coral cover has fallen about **9.5% relative to the 1980–2009 baseline**, with an **8.9% relative drop between 2023 and 2024 alone** — the sharpest single-year contraction on record. The report's central finding is not the loss but the **interval**: mass disturbance events that used to recur roughly once a decade now recur every **4–6 years**, shorter than the time slow-calcifying corals need to regrow.

**Exam angle.** Three separable hooks. **(a) Mechanism** — bleaching is the expulsion of endosymbiotic *zooxanthellae* (dinoflagellates of the genus *Symbiodinium*) under thermal stress; the coral is not dead at that point but loses ~90% of its energy supply and becomes vulnerable to disease and starvation. UPSC has asked the *zooxanthellae* relationship directly. **(b) The metric** — **Degree Heating Weeks (DHW)**, NOAA's accumulated thermal-stress unit: 4 °C-weeks triggers significant bleaching, 8 °C-weeks widespread mortality. The report's line that over 25% of global reef area crossed 4 °C-weeks four or more times between 2019 and 2025 is exactly the sort of quantified statement worth carrying into a Mains answer. **(c) India relevance** — India's four major reef systems are the **Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands**, all in the Indo-Pacific province the report flags; corals are on **Schedule I of the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972**, and reef areas fall under **CRZ-I** (the most restricted zone) under the CRZ Notification, 2019. The **ROPME Sea Area (Persian Gulf and Sea of Oman) lost roughly 50% of hard coral cover between 2020 and 2024** — a useful, precise regional figure, and one that connects neatly to today's Larak Island item.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Publisher: **GCRMN**, the reef-monitoring network operating under the **International Coral Reef Initiative (ICRI, 1994)**, with **UNEP** support. Release **31 August 2026**.
- Evidence base: **21.1 million observations, 36,886 sites, 124 countries and territories** — largest ever.
- **Net decline ~9.5%** (range 4%–14.5%) in hard-coral cover relative to the **1980–2009** baseline; cover fell from just over **30% (2009–2020)** to **25.8% in 2024**.
- **8.9% relative fall between 2023 and 2024** — largest annual contraction recorded.
- **2024: 87% of monitored reefs** hit unprecedented heat anomalies; **42.6%** crossed extreme-mortality thresholds (27.4% in 2025).
- **Four global mass-bleaching events: 1998–99, 2010–11, 2016–17, 2023–25**, each costing 6.5%–9.9% relative cover.
- **Disturbance interval down from ~10 years to 4–6 years.** Over **25% of reef area** exceeded **4 °C-weeks** of heat stress on four or more occasions, 2019–2025.
- Reef-province mean **SST rose 0.81 °C between 1985 and 2025**.
- Regional collapse points: **Caribbean average hard-coral cover ~1%**; **>90% mortality of wild and restored *Acropora* in Belize**; **100% loss of *Acropora palmata* at Puerto Morelos, Mexico**; **ROPME Sea Area −50% (2020–24)**.
- One genuine positive: about **6% relative recovery, 2017–2019** — reefs do rebound if the interval is long enough.
- Threshold to quote: at **1.5 °C** of warming 70–90% of warm-water reefs are lost; at **2 °C**, **over 99%**.
- Reefs cover **<0.1% of the ocean floor** but support roughly **25% of all marine species**.
- India: **Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep, Andaman & Nicobar**; corals **Schedule I, WPA 1972**; reefs classified **CRZ-I** under the **CRZ Notification, 2019**.
- Policy anchors: **Kunming-Montreal GBF Target 3 (30×30)**; **Coral Triangle**; ocean acidification lowers the **aragonite saturation state**, slowing calcification.

### 3. NASA's Roman Space Telescope is up — a Hubble-resolution camera with 100× the field of view — Science & Technology

**Headline.** The **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope** launched on **30 August 2026 at 7:26 a.m. EDT** aboard a **SpaceX Falcon Heavy** from **Launch Complex 39A, Kennedy Space Center**, separating from the upper stage 31 minutes into flight. Roman carries a **2.4-metre primary mirror** — the same aperture as Hubble — but a **300-megapixel near-infrared Wide Field Instrument** that images an area **about 100 times larger** per exposure. It is heading for a **halo orbit around the Sun-Earth L2 point**, roughly 1.5 million km from Earth.

**Exam angle.** Prelims will not ask you about Roman's science operations; it will ask you to **place a telescope on the electromagnetic spectrum and in an orbit**. Build the comparison table: **Hubble** — visible/UV/near-IR, low Earth orbit ~540 km, serviceable; **JWST** — infrared, L2, 6.5 m segmented mirror, deep and narrow; **Roman** — visible/near-IR, L2, 2.4 m, wide and fast; **Chandra** — X-ray, highly elliptical Earth orbit; **India's AstroSat** — multi-wavelength (UV to hard X-ray), LEO, launched 2015. The second high-yield concept is **why L2**: it is one of the five **Lagrange points** where the gravitational pull of two large bodies plus centripetal requirement lets a small object hold station; L2 sits on the far side of Earth from the Sun so Sun, Earth and Moon stay in one direction, allowing a single sunshield and a stable cold environment. India's **Aditya-L1** sits at **L1** — the halo orbit that gives continuous solar view — so the L1-vs-L2 distinction is directly examinable. Third: Roman's two detection techniques are both concept-questions in their own right — **gravitational microlensing** (a foreground star's gravity briefly magnifies a background star; a planet around the lens adds a secondary spike — this is how free-floating "rogue" planets are found) and **coronagraphy** (blocking host starlight to image faint companions directly). Roman's **Coronagraph Instrument suppresses starlight by a factor of over one billion**.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Launch **30 August 2026**, **Falcon Heavy**, **LC-39A, Kennedy Space Center**; managed by **NASA Goddard Space Flight Center**.
- Former name **WFIRST** (Wide Field Infrared Survey Telescope); renamed for **Dr Nancy Grace Roman**, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy, called the "Mother of Hubble".
- **2.4 m primary mirror** — Hubble-equivalent resolution; **Wide Field Instrument**, **300-megapixel** near-infrared camera; **field of view ~100× Hubble's**.
- Can map the **entire Milky Way plane in about a month** — a task that would take Hubble on the order of a century.
- **Coronagraph Instrument (CGI)** with active wavefront control; starlight suppression **>10⁹**.
- Destination: **quasi-halo orbit at Sun-Earth L2, ~1.5 million km** from Earth.
- Science goals: **dark energy**, **dark matter**, tests of **general relativity** at cosmic scale, and a statistical census of **exoplanets** via **gravitational microlensing** — including rogue planets.
- Complementarity: **JWST = deep and narrow; Roman = wide and fast; Hubble = visible baseline.**
- Lagrange points: **five (L1–L5)**; **L1, L2, L3 unstable; L4 and L5 stable** (Trojan points). **Aditya-L1 → L1**; **JWST and Roman → L2**.
- Indian comparators for the same page: **AstroSat (2015)**, India's first dedicated multi-wavelength space observatory; **XPoSat (Jan 2024)**, X-ray polarimetry; **Aditya-L1 (Sept 2023)**.

**Notebook:** GS3 — Science & Technology (space technology; awareness in the field of space).

**Static anchor:** **Sci-Tech notes — "Space Observatories / Astronomy Missions"** page and the **Lagrange Points** diagram. *Nudge: convert your telescope notes into a single five-row table — Mission | Wavelength | Orbit | Aperture | USP — with Hubble, JWST, Roman, Chandra and AstroSat as rows. ### 4. Places in News: Larak Island, and why the Strait of Hormuz is India's problem — Geography

**Headline.** US forces struck **two IRGC rocket launchers on Larak Island** on **30 August 2026** after surveillance showed crews preparing to fire rockets carrying **naval sea mines** into the Strait of Hormuz — days after the US Navy had finished clearing mines from the same international shipping lanes. Iran's IRGC called it a "fatal mistake" and promised military and economic retaliation.

**Exam angle.** Read this as a **map-plus-chokepoint** question rather than a war story. **Locate it precisely:** Larak is an Iranian island in the **Strait of Hormuz**, off **Bandar Abbas**, immediately **east of Qeshm Island** and **south of Hormuz Island**. The Strait separates **Iran (north)** from **Oman's Musandam exclave and the UAE (south)** and connects the **Persian Gulf** to the **Gulf of Oman**, which opens into the **Arabian Sea**. **Why it matters to India:** roughly a fifth to a quarter of globally traded seaborne oil and a very large share of LNG transit here; India imports the bulk of its crude and much of its LPG from Gulf suppliers, and about **8–9 million Indians** live and work in the Gulf, making this simultaneously an **energy security**, **remittance** and **diaspora-evacuation** question. Note the workaround infrastructure: **Saudi Arabia's East–West (Petroline) pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea** and the **UAE's Habshan–Fujairah pipeline** can bypass the Strait, but only for a fraction of Gulf volumes; **Qatar's LNG has no bypass at all**. Add the legal layer: the Strait's narrowest point is about **21 nautical miles**, so the whole channel lies within the territorial seas of the littorals — commercial and naval passage rests on the **UNCLOS regime of transit passage through straits used for international navigation**, which Iran (a signatory but not a ratifying party to UNCLOS) contests in principle. A neat physical-geography bonus: **Larak is a salt diapir** — a *salt plug* formed by **halokinesis**, buoyant subsurface salt pushing up through overlying sediments, the same process that creates the salt domes used to store strategic petroleum reserves.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Larak Island** (also "Lark") — **Iranian**, in the **Strait of Hormuz**, off **Bandar Abbas**; **east of Qeshm**, **south of Hormuz Island**; highest point about **138 m**; main settlement **Larak Shahri**.
- Formation: **salt plug / salt diapir** via **halokinesis**; surface of rock salt, sandstone and iron oxide.
- Inhabitants largely speak **Laraki**, a dialect of the endangered **Kumzari** language. **16th-century Portuguese fortifications** survive on the island.
- Ringed by some of the Persian Gulf's most diverse reefs — about **37 documented stony coral species** (ties directly to today's GCRMN item).
- **Strait of Hormuz:** connects **Persian Gulf → Gulf of Oman → Arabian Sea**; **Iran** to the north, **Oman (Musandam exclave)** and **UAE** to the south; narrowest width about **21 nautical miles (~33–39 km)**; carries roughly **a fifth of global seaborne oil** and a large share of LNG.
- Landlocked-on-the-Gulf logic for the map question: ships from **Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait and Iraq** must cross Hormuz to reach the open Indian Ocean; **Oman, UAE (Fujairah) and Saudi Arabia (Red Sea coast)** have coastline outside it.
- Bypass pipelines: **Saudi East–West (Petroline) to Yanbu**; **UAE Habshan–Fujairah**. Qatar has **no bypass**.
- Other Gulf islands routinely confused with Larak: **Kharg** (Iran's main oil export terminal), **Abu Musa**, **Greater and Lesser Tunbs** (disputed between Iran and the UAE), **Qeshm** (largest island in the Gulf).
- India's stake: crude and LPG import dependence; **~8–9 million-strong Gulf diaspora**; the 2019 **Operation Sankalp** naval escort mission in the Gulf is the precedent for Indian Navy deployment here.

### 5. Rani Avantibai Lodhi gets a statue in Lucknow — the 1857 revolt beyond the four famous names — Modern History

**Headline.** Defence Minister **Rajnath Singh**, with UP Chief Minister **Yogi Adityanath**, unveiled a statue of **Veerangana Rani Avantibai Lodhi** in **Lucknow on 30 August 2026**, drawing a line from the 1857 warrior queens to women officers who flew combat roles in Operation Sindoor.

**Exam angle.** UPSC's 1857 questions have moved steadily away from Delhi–Kanpur–Lucknow and toward **regional centres and their leaders** — exactly the space Avantibai occupies. Her case is also the textbook illustration of a **doctrine that is not the Doctrine of Lapse**: her husband died leaving **minor** heirs, so the Company did not annex Ramgarh outright — it placed the estate under the **Court of Wards** and installed *sarbarahkars* (superintendents), a mechanism used for minor or "incompetent" heirs. That is a distinction students routinely get wrong, and it is precisely the sort of thing a statement-based Prelims question exploits. Avantibai expelled the Company's administrators, called on cultivators to refuse British revenue demands, and coordinated with the **Gond ruler Raja Shankar Shah of Garha-Mandla** through the famous **bangle appeal** — glass bangles sent with letters to neighbouring chiefs and zamindars, taunting them to fight or accept the bangles. She won at the **Battle of Kheri** near Mandla against Deputy Commissioner Waddington in 1857, then shifted to guerrilla operations from the **Devhargarh** hills, and took her own life on **20 March 1858** at Sukhi-Talaiya rather than be captured. Her Mahakoshal base — Gond and farming country — is the entry point for the **tribal dimension of 1857**, which is separate from and usually more useful than the sepoy narrative.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Maharani Avantibai Lodhi**, **16 August 1831 – 20 March 1858**; queen-regent of the estate of **Ramgarh** (present-day **Dindori and Mandla districts, Madhya Pradesh**); born at **Mankedi village, Seoni district**.
- Married **Raja Vikramaditya Singh of Ramgarh**; assumed regency for minor sons **Aman Singh** and **Sher Singh** when he was incapacitated.
- Mechanism of British seizure: **Court of Wards** with **sarbarahkars** — not the **Doctrine of Lapse** (which applied to rulers dying *without* a natural heir).
- Ally: **Raja Shankar Shah**, Gond ruler of **Garha-Mandla**, executed by the British at Jabalpur in **September 1857** along with his son **Raghunath Shah** (blown from a cannon).
- **The bangle appeal** — glass bangles sent with letters to neighbouring rulers and zamindars as a challenge to join the revolt.
- **Battle of Kheri** (near Mandla, late 1857) — roughly **4,000 troops** defeated the force under Deputy Commissioner **Waddington**.
- Retreat to the **Devhargarh** hills; guerrilla strikes; encircled at **Sukhi-Talaiya** between Balapur and Ramgarh; **martyrdom on 20 March 1858**, aged 27.
- Group her with the other *veeranganas*: **Rani Lakshmibai (Jhansi)**, **Jhalkari Bai** (Lakshmibai's Durga Dal commander), **Begum Hazrat Mahal (Awadh)**, **Rani Durgavati** (16th-century Gond queen of Garha-Katanga — *not* 1857; a standard trap), **Uda Devi (Sikandar Bagh)**.
- Other 1857 centre-and-leader pairs to have cold: **Delhi — Bahadur Shah II / Bakht Khan; Kanpur — Nana Saheb / Tantia Tope; Lucknow — Begum Hazrat Mahal; Jhansi — Rani Lakshmibai; Bareilly — Khan Bahadur Khan; Bihar (Jagdishpur) — Kunwar Singh; Faizabad — Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah; Allahabad — Liaquat Ali; Assam — Kandarpeshwar Singha; Orissa (Sambalpur) — Surendra Sai.**

### C. Mains-Style Question

**GS2 — Polity & Governance (250 words, 15 marks)**

*"Article 142 was conceived as a residuary power to fill the gaps left by law, not as a parallel source of law."* **Critically examine** this proposition in the light of recent orders of the Supreme Court quashing criminal proceedings and issuing prospective directions to the executive.

**Model-answer skeleton.**

1. **Frame the provision, not the controversy.** Open with the text: Article 142(1) permits "such decree or order as is necessary for doing complete justice" in any cause or matter *pending* before the Court, enforceable throughout India; 142(2) covers attendance of witnesses, discovery and contempt. Note its constituent-assembly rationale — a residuary equitable power for a court of last resort in a system where statutes cannot anticipate every injustice. Note also that it is **exclusive to the Supreme Court**; High Courts operate under Article 226 and Section 528 BNSS (*Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain*).

2. **The case for a wide reading.** *Union Carbide* (1991) and *Delhi Judicial Service Association* (1991) hold that ordinary statutory limits do not fetter Article 142; the power has closed proceedings, dissolved marriages on irretrievable breakdown (*Shilpa Sailesh*, 2023), and delivered relief where executive inaction or administrative failure left no remedy. The September 2026 order quashing all FIRs against NEET-UG protesters across States that were not parties, and directing a national compensation policy, is the current illustration: without Article 142, relief would have required separate proceedings in every High Court, and the protesters' Article 19(1)(b) claim would have been vindicated unevenly, if at all.

3. **The case for restraint.** *Prem Chand Garg* (1963) — the power cannot violate fundamental rights or ignore substantive law; *SCBA v. Union of India* (1998) — it cannot supplant substantive statutory law. Three specific concerns arise from the recent pattern: **(i)** binding non-parties and States that were never heard raises an audi alteram partem problem; **(ii)** *prospectively barring* FIRs pre-empts the police's statutory duty under the BNSS, which is executive function, not adjudication; **(iii)** directing the Centre to notify a policy within a fixed period edges into legislative and executive policy space, engaging the separation-of-powers concern the Court itself flagged in cautioning that Article 142 is not a "carte blanche". There is also an equality dimension — case-specific equity produces outcomes not available to similarly placed litigants who cannot reach the Supreme Court.

4. **Resolve, don't hedge.** Argue for a **principled, self-limiting standard** rather than either abdication or expansion: Article 142 is defensible where (i) the injustice is systemic and remediless, (ii) no substantive statutory bar is overridden, and (iii) the order is transitional, expressly stated to operate only until the competent organ acts (the *Vishaka* model). Close with the institutional point: the legitimacy of the power depends on its rarity, and the surest safeguard against overreach is legislative and executive responsiveness — where Parliament and the executive act promptly, the gap Article 142 fills does not open in the first place.

### D. PYQ Mirror — how UPSC has asked these themes before

**Coral reefs.** *Prelims 2018* asked a three-statement question: (1) most of the world's coral reefs are in tropical waters; (2) more than one-third of the world's coral reefs are in the territories of Australia, Indonesia and the Philippines; (3) coral reefs host far more animal phyla than tropical rainforests. **Answer: all three are correct.** The examiner's interest was **distribution and biodiversity**, not bleaching statistics — so the GCRMN report matters to you mainly as the source of the *numbers* you would deploy in a Mains answer, while the Prelims-relevant residue is the phyla point (reefs host 32 of 34 recognised animal phyla against about 9 in tropical rainforests) and the geography of reef distribution. UPSC has separately tested the **coral–*zooxanthellae* symbiosis** and the **CRZ classification** of reef areas.

**Strait of Hormuz.** *Prelims 2026* (GS Paper I) asked: **"Ships from which of the following countries have to cross the Strait of Hormuz to reach the Indian Ocean?"** — the answer turning on Bahrain and Qatar, both of which have coastline only inside the Persian Gulf. This is exactly the format the Larak Island story feeds: not "what happened", but **who is enclosed by the chokepoint**. Practise the same logic for Bab-el-Mandeb (which countries must cross it to leave the Red Sea) and the Turkish Straits.

**Revolt of 1857.** *Prelims 2005* asked: **"Which one of the following places did Kunwar Singh, a prominent leader of the Revolt of 1857, belong to?"** (answer: **Jagdishpur**, Bihar). The centre-and-leader pairing format has recurred; assume it can appear as a match-the-pairs or "how many pairs are correctly matched" question, which is why the twelve-row table in the static-anchor nudge above is worth building. *[Flag: the 2005 attribution is verified; UPSC's Mains treatment of Article 142 specifically could not be confirmed as a stand-alone PYQ, so treat the Mains question above as a constructed prompt in UPSC's idiom rather than as a reproduced PYQ.]*

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**Q1 — (c) 1 and 3 only.** Statement 1 restates Article 142(1). Statement 2 is wrong: Article 142 is exclusive to the Supreme Court, and the Court held in *Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain* that High Courts cannot exercise an equivalent power under Article 226. Statement 3 reflects *Prem Chand Garg* (1963) and *SCBA v. Union of India* (1998).

**Q2 — (b) 1 and 3 only.** GCRMN operates under the International Coral Reef Initiative with UNEP support. Statement 2 inverts the finding: the interval has **shortened** from roughly once a decade to every 4–6 years. Statement 3 is correct — global hard coral cover fell 8.9% in relative terms between 2023 and 2024, the sharpest single-year contraction recorded.

**Q3 — (b) 1 and 3 only.** Roman carries a 2.4 m primary mirror, the same diameter as Hubble's, but with a field of view about 100 times larger. Statement 2 is wrong: Roman is bound for a halo orbit around Sun-Earth **L2**, about 1.5 million km away, which is far beyond servicing range — Hubble, in low Earth orbit, was the serviceable one. Microlensing is one of Roman's two principal exoplanet techniques, alongside coronagraphy.

**Q4 — (a).** Larak is an Iranian island in the Strait of Hormuz off Bandar Abbas, east of Qeshm and south of Hormuz Island, geologically a salt diapir rising to about 138 m. Option (b) misplaces it at the Gulf of Aden; (c) describes the Tiran/Sanafir dispute; (d) is fabricated — the Habshan–Fujairah pipeline terminates on the UAE's Gulf of Oman coast, not on an island.

**Q5 — (b).** Avantibai Lodhi (1831–1858) was queen-regent of Ramgarh in the Mahakoshal region (present-day Dindori/Mandla, MP) and led the 1857 revolt there, winning at Kheri before her martyrdom on 20 March 1858. Option (a) describes **Rani Durgavati**, the sixteenth-century Gond queen of Garha-Katanga — the classic confusion this question tests.

**Q6 — (a) 1 and 2 only.** L4 and L5 are the stable Lagrange points; L1, L2 and L3 are unstable and require station-keeping. L2 lies on the anti-Sun side of the Earth, which is why JWST and Roman use it. Statement 3 is wrong: **Aditya-L1** is at **L1**, the point between the Sun and the Earth, chosen for continuous unobstructed solar observation.

**Q7 — (c) Only three.** Pairs 1, 2 and 4 are correct — Kunwar Singh at Jagdishpur, Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah at Faizabad, and Shankar Shah at Garha-Mandla. Pair 3 is wrong: **Sambalpur** was led by **Surendra Sai**; **Khan Bahadur Khan** led the revolt at **Bareilly**.
