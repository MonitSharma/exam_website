## PART A — BRIEFING

### 1. Parliament passes the Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026 — a National Tribunals Commission at last — Polity & Governance

**Headline:** The Rajya Sabha passed the **Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026** on **11 August 2026** amid an Opposition walkout, completing Parliamentary passage (Lok Sabha had introduced *and* passed it on **10 August**, without debate). The Bill **repeals the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** and creates a permanent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** to run appointments, performance review and discipline across India's tribunal system.

**Exam angle:** This is the single most examinable Polity development of the fortnight because it closes a two-decade judicial-executive standoff. The 2021 Act's core provisions — four-year tenure, minimum age of 50, and the government's freedom to pick from a two-name panel — were struck down by the Supreme Court in **Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2021)** for violating **separation of powers and judicial independence**; the 2026 Bill is Parliament legislating *in consonance with* those directions rather than against them. The NTC itself is a demand traceable to **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)** and repeated in the *Madras Bar Association* line of cases and by the **Law Commission (272nd Report, 2017)**. Note the residual criticism to carry into Mains: appointments to the Commission are still **made by the Central Government**, and salaries/service conditions are still left to **executive rule-making** — so "who controls the controller" survives as a live objection.

**Facts to memorise:**

- Introduced **and** passed in Lok Sabha on **10 August 2026**; passed by Rajya Sabha **11 August 2026**. Ministry: **Law and Justice** (Minister of State Arjun Ram Meghwal). **Repeals the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021.** Jurisdiction of tribunals is **not** altered.
- **National Tribunals Commission — functions:** (i) conduct the selection process for tribunal vacancies, (ii) review tribunal performance, (iii) oversee inquiries into complaints against chairpersons/members, (iv) develop and maintain a **National Tribunals Data Grid**.
- **NTC composition (5):** a **chairperson** who has been a **Judge of the Supreme Court or a Chief Justice of a High Court**; **two judicial members** (former CJ/Judge of a High Court); **two technical members** with ≥ **25 years'** experience in public administration, finance, law, accountancy, banking, management or technology.
- **NTC tenure:** 5 years or age **70**, whichever is earlier. Appointed by the **Central Government**; chairperson and judicial members appointed **after consultation with the CJI**. The NTC gets its own **Secretariat**, headed by a Secretary to the Government.
- **Selection to tribunals:** a search-cum-selection committee, chaired by the NTC chairperson (for a tribunal chairperson) or an NTC judicial member (for a member). It recommends **one name plus one waitlisted name** per vacancy; the Centre must appoint **within three months**. The committee chairperson has a **casting vote**; expert members and the Commission Secretary have **no vote**.
- **Tribunal tenure:** 5 years, or age **70 for chairpersons** and **67 for members**, whichever is earlier. **Re-appointment permitted**, subject to past performance (and, for a member, consultation with the tribunal's chairperson).

**Static anchor:** **Laxmikanth, Chapter 42 — "Tribunals"** (read with Ch. 25, "Supreme Court", on judicial review). Add a dated timeline strip — *Art. 323A/323B (42nd Amdt, 1976) → L. ### 2. Centre clears one billion each of ₹10 and ₹20 **polymer** banknotes for field trials — Economy

**Headline:** Replying in the **Rajya Sabha on 11 August 2026**, the Finance Ministry confirmed that the Government has approved the RBI's proposal — submitted under **Section 25 of the RBI Act, 1934** — to introduce **one billion pieces each of ₹10 and ₹20 polymer banknotes** for **nationwide field trials**, with regular issuance to follow only if the trials succeed. (The same approval was first disclosed in a Lok Sabha written reply on 28 July 2026; the 11 August reply added that procurement is still at an initial stage, so neither the timeline nor the expenditure can yet be fixed.)

**Exam angle:** A near-perfect Prelims item because it forces you back into the plumbing of currency management — *who* decides denominations versus *who* decides design and material. Denominations are governed by **Section 24**; **form, material and design** require **Central Government approval on the recommendation of the RBI's Central Board** under **Section 25** — which is exactly the section cited here, and exactly the kind of distinction UPSC monetises. For Mains, the framing is soil-to-shelf economics of cash: polymer notes cost more to print but last **2–5× longer**, resist soiling and are far harder to counterfeit, which matters most in **low-denomination, high-velocity** notes like ₹10 and ₹20 that get destroyed fastest. Note the explicit official position: polymer notes will circulate **alongside** paper notes, and there is **no proposal to replace paper currency**. Also note the counter-argument you should carry — polymer is a **thermoplastic (BOPP)** substrate, so end-of-life disposal and recyclability become an environmental question, and India has tried and shelved plastic ₹10 field trials before (announced 2009, revived 2015–17, never rolled out).

**Facts to memorise:**

- Quantum approved: **1 billion pieces each of ₹10 and ₹20** (2 billion pieces in total), **for field trials only**. Enabling provision: **Section 25, RBI Act, 1934**.
- **Section 22, RBI Act:** RBI has the **sole right to issue banknotes** in India. **Section 24:** denominations — the RBI may issue notes up to a maximum of **₹10,000**, in denominations specified by the Central Government on the Central Board's recommendation. **Section 26:** every banknote is **legal tender**, guaranteed by the Central Government.
- The **₹1 note is issued by the Government of India** (Ministry of Finance), signed by the **Finance Secretary**, under the **Coinage Act, 2011** — not by the RBI.
- **Minimum Reserve System** (since **1957**): RBI must hold minimum reserves of **₹200 crore**, of which **gold ≥ ₹115 crore**.
- Currency printing presses: **Nashik** and **Dewas** (SPMCIL, Government-owned) and **Mysuru** and **Salboni** (BRBNMPL, RBI-owned). Paper/ink security features fall under the **Bharatiya Reserve Bank Note Mudran** and SPMCIL mandates.
- Global context: **Australia** was the first country to issue a full polymer series (from 1988); the UK, Canada, Singapore, Vietnam and Nigeria have followed for selected denominations.

**Static anchor:** **Mrunal — Pillar 1A (Money & Banking: RBI's functions)**. Add a one-page "Who decides what about a banknote?" table — *Sec 22 (sole right) / Sec 24 (denomination, ≤ ₹10,000) / Sec 25 (design, form, material) / Sec 26 (legal tender) / Coinage Act 2011 (₹1 note & coins)* — and **cross-link** it to your Currency in Circulation and Clean Note Policy notes.

### 3. NASA invites ISRO to join its Moon Base programme — the India–US space partnership goes beyond Earth orbit — International Relations

**Headline:** Disclosed on **11 August 2026**: NASA has invited **ISRO to join its Moon Base programme**, aimed at building humanity's first permanent lunar outpost near the **Moon's South Pole**. The offer was made at the **9th India–US Civil Space Joint Working Group**, held at **ISRO Headquarters, Bengaluru, on 5–6 August 2026**, and is framed within the **Artemis Accords**.

**Exam angle:** Treat this as an IR item first and a Sci-Tech item second — it is the space leg of the India–US strategic partnership, and it tells you where the relationship is heading after **NISAR**. The high-yield hook is *why the lunar south pole*: it holds **permanently shadowed craters** at the floors of which water ice is stable, and adjacent "**peaks of eternal light**" with near-continuous solar power — the two things a permanent habitat needs. That is also exactly why **Chandrayaan-3** landed there in August 2023, making India's own south-polar experience the bargaining chip in this invitation. For Mains, the tension to flag is legal: the **Artemis Accords** are a **US-led, non-binding political framework**, not a treaty, and their provision for "**safety zones**" and recognition of **space resource extraction** is contested by Russia and China (who run the rival **ILRS**, International Lunar Research Station) as sitting uneasily with the **non-appropriation principle in Article II of the Outer Space Treaty, 1967**.

**Facts to memorise:**

- Forum: **9th India–US Civil Space Joint Working Group**, ISRO HQ, **Bengaluru, 5–6 August 2026**. Invitation publicly reported **11 August 2026**.
- India became the **27th signatory of the Artemis Accords** on **21 June 2023** (during PM Modi's State Visit to the US). The Accords are **non-binding**, grounded in the **Outer Space Treaty, 1967**, and cover transparency, interoperability, emergency assistance, registration, release of scientific data, heritage preservation, space resources, deconfliction ("safety zones") and orbital debris.
- Immediate backdrop: **NISAR** (NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar), launched **30 July 2025** from Sriharikota on a **GSLV-F16**, the world's **first dual-frequency L-band + S-band radar imaging satellite**; it maps nearly all land and ice surfaces **twice every 12 days**. NASA supplied the L-band SAR, ISRO the S-band SAR and the spacecraft bus.
- Other live strands: **Gaganyaan** (uncrewed missions with the humanoid **Vyommitra**, crewed flight targeted 2027), and Group Captain **Shubhanshu Shukla**'s ISS flight on **Axiom-4** (2025) — the first Indian aboard the ISS.
- **Outer Space Treaty, 1967**, Article II: outer space, including the Moon, is **not subject to national appropriation** by claim of sovereignty, use, occupation or any other means. India has **not** ratified the **Moon Agreement, 1979**.
- **Chandrayaan-3** soft-landed near the lunar south pole (~**69.4° S**) on **23 August 2023**; the site is named **Shiv Shakti Point**, and 23 August is observed as **National Space Day**.

**Notebook:** GS2 — International Relations (India–US bilateral); cross-file in GS3 — Sci-Tech (Space)

**Static anchor:** **Your GS2 India–US country file** plus the **Sci-Tech "Space" note**. Add a running "India–US space cooperation ladder" — *Artemis Accords (2023) → NISAR (2025) → Axiom-4 (2025) → Moon Base invitation (2026)* — and **cross-link** it to your Outer Space Treaty page with a two-line note on the **Artemis Accords vs ILRS** contest, which is the ready-made "emerging domains of contestation" paragraph.

### 4. Ladakh's glaciers are **slowing down**, not just shrinking — Zanskar records a multi-decadal deceleration — Geography

**Headline:** A study in **The Cryosphere** (Ghosh, Ramsankaran, McCormack & Mackintosh, 2026) analysing **12 glaciers in the Zanskar Basin of Ladakh over 1992–2023** reports a **statistically significant, region-wide slowdown in glacier flow of 2.43 m yr⁻¹ per decade**, driven by thinning — the first clear evidence that Ladakh's ice is losing not just mass but *movement*.

**Exam angle:** The counter-intuitive physics is the whole point, and it is a Prelims-grade trap. Glacier flow is driven by **driving stress**, which scales with **ice thickness × surface slope**. As a glacier **thins**, driving stress falls, so the ice flows **more slowly** — thinning and deceleration are two faces of the same mass loss, not opposites. The exception the paper itself records is equally examinable: **lake-terminating glaciers showed local speed-ups near their termini**, because a proglacial lake removes back-stress and lets the terminus calve and slide. That single sentence links glaciology to **GLOF risk** — the Ladakh–Zanskar belt is precisely where proglacial lakes are expanding. Add the regional geography: Zanskar is a **cold desert in the trans-Himalaya**, where the **Indian summer monsoon barely penetrates** and most precipitation falls as **winter snow** brought by **Western Disturbances** — so this basin is a monsoon-independent, Western-Disturbance-fed cryosphere, which is a favourite UPSC contrast with the monsoon-fed Central Himalaya.

**Facts to memorise:**

- Study: *Thinning-induced glacier deceleration in the Zanskar Himalayas*, **The Cryosphere, vol. 20, pp. 3415–3433 (2026)**. Method: **satellite image correlation** (feature tracking) of surface velocity; elevation change from the Hugonnet et al. (2021) dataset.
- **Mean deceleration: 2.43 m yr⁻¹ per decade**, 1992–2023, across 12 glaciers.
- **Surface thinning accelerated**: from **~0.22 m yr⁻¹ (2000–2005)** to **~0.57 m yr⁻¹ (2015–2020)**; strongest thinning and velocity loss at **lower elevations** (ablation zones).
- **Dominant control:** ice mass loss → reduced **driving stress** → deceleration. Modifying factors: geometry, topography, **debris cover**, and **terminus type**. **Lake-terminating glaciers showed local velocity increases near the terminus.**
- **Zanskar Basin**: high-altitude **cold desert** in the **trans-Himalayan/Ladakh** region of the Western Himalaya; drained by the **Zanskar river**, the largest tributary of the **Indus** in Ladakh, joining it at **Nimmu (Nimoo)**; the frozen-river **Chadar trek** runs along it.
- Context: the **Karakoram Anomaly** — the anomalous stability or slight gain of Karakoram glaciers, contrasting with retreat across the rest of HKH. Also recall the **HKH Assessment (ICIMOD)**: ~one-third of HKH ice volume is projected to be lost by 2100 even at 1.5 °C.

**Notebook:** GS1 — Geography (Physical Geography: Glaciers & Cryosphere); cross-file in GS3 — Environment (Climate Change impacts, Disaster Management/GLOF)

**Static anchor:** **NCERT Class 11, *Fundamentals of Physical Geography*, Ch. 6–7 (Geomorphic processes / Landforms — glacial)** and **Shankar IAS, "Climate Change" chapter**. Add a "why thinning slows a glacier" box with the *driving stress ∝ thickness × slope* line, and **cross-link** it to your GLOF/Himalayan disasters note through the lake-terminating-glacier exception.

### 5. Deepak Dhar shares the **2026 ICTP Dirac Medal** — statistical mechanics from sandpiles to AI — Science & Technology

**Headline:** The **International Centre for Theoretical Physics (ICTP), Trieste** announced its **2026 Dirac Medal** on **8 August 2026**, awarded jointly to **Deepak Dhar** (INSA Distinguished Professor, **ICTS-TIFR Bengaluru**), **Bernard Derrida**, **Marc Mézard** and **Haim Sompolinsky** — for pioneering contributions to **equilibrium statistical mechanics** and for extending its concepts into **non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, optimization, theoretical neuroscience and artificial intelligence**.

**Exam angle:** Awards are usually skippable, but this one earns its place on three counts. First, it is a rare India-based recognition in fundamental theory — **only the second time a scientist working in India has won the Dirac Medal**, after **Ashoke Sen (2014)**. Second, Dhar is already the **first Indian to win the Boltzmann Medal (2022)**, so the pair makes a clean, memorable award-mapping question. Third — and this is the part worth actually understanding — the citation is the clearest official statement yet of *why* statistical physics underpins modern AI: the same tools that describe spin glasses and disordered magnets became the mathematics of **neural networks, optimization landscapes and learning dynamics**, the lineage that also produced the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** to Hopfield and Hinton. If you are writing **Physics optional**, this is your own paper's headline story of the year: Dhar's exactly solved **Abelian Sandpile Model** is the canonical model of **self-organized criticality**.

**Facts to memorise:**

- **Dirac Medal (ICTP)** — instituted **1985**; announced **every 8 August**, the birthday of **Paul A. M. Dirac**; awarded to living scientists for outstanding contributions to theoretical physics; recipients may **not** be prior Nobel, Wolf or Fields laureates. Administered by **ICTP, Trieste**, founded **1964** by **Abdus Salam**, operating under a tripartite agreement between **UNESCO, the IAEA and the Government of Italy**.
- **Indian-affiliated Dirac Medallists: Ashoke Sen (2014)** — shared with Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa — and **Deepak Dhar (2026)**.
- **Deepak Dhar**: previously at **TIFR Mumbai**, then **IISER Pune (2016)**, and **INSA Distinguished Professor at ICTS-TIFR since 2024**. **First Indian to receive the Boltzmann Medal (2022)**, awarded by the IUPAP Commission on Statistical Physics. Best known for the exact solution of the **Abelian Sandpile Model**, the foundational model of **self-organized criticality**.
- **Co-recipients:** **Bernard Derrida** (ASEP, random energy model), **Marc Mézard** (replica method, spin glasses, statistical inference), **Haim Sompolinsky** (theoretical neuroscience, chaos in neural networks).
- Useful pairing: the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** went to **John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton** for foundational discoveries enabling machine learning with artificial neural networks — the same physics-to-AI lineage.

**Notebook:** GS3 — Science & Technology (Awards & Institutions; AI foundations). *Physics optional:* Statistical Mechanics — critical phenomena and self-organized criticality.

**Static anchor:** **Your GS3 Sci-Tech "Awards & International Scientific Institutions" note** (add ICTP alongside CERN, ITER and the IAEA). **Cross-link** it to your **AI/Emerging Technologies** note with a two-line "statistical physics → neural networks" bridge, and, in your **Physics optional statistical mechanics** notes, add a margin entry on the Abelian Sandpile Model and self-organized criticality.

### C. Mains-Style Question

**GS2 — Polity & Governance (250 words, 15 marks)**

> *"The tribunalisation of justice in India has repeatedly foundered on the question of who controls the tribunals rather than what they adjudicate."* **Examine** this statement in the light of the constitutional and judicial evolution of tribunals, and assess whether the institutional architecture created by the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 addresses the concerns raised by the Supreme Court.

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Frame the original bargain.** Articles 323A/323B (42nd Amendment, 1976) sought speed and specialisation by shifting adjudication out of ordinary courts; today ~16–20 central tribunals (CAT, NGT, NCLT/NCLAT, ITAT, AFT, TDSAT, DRT/DRAT) carry a substantial share of litigation. But every tribunal was tethered to its **parent ministry** for appointments, tenure, salaries and infrastructure — and that parent ministry is very often the **largest litigant** before it. State the thesis: the deficit was never subject-matter competence but **institutional independence**.

2. **Trace the judicial correction.** *S. P. Sampath Kumar* (1987) accepted tribunals as substitutes for High Courts; *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) pulled back, holding judicial review under Arts. 226/227 to be **basic structure** and making tribunal orders subject to High Court Division Benches — which also destroyed the pendency rationale by adding a tier; *Madras Bar Association* (2010, 2014, 2020, 2021) struck down short tenures, low minimum ages, executive-dominated selection and the two-name panel. The **Law Commission's 272nd Report (2017)** recommended a **National Tribunals Commission**. Consequence: two decades of legislative-judicial ping-pong, with chronic vacancies as the real casualty.

3. **Assess the 2026 architecture.** Genuine gains: a **permanent, judicially-led NTC** (SC Judge / HC Chief Justice as chairperson, two judicial members, CJI consultation) replacing ad-hoc ministry-run committees; a **single recommended name plus one waitlist** with a **three-month** appointment deadline, which attacks the vacancy problem directly; **tenure raised to five years** with re-appointment allowed and age limits of 70/67, restoring the security of tenure the Court demanded; and a **National Tribunals Data Grid** plus performance review, giving oversight an evidentiary basis for the first time.

4. **State the residual gaps and conclude.** Appointments to the NTC itself are still made by the **Central Government**; **salaries, allowances and service conditions remain executive rule-making**, not statutory; removal grounds such as "incompetent or inefficient" are open-textured; nothing addresses **infrastructure, benches outside metros, or the nodal-ministry link** for day-to-day administration. Conclude that the Act shifts the system from **executive discretion to institutionalised process** — a substantial advance — but genuine independence will turn on whether the NTC is financed and staffed as a body **outside** the parent ministries, on the model the Court envisaged.

## PART C — ANSWERS & EXPLANATIONS

**Q1 — (a) 1 and 2 only.** The Bill repeals the 2021 Act and creates the National Tribunals Commission (1 ✓); the chairperson must have been an SC Judge or an HC Chief Justice (2 ✓). Statement 3 is wrong — the committee recommends **one name plus one waitlisted name** per vacancy, and the Centre must appoint within three months. Statement 4 is wrong — the Law Minister expressly stated that **tribunal jurisdiction is not changed**.

**Q2 — (a) 1 and 2 only.** The proposal came under **Section 25** of the RBI Act, 1934 (form, material and design of banknotes) and covers **one billion pieces each** of ₹10 and ₹20 for **field trials** (1 ✓, 2 ✓). Statement 3 is wrong — the RBI has stated that polymer notes will be issued **alongside** paper notes and there is **no proposal to replace** paper currency.

**Q3 — (a) 1, 2 and 3 only.** The invitation came at the 9th India–US Civil Space Joint Working Group in Bengaluru, 5–6 August 2026 (1 ✓); India signed the Artemis Accords on 21 June 2023 as the 27th signatory (2 ✓); NISAR, launched 30 July 2025, is the first dual-frequency L-band + S-band SAR satellite (3 ✓). Statement 4 is wrong — the Artemis Accords are a **non-binding political framework**, not a treaty.

**Q4 — (b).** Glacier flow is driven by **driving stress**, which is proportional to **ice thickness × surface slope**. Sustained thinning (from ~0.22 m yr⁻¹ in 2000–2005 to ~0.57 m yr⁻¹ by 2015–2020) lowers driving stress and therefore slows the ice — the study's central finding. Option (a) inverts the physics; (c) misstates the role of debris cover, which insulates but does not freeze ice to its bed; (d) is wrong because subglacial meltwater **lubricates** the bed and reduces drag, and would accelerate rather than lock the ice.

**Q5 — (a) 1 and 2 only.** The Dirac Medal is announced each **8 August**, Dirac's birthday (1 ✓), and the 2026 award went to **four** physicists — Deepak Dhar, Bernard Derrida, Marc Mézard and Haim Sompolinsky (2 ✓). Statement 3 is wrong: **Ashoke Sen** received it in **2014**; Dhar is the **second** scientist working in India to do so.

**Q6 — (d) 1, 2 and 3.** Articles 323A and 323B were inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976** (1 ✓). Article 323A tribunals (public service matters) may be set up **only by Parliament**; Article 323B tribunals (taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, elections, etc.) may be set up by **Parliament or State Legislatures** (2 ✓). *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) held judicial review under Arts. 226/227 to be part of the **basic structure**, so tribunal decisions remain subject to High Court scrutiny (3 ✓).

**Q7 — (a) 1, 2 and 4 only.** Section 22 gives the RBI the **sole right to issue banknotes** (1 ✓); Section 24 caps denominations at **₹10,000** (2 ✓); the **Minimum Reserve System** since 1957 requires ₹200 crore of reserves, gold at least ₹115 crore (4 ✓). Statement 3 is wrong — the **₹1 note is issued by the Government of India** and bears the **Finance Secretary's** signature, under the Coinage Act, 2011.
