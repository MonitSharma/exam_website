### 1. ISRO's semi-cryogenic engine reaches full 200-tonne power for the first time — Science & Technology

**Headline.** On **5 September 2026**, ISRO hot-tested the **Power Head Test Article (PHTA)** of its **semi-cryogenic engine (SCE-200 / SE-2000)** at **100% — the full 200-tonne thrust level** — at the **Semi-cryogenic Integrated Engine Test (SIET) facility, ISRO Propulsion Complex (IPRC), Mahendragiri, Tamil Nadu**. The test ran **35 seconds**, of which **5 seconds** were at full power. It was the **ninth** hot test in the PHTA series, and the first to reach the rated level.

**Exam angle.** This is the cleanest available hook for the propulsion question UPSC keeps circling: *what actually distinguishes India's engine families*. Three propellant classes run through the Indian fleet — **earth-storable** (UDMH + nitrogen tetroxide, as in the Vikas engine powering PSLV's second stage and LVM3's L110 core), **cryogenic** (liquid hydrogen + liquid oxygen, boiling at ~20 K and ~90 K, as in the CE-7.5 and CE-20 upper stages), and now **semi-cryogenic** (refined kerosene, ISROSENE, at ordinary temperature burning with *cryogenic* liquid oxygen — hence "semi"). The semi-cryo case rests on three things: kerosene is **denser** than liquid hydrogen, so a given tank volume carries far more propellant and boosts lift-off thrust; it is **non-toxic and storable**, unlike hydrazine derivatives; and it is far cheaper to handle than LH₂. The second examinable distinction is the **engine cycle**: SCE-200 uses **oxidiser-rich staged combustion**, where the pre-burner exhaust is routed *into* the main chamber rather than dumped overboard as in the open **gas-generator cycle** — higher efficiency, but brutal on materials because hot oxygen-rich gas attacks the turbine. The **PHTA** is not a full engine: it is the **powerhead** — pre-burner, turbopumps, control components — tested without the full thrust chamber, which is why the correct phrasing is "power level", not "flight thrust". Note also that this is an *incremental qualification ladder*, the sort of detail a statement-based question rewards.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Test date **5 September 2026**; venue **SIET, IPRC Mahendragiri, Tamil Nadu**; **9th** PHTA hot test; **first at 100% (200 t)**; duration **35 s**, with **5 s** at full power.
- Earlier rungs of the same ladder: **47% (≈94 t) → 60% (≈120 t) → 88% (≈175 t) → 100% (200 t)**.
- Engine: **SCE-200**, also designated **SE-2000**; thrust class **2,000 kN ≈ 200 tonne**; propellants **liquid oxygen + ISROSENE (refined kerosene, RP-1 class)**; cycle **oxidiser-rich staged combustion**; chamber pressure of the order of **180 bar**, feed pressures up to **~600 bar**.
- Developed by the **Liquid Propulsion Systems Centre (LPSC)**; the semi-cryogenic programme was sanctioned in **2009** at about **₹1,798 crore**.
- Intended use: replace the **L110** core stage of **LVM3** (two Vikas engines on UDMH/N₂O₄) with an **SC120** semi-cryo stage, raising payload; and serve as the booster engine for the **Next Generation Launch Vehicle (NGLV)**.
- Earlier milestones in the same programme: first **pre-burner ignition trial, May 2024**; first **PHTA hot test, March 2025**.
- Propellant map to keep straight: **PSLV** — solid (S139) / earth-storable (Vikas) / solid / earth-storable; **GSLV Mk II** — solid, Vikas, **CE-7.5** cryogenic; **LVM3** — two S200 solid boosters, L110 (2× Vikas), **C25 with CE-20** cryogenic.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Science & Technology (achievements of Indians in S&T; indigenisation of technology and developing new technology; space).

**Static anchor.** *Science & Tech notes → Space Technology chapter*, plus **NCERT Class 11 Physics Ch. 5 (Laws of Motion, rocket equation)** and **Ch. 12 (Thermodynamics)** for the cycle logic. **Nudge:** build one comparison table — *engine | propellant pair | cycle | stage it flies on | vehicle* — with rows for **Vikas, CE-7.5, CE-20, SCE-200/SE-2000**, and a fifth column for *cryogenic vs semi-cryogenic vs earth-storable*. ### 2. India's geothermal file reopens — why the first national policy treats heat, not electricity, as the prize — Environment

**Headline.** A **PIB backgrounder issued on 5 September 2026** set out where India's geothermal programme now stands, following the **National Policy on Geothermal Energy notified by MNRE on 15 September 2025** — India's first — and the **commissioning of the country's first two geothermal wells at Puga Valley, eastern Ladakh, in July 2026**.

**Exam angle.** The interesting policy fact is a *downgrade of ambition that is actually good engineering*. India's geothermal resources are overwhelmingly **low-to-medium enthalpy** — hot water in the 60–150 °C band rather than the high-pressure dry steam that Iceland, Kenya or the Philippines convert to power. So the 2025 policy deliberately prioritises **direct-use** applications — ground-source heat pumps for space heating and cooling, industrial process heat, greenhouse cultivation, cold storage, crop drying — over grid electricity, while keeping a demonstration power project alive at Puga. The second point worth an examiner's attention: geothermal is a **firm, dispatchable** renewable, running around the clock with a high capacity factor, which is precisely the gap in a grid that is filling up with variable solar and wind — so it belongs in your answer on *round-the-clock renewable power and storage*, not merely in a list of RE sources. Third, the policy is an unusually clean example of **repurposing sunk assets**: abandoned or marginal oil-and-gas wells can be converted for geothermal extraction, which is why ONGC's research arm, not a power utility, is executing Puga. The obvious constraint set — high upfront exploration risk, no dedicated regulatory or licensing regime for subsurface heat, Himalayan sites that are remote, seismically active and ecologically fragile, and induced-seismicity and thermal-water-discharge concerns — is your critique paragraph.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **National Policy on Geothermal Energy, 2025** — notified **15 September 2025** by the **Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE)**, the nodal ministry, with a **single-window** clearance mechanism; **100% FDI**; fiscal support via **viability gap funding, accelerated depreciation, GST and import-duty exemptions**; explicit provision for **repurposing abandoned oil and gas wells**.
- **Geological Survey of India** estimate: about **10,600 MW** of theoretical geothermal potential, from **381 thermally anomalous areas** (hot springs) grouped into **10 geothermal provinces** — **Himalayan, Naga-Lushai, Andaman-Nicobar, Son–Narmada–Tapi (SONATA), West Coast, Cambay Graben, Aravalli, Mahanadi, Godavari, South Indian Cratonic**.
- **Puga Valley** — **Changthang region, eastern Ladakh**, at over **14,000 ft**; India's **first two geothermal wells**, each drilled to **1,000 m**; temperatures of about **135 °C** recorded at roughly **400 m**; the second well was spudded **3 June 2026** and completed **8 July 2026**; commissioned **July 2026**.
- Executing agency: the **ONGC Energy Centre**, with the **Ladakh Administration** and the **LAHDC, Leh**. The wells are the technical basis for a proposed **1 MW demonstration geothermal power plant** — India's first.
- Puga is known for **sulphur-bearing hot springs and fumaroles**; other well-known Indian geothermal sites include **Manikaran (Himachal)**, **Tattapani (Chhattisgarh)** and **Bakreshwar (West Bengal)**.
- Why the Himalayan belt is hot: **collisional tectonics** — crustal thickening, radiogenic heat in the thickened crust and deep circulation along faults — as against volcanic-arc geothermal systems elsewhere.
- Character of the resource: **firm/dispatchable**, unlike solar and wind; hence relevant to the **round-the-clock renewable power** debate and to **Net Zero 2070**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Environment (conservation, environmental pollution) and GS3 — Infrastructure (energy); GS1 — Geography (energy resources, Himalayan geology) for the provinces map.

**Static anchor.** *Shankar IAS Environment* → **Renewable Energy / Climate Change chapter**, and *Mrunal* → **Pillar 5 (Infrastructure — Energy)**. **Nudge:** add a **10-geothermal-provinces sketch map** to the Geography energy-resources page and mark Puga, Manikaran, Tattapani and Bakreshwar on it. Then, in the RE notes, replace the flat list of renewables with a two-column split — **variable (solar, wind)** vs **firm (large hydro, biomass, geothermal, nuclear-adjacent)** — because that distinction, not the list, is what a Mains answer on grid stability actually needs.

### 3. UPI stops showing your phone number — NPCI's masking mandate takes effect — Economy

**Headline.** From **4 September 2026**, NPCI's directive to banks and UPI applications took effect: apps may **no longer display a user's full mobile number** during a transaction. Only the **last four digits** of the registered mobile number are shown to the counterparty; **account numbers and Virtual Payment Addresses (VPAs)** are subject to the same masking; and for **QR-code payments** the full mobile number is not displayed even after the transaction completes. NPCI has additionally pushed apps to make a **username-based VPA the default** at onboarding, in place of a mobile-number-based one.

**Exam angle.** Treat this as a data-protection story that happens to live inside a payments system, because that is how it will be asked. UPI's original design used the **mobile number as the discovery key** — convenient, and the reason UPI scaled, but it means every payment leaks a persistent personal identifier to a stranger. The masking mandate is a straightforward application of **data minimisation** and **purpose limitation** — you need to know *who* you are paying, not *how to call them* — and lands squarely against the backdrop of the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**. Note the *instrument*: this is regulation by **network circular from NPCI**, an industry body, rather than by statute or RBI direction — an example of the "self-regulatory organisation with quasi-regulatory reach" model that also shows up in your notes on SEBI-recognised SROs and on the proposed fintech SRO framework. For a Mains answer, the quotable line is that **India's payments stack achieved scale first and privacy second**, and this is the retrofit; the counter-argument worth acknowledging is that masking raises the friction of verifying whom you are paying, which is itself a fraud vector.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Compliance date **4 September 2026**. Masking applies to the **registered mobile number (last 4 digits visible)**, **account numbers** and **UPI IDs/VPAs**; for **QR payments** the number is hidden even post-transaction. **Username-based VPA to be the default** option.
- Stated drivers: **privacy and safety** (complaints, notably from women, about number exposure and consequent harassment), **identity-theft risk**, and alignment with the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**.
- **NPCI** — an **umbrella organisation for retail payments in India**, set up in **2008** at the initiative of the **RBI and the Indian Banks' Association**, under the **Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007**; incorporated as a **not-for-profit company** (Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013; originally Section 25 of the Companies Act, 1956).
- NPCI's stable: **UPI (2016), IMPS, RuPay, NACH, AePS, BBPS, NETC FASTag, NFS (ATM switch), Bharat BillPay**; international arm **NPCI International Payments Ltd (NIPL), 2020**.
- **UPI** architecture: an interoperable layer built over **IMPS**; the **VPA** abstracts away bank account number and IFSC; single-click two-factor authentication; supports **P2P and P2M**, **UPI Lite**, **UPI 123PAY** (feature phones), **UPI AutoPay** and **credit lines on UPI**.
- Distinction to keep sharp for Prelims: **UPI** moves *bank deposit money* and is a **payment interface**; the **Digital Rupee (e₹)** is a **central bank liability** — a CBDC — and is *not* a deposit; interoperability between the two exists at the QR level but the legal character differs.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Economy (banking, financial inclusion, digital payments) and GS3 — Science & Technology / Internal Security (data protection, cyber security); GS2 — Governance (right to privacy, regulatory bodies).

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* → **Pillar 1D (Financial Inclusion / Payment Systems)**, plus the **Puttaswamy (2017)** entry in your Fundamental Rights notes. **Nudge:** on the NPCI page, add a dated timeline — *2008 NPCI incorporated → 2016 UPI → 2020 NIPL → 2023 DPDP Act → 2026 masking mandate* — and cross-link it to the **Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile (JAM)** entry, because the same mobile number that made JAM work is the identifier now being masked. Second nudge: add a two-line box distinguishing **UPI vs e₹** — this exact distinction has already been tested.

### 4. NFHS-6 reopens the schooling question: the education gender gap is a rural problem, not a national one — Society

**Headline.** Analyses of **NFHS-6 (2023-24)** reported on **4 September 2026** find that India's male–female gap in educational attainment is dramatically wider in **rural** India than in urban India — reported at roughly **four times** the urban gap — with the break-point located not at entry into school but at the **transition to secondary education**.

**Exam angle.** The examinable insight is that the binding constraint has moved. Universal enrolment at the primary stage is largely achieved; the loss now happens at **Classes 8–10**, and the mechanism is **distance**. Secondary schools are sparser and further away than primary schools; distance interacts with **safety concerns, transport cost, household and care responsibilities, and early marriage norms** in a way that is sharply gendered — a 3 km walk costs a boy time and a girl her enrolment. That reframes the policy question from *build schools* to *build secondary schools within reach, plus transport, plus residential facilities* — which is exactly what **KGBVs, the Kasturba residential model, cycle-distribution schemes and the NEP's school-complex idea** are for. Keep the counterpoint too: NFHS-6 shows the *direction* is right — more women have ever attended school and more have completed ten years — so the honest Mains framing is **narrowing but unequal convergence**, not stagnation. Pair it with the survey's other headline finding, the simultaneous fall in stunting and rise in adult obesity and diabetes, and you have a single citable source for the "**double burden of malnutrition**" line.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **NFHS-6** — fieldwork **2023-24**; released **May 2026**; conducted by the **Ministry of Health and Family Welfare** with the **International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai** as nodal agency; roughly **6.79 lakh households** across **715 districts**.
- Education indicators: females aged **6+ who ever attended school** rose from **71.8% → 73.7%**; women with **at least 10 years of schooling** rose from **41% → 46.4%**.
- Reported rural–urban contrast: the male–female attainment gap in rural India is about **four times** the urban gap; driver identified is **secondary-school availability and distance**, compounded by safety, transport and household work. *(Reported analysis of NFHS-6 unit data, 4 September 2026 — cite as "reported", not as an official NFHS headline.)*
- Other NFHS-6 headlines worth carrying: **institutional deliveries 90.6%**; **ANC coverage 92.6% → 95.9%**; **≥4 ANC visits 58.5% → 65.2%**; **full immunisation (12–23 months) 83.8% → 87%**; **child stunting 35.5% → 29.3%**; **health-insurance coverage 60.2%** (largely Ayushman Bharat–PMJAY); rising **adult obesity and diabetes**.
- Constitutional and statutory anchors: **Article 21A** (86th Amendment Act, 2002); **RTE Act, 2009** covering ages **6–14** (i.e. **not** secondary schooling — the very stage where the gap opens); **Article 45** recast as early-childhood care and education below six; **Article 51A(k)** parental duty.
- Programme anchors: **Samagra Shiksha** (merging SSA, RMSA and Teacher Education), **Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas**, **NEP 2020** (5+3+3+4 structure; target of universal secondary participation), **PM SHRI**, **Mid-Day Meal / PM POSHAN**.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Indian Society (role of women, social empowerment); GS2 — Social Justice (issues relating to development and management of education, human resources).

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* → **Pillar 6 (Human Resource Development: Education)**, plus **NCERT Class 12 Sociology (Social Change and Development in India)**. **Nudge:** in the education notes, replace the single "GER" line with a **stage-wise ladder** — *primary → upper primary → secondary → higher secondary* — and write the male/female and rural/urban drop-off against each rung, marking the RTE Act's coverage boundary (6–14) with a bright line, since the entire argument of this item is that the gap opens *just outside* the statutory guarantee. ### C. Mains-Style Question (GS3 — Infrastructure/Energy, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **India's first National Policy on Geothermal Energy (2025) treats geothermal chiefly as a source of *heat* rather than of *electricity*. Examine the resource and economic rationale behind this approach, and the constraints on scaling geothermal energy in India.**

**Model-answer skeleton.**

1. **Frame the resource.** India's ~10,600 MW theoretical potential (GSI) across 381 thermally anomalous areas and 10 geothermal provinces is overwhelmingly **low-to-medium enthalpy** — hot water, not high-pressure dry steam. Conversion to electricity at such temperatures needs binary/ORC plants at low efficiency and high cost; the same heat used *directly* delivers far more usable energy per rupee. Hence the policy's ordering: ground-source heat pumps, industrial process heat, greenhouse cultivation, cold storage, drying — with a **1 MW demonstration plant at Puga** kept alive to prove the power pathway.
2. **State the economic and system case.** Geothermal is **firm and dispatchable**, with a high capacity factor — precisely what a grid saturating with variable solar and wind needs, and an alternative to storage. Direct use also **displaces the hardest-to-abate demand** (thermal energy in industry and cold chains) rather than adding to an already-crowded electricity segment. Repurposing **abandoned oil and gas wells** converts a decommissioning liability into an asset and lowers drilling cost.
3. **Set out the constraints.** High **upfront exploration risk** with no guarantee of a productive well; absence of a **dedicated subsurface-heat licensing and regulatory regime** (unlike petroleum); remote, high-altitude, seismically active and ecologically fragile Himalayan sites with weak evacuation infrastructure; **induced seismicity** and disposal of mineral-laden thermal water; shortage of domestic drilling and ORC technology; and financing that must price a long gestation.
4. **Conclude with the policy ask.** Public de-risking of exploration (a survey-and-drilling fund on the lines of exploration subsidies elsewhere), a statutory framework for geothermal rights and clearances, mandatory environmental baselines at Himalayan sites, and a demand-side push through building codes for ground-source cooling — so that geothermal enters India's mix as **round-the-clock clean heat** first, and clean power where the resource genuinely allows.

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**Q1 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: SCE-200/SE-2000 burns **ISROSENE (refined kerosene)** with **liquid oxygen** in an **oxidiser-rich staged combustion** cycle. Statement 3 is wrong — it is intended to replace the **L110 earth-storable core stage** (two Vikas engines) of LVM3 with an SC120 semi-cryo stage, **not** the C25 cryogenic upper stage powered by CE-20.

**Q2 — (b).** Statements 1 and 3 are correct: **MNRE** is the nodal ministry under the 2025 policy, and India's first geothermal wells were commissioned at **Puga Valley, eastern Ladakh** (July 2026). Statement 2 inverts the position — India's resources are predominantly **low-to-medium enthalpy**, which is precisely why the policy **prioritises direct-use** applications over grid electricity.

**Q3 — (b).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the NPCI directive effective **4 September 2026** limits display to the **last four digits** of the registered mobile number and extends masking to **account numbers and VPAs**, with QR payments hiding the number even after completion. Statement 3 is fabricated — there is **no requirement to route UPI transactions through the Digital Rupee**; UPI and e₹ are distinct instruments.

**Q6 — (c).** Pairs 1, 2 and 3 are correct. **Bakreshwar** is a well-known hot-spring/geothermal site in **West Bengal (Birbhum district)**, not Odisha — hence pair 4 is wrong.

**Q7 — (b).** Statements 1 and 3 are correct: **Article 21A** was inserted by the **86th Amendment Act, 2002**, and the amended **Article 45** directs early childhood care and education up to the age of six.
