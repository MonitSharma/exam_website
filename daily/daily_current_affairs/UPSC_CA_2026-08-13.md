## PART A — BRIEFING

### 1. Parliament passes the MMDR (Amendment) Bill, 2026 — the captive-mine cap goes, NMET becomes NMEDT — Economy

**Headline.** The **Rajya Sabha** cleared the **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026** on **13 August 2026**, a day after the **Lok Sabha** passed it (12 August, without debate amid Opposition protests). Introduced by Mines Minister **G. Kishan Reddy** on 10 August, it now awaits Presidential assent.

**Exam angle.**

- This is the **third** amendment to the MMDR Act, 1957 in four years (2021, 2023, 2026) and the second explicitly built around **critical and strategic minerals**. UPSC has already tested the 2023 amendment directly (Prelims 2025 — see PYQ Mirror), so the 2026 changes are squarely in the danger zone for Prelims 2027.
- The economic logic is **supply-chain de-risking**: India imports nearly all of its lithium, cobalt and nickel. The Bill's design is to let *existing* leaseholders bolt critical minerals onto leases they already hold — cutting the auction-to-production lag rather than creating new blocks.
- The Mains angle is the trade-off: freeing captive mines to sell in the open market improves capacity utilisation and price discovery, but weakens the original rationale for captive allocation (assured input security for a linked plant) and raises **displacement, forest-diversion and District Mineral Foundation** questions.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Multi-mineral leases:** a mining-lease holder may apply to the **State Government** to add other minerals to an existing lease. **No additional payment** is required for adding **critical and strategic minerals** and specified minerals — **lithium, graphite, nickel, cobalt, gold, silver**.
- **Captive mines:** the existing **50% ceiling on sale of output from captive mines** is **removed**.
- **NMET → NMEDT:** the **National Mineral Exploration Trust** is renamed the **National Mineral Exploration *and Development* Trust**, with its remit widened from exploration alone to **mineral and mine development**.
- **Deep-seated minerals** (below **200 metres**): one-time lease-area expansion of up to **30% for composite licences** and **10% for mining leases**.
- **Mineral dumps:** States may permit sale of mineral dumps accumulated within leased areas up to a cut-off date specified by the Centre.
- Anchor facts to carry alongside: India has identified **30 critical minerals**; joined the **Minerals Security Partnership** in **2023**; the **National Critical Mineral Mission** is the umbrella programme.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Economy (Infrastructure & Resources) *and* GS3 — Internal/Economic Security (supply chains).

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* **Pillar 4A — Infrastructure / Mining & Minerals**, plus your **MMDR Act evolution** page. **Update:** convert that page into a four-row table — **1957 base → 2021 (captive sale up to 50%, seamless transfer) → 2023 (Part D critical minerals, Centre-auctioned; exploration licence) → 2026 (multi-mineral leases, 50% cap removed, NMET→NMEDT, 200 m deep-seated relief)** — and cross-link to the **National Critical Mineral Mission** and **GSI** entries.

### 2. Supreme Court: environmental compensation cannot be left to discretion — MoEF&CC ordered to frame guidelines under the new Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026 — Environment

**Headline.** In ***Amravati Municipal Corporation v. Ganesh Dadarao Anasane* (2026 INSC 796)**, a Bench of **Justices P.S. Narasimha and Alok Aradhe** laid down a structured set of principles for **environmental compensation** and directed the **MoEF&CC** to frame guidelines under **Rule 17(2) of the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026**. (Judgment 5 August 2026; carried in the environment court digests through this week. Next listed **29 September 2026**.)

**Exam angle.**

- Two things are new at once and both are examinable: the **Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026** have replaced the 2016 Rules, and the Court has read **Rule 17 as the statutory incorporation of the polluter pays principle** for waste violations.
- The doctrinal payload is the cleanest recent statement of the **compensation-vs-penalty distinction**: compensation is **restitutionary**, is **additional to** any penalty or fine, and continues until the damage is **reversed**. That single line is worth memorising verbatim for GS3.
- The Court also drew an institutional line — "hand-holding" for regulators **without micromanaging environmental governance** — which is a ready-made Mains argument on judicial overreach in environmental adjudication.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Cause title / citation:** *Amravati Municipal Corporation v. Ganesh Dadarao Anasane*, **2026 INSC 796**. Bench: **P.S. Narasimha + Alok Aradhe**.
- **Rule 17(2), SWM Rules 2026** — guidelines for imposition and collection of environmental compensation. **Rule 18 r/w Rule 38(3)** — the **Central Implementation Committee** (constituted by MoEF&CC office memorandum). **Rule 16** — online centralised portal.
- **Principles laid down:** compensation is *restitutionary*, paid **in addition to** penalty; liability runs **until damage is reversed**; covers **tangible and intangible** damage; can attach even where harm has **not yet occurred** but the activity has potential to cause harm; must be **proportional** with a **rational nexus** to the pollution; all courts/tribunals must **record reasons**, factors, valuation standard and formula used.
- **Precedent chain the Court surveyed:** *M.C. Mehta v. UoI* (1987, absolute liability), *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996, polluter pays + precautionary principle into Indian law), *M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath* (2000, public trust), *Research Foundation for Science* (2005), *Sterlite Industries* (2013).
- **Comparator regime:** **Rule 18 of the Plastic Waste Management Rules** — EC levied on polluter pays per **CPCB**-notified graded formula (per-tonne, repeat violation, waste category, type of polluter). Also **Environment Protection (Manner of Holding Inquiry and Imposition of Penalty) Rules, 2024**.

**Quotable Mains line.** *"It is high time that the determination and imposition of environmental compensation is not left to the discretion of regulatory bodies, tribunals, or Courts."*

**Notebook.** GS3 — Environment (Pollution & Waste Management); GS2 — Polity (judicial review of regulatory bodies).

**Static anchor.** *Shankar IAS Environment* — **"Solid Waste Management"** and **"Environmental Legislations / Principles of Environmental Law"** chapters. **Update:** overwrite every "SWM Rules, **2016**" reference with **2016 → superseded by SWM Rules, 2026**, add a Rule 16/17/18 mini-map, and cross-link the polluter-pays box to *Vellore Citizens* (1996) and to the **EP (Inquiry & Penalty) Rules, 2024** so principle, statute and rule sit on one page.

### 3. PLFS Quarterly Bulletin (April–June 2026): unemployment up to 5.4%, LFPR slips to 54.6% — Reports & Indices

**Headline.** The **National Statistical Office (NSO), MoSPI** released the **Periodic Labour Force Survey Quarterly Bulletin for April–June 2026** on **10 August 2026**. Unemployment for persons **aged 15+** rose to **5.4%** from **5.0%** in January–March 2026; LFPR fell to **54.6%**.

**Exam angle.**

- The interesting fact is not the headline UR but the **composition**: the UR rose *while* the LFPR *fell*. Falling participation usually flatters the unemployment rate, so a simultaneous rise in both distress indicators is the analytically loaded point.
- The entire deterioration is **rural**: rural LFPR dropped **58.2% → 56.9%** and rural UR rose **4.3% → 4.8%**, while urban LFPR was **flat at 50.2%**. Read this against the **VB-G RAM G** first-month rural person-day contraction already in your notes (10 August briefing) — that is your cross-link.
- Prelims trap territory: **UR is a share of the labour force, not of the population**; **WPR is a share of the population**. And PLFS reports both **Usual Status (ps+ss)** and **Current Weekly Status (CWS)** — quarterly bulletins are **CWS**.

**Facts to memorise (all persons aged 15+, CWS, April–June 2026).**

| Indicator | Apr–Jun 2026 | Jan–Mar 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Unemployment Rate | **5.4%** | 5.0% |
| LFPR | **54.6%** | 55.5% |
| Worker Population Ratio | **51.7%** | 52.8% |
| Rural LFPR | **56.9%** | 58.2% |
| Urban LFPR | **50.2%** | 50.2% |
| Rural UR | **4.8%** | 4.3% |
| Urban UR | **6.7%** | — |

- PLFS launched **2017** (first annual report for July 2017–June 2018, released May 2019); conducted by **NSO under MoSPI**; since 2025 it also publishes a **monthly** bulletin.
- Context to pair it with: **SBI Research (Ecowrap)** projected **~8% Q1 FY27 GDP growth** against the **RBI's 7%**, using a nowcasting model on **54 high-frequency indicators**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Economy (Employment, Growth & Development); Reports & Indices ledger.

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* **Pillar 4B — Human Resource Development / Employment**. **Update:** add a running PLFS quarterly strip (Jan–Mar 2026 → Apr–Jun 2026) to the same page that already holds **PM-VBRY** and **VB-G RAM G**, and pin the definition box — **LFPR = (employed + unemployed)/population; WPR = employed/population; UR = unemployed/labour force** — at the top so no figure is ever read against the wrong denominator.

### 4. Vande Mataram gets statutory protection — President assents to the Prevention of Insults to National Honour (Amendment) Act, 2026 — Polity & Governance

***What is new since 11 August:*** the 11 August note recorded that the National Song is **absent** from both **Article 51A(a)** and the **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971**. That second half is now **out of date** — **President Droupadi Murmu has given assent** to the 2026 amendment, which inserts Vande Mataram into **Section 3** of the 1971 Act. Nothing changes constitutionally; everything changes statutorily.

**Exam angle.**

- The examinable asymmetry is now **three-tiered**, and this is exactly the kind of distinction UPSC builds a statement-question around: the National Song is (a) **still absent from Article 51A(a)**, which names only the National Flag and the National Anthem; (b) **still absent from the Constitution generally** — its status rests on **Rajendra Prasad's Constituent Assembly statement of 24 January 1950**, which gave it *equal honour* with the Anthem; but (c) **now present in the 1971 Act**.
- A second trap: the Bill was **introduced in the Rajya Sabha**, not the Lok Sabha. Perfectly valid for an ordinary bill — but a common wrong assumption.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Act amended:** Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, **1971**; the amendment is to **Section 3** (which had covered only the **National Anthem**).
- **Offence created:** intentionally **preventing the singing** of the National Song, or **causing disturbance** to any assembly engaged in singing it.
- **Punishment (as under the existing Section 3):** imprisonment up to **3 years**, or fine, or both; repeat offenders face a **minimum of 1 year**.
- **Legislative track:** introduced in **Rajya Sabha, 24 July 2026** → **Rajya Sabha passed, 29 July 2026** → **Lok Sabha passed by voice vote, 30 July 2026** → **Presidential assent, August 2026**.
- Related but separate: the 1971 Act's **Section 2** covers insult to the **National Flag and the Constitution**; **Article 111** governs assent to ordinary bills.

**Notebook.** GS2 — Polity (Fundamental Duties, National Symbols, legislative procedure).

**Static anchor.** *Laxmikanth* — **"Fundamental Duties"** and the **"National Symbols" / Appendix** section, plus the **"Parliament — Legislative Procedure"** chapter. **Update:** correct your National Song box to read **"absent from Art. 51A(a) and from the Constitution; present in the 1971 Act from 2026"**, and cross-link to the 11 August entry so the pre- and post-amendment positions sit side by side rather than contradicting each other.

### 5. Skyroot signs its first commercial launch contract — three Vikram missions for HEX20 — Science & Technology

**Headline.** **Skyroot Aerospace** (Hyderabad) has signed a **Multi-Launch Agreement** with Indian space-tech startup **HEX20** for **three dedicated Vikram-series missions**, its **first launch contract** after the successful **Vikram-1** orbital flight. Launches begin in **Q4 2027**.

**Exam angle.**

- The significance is commercial, not technical: an Indian private launch vehicle has now converted a demonstrated capability into a **contracted order book** — the step at which "space reform" stops being policy and starts being an industry. Pair it with **IN-SPACe** as the single-window authoriser and **NSIL** as ISRO's commercial arm.
- The **rideshare** model in the contract is the same economics that made SpaceX's Transporter missions dominant — cost-sharing across multiple smallsat operators. Vietnam's **VinSpace** signed with SpaceX for a 2027 Transporter mission the same week, which is your ready-made contrast: **build indigenous launch capacity vs buy foreign rideshare**.
- Careful with the **Vikram** name — UPSC loves collisions. Distinguish: **Vikram-S** (2022 suborbital demonstrator, Mission *Prarambh*) → **Vikram-1** (2026 orbital, Mission *Aagaman*) → **Vikram 1601 / Vikram 3201** (16-bit and 32-bit space-grade microprocessors from **SCL Chandigarh + VSSC**) → **Vikram lander** (Chandrayaan). Four different things.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Parties:** Skyroot Aerospace Pvt Ltd (Hyderabad) and **HEX20**; three dedicated Vikram-series missions; launches from **Q4 2027**.
- **Purpose:** HEX20's **Earth-observation constellation**, in-orbit payload demonstration, plus **rideshare capacity** for third-party spacecraft.
- **Vikram-1 / Mission *Aagaman* (July 2026):** India's **first privately developed orbital-class rocket** to reach orbit on its maiden flight.
- **Vikram-S / Mission *Prarambh* (2022):** India's first privately built **suborbital** rocket.
- Ecosystem anchors: **Indian Space Policy 2023**, **IN-SPACe** (single-window authorisation, Ahmedabad), **NSIL** (commercial arm), **100% FDI** in specified space segments.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Science & Technology (Space; Indigenisation & Private Sector).

**Static anchor.** Sci-Tech notes → **"Space Sector Reforms"** module. **Update:** add an **"Indian private launch providers"** row (Skyroot, Agnikul, plus the engine-development entrants) with each firm's demonstrated milestone and first commercial contract, and cross-link to the 28 July entry on **Vikram 3201** so the name-collision trap is disarmed in one place.

### C. Mains-Style Question

**GS3 — Environment (250 words, 15 marks)**

*"Enforceable rules are only the starting point of environmental governance."* **Examine** this proposition in the light of recent judicial directions on environmental compensation for solid waste management violations in India.

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Frame the gap.** India does not lack environmental law — it lacks predictable enforcement. The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026 supersede the 2016 Rules, yet compliance remained weak because Rule 17 compensation was applied at the discretion of regulators, tribunals and courts, producing inconsistent and unreasoned awards. State the proposition's core: rule-making is a necessary but insufficient condition.
2. **What the Court supplied.** Structure, not micromanagement — compensation is **restitutionary and additional to penalty**; liability persists **until damage is reversed**; covers **tangible and intangible** harm and **potential** harm; must be **proportional with rational nexus**; and every award must carry **recorded reasons, factors, valuation standard and formula**. Institutional scaffolding: the **Central Implementation Committee** (Rule 18 r/w 38(3)) and the centralised portal (Rule 16).
3. **The doctrinal lineage and the transferable template.** Locate this in *M.C. Mehta* (1987), *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) and *Sterlite* (2013); note the working model already in **Rule 18 of the Plastic Waste Management Rules** with CPCB's graded per-tonne formula, and the **EP (Inquiry & Penalty) Rules, 2024**. Argue that a common, formula-driven compensation architecture across waste streams is the real deliverable.
4. **Residual constraints and the balanced close.** Municipal capacity and finance, the awkwardness of urban local bodies as both polluter and enforcer, legacy dumpsites, and the informal recycling economy. Conclude with the Court's own institutional caution — judicial review should **hand-hold regulators into competence, not substitute for them** — and the "polluter pays" principle as a pricing mechanism rather than a punishment.
