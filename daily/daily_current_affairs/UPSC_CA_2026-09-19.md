### 1. Six Chief Ministers sign the Kishau agreement — a 232.6-metre dam on the Tons, and a dispute settled without a tribunal — Polity & Governance

**The headline.** On **15 September 2026** the Chief Ministers of **Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan, Delhi and Haryana** signed the agreement on the long-pending **Kishau Multipurpose Project** in the upper Yamuna basin — a project surveyed and argued over for decades, now cleared of its political and cost-sharing obstacle but not of its clearances.

**The exam angle.** This is the cleanest available illustration of a theme UPSC returns to: India increasingly settles inter-State river water disputes by **negotiated agreement rather than tribunal adjudication**. Water is a State subject (Entry 17, State List), but the Union holds Entry 56 of the Union List over inter-State rivers, and Article 262 lets Parliament provide for adjudication *and* oust the courts. A signed agreement short-circuits all of it. The second angle is the *quality of the record*: environmental researchers have flagged unexplained discrepancies in the project's own official documents, which is a rare, citable example for a Mains answer on environmental governance.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **River:** the **Tons**, the **largest tributary of the Yamuna**, flowing along the **Uttarakhand–Himachal Pradesh** border. The Yamuna rises at **Yamunotri** and joins the Ganga at Prayagraj.
- **Dam:** a **concrete gravity dam**, **232.6 m** — among India's tallest. (**Tehri**, ~260 m, remains the tallest.)
- **Design figures:** storage **1,562 MCM**; irrigation **97,000 hectares**; **1,476 million units** of hydropower.
- **Funding:** declared a **national project** — the Centre bears about **90%** of the cost, the States the remaining **10%**. Cost estimated in media reports at **₹15,000–15,624 crore** (the MHA statement gave no figure).
- **The swap worth remembering:** **Himachal Pradesh's water share goes to Delhi and Rajasthan**; in return those two bear the cost of Himachal's share of the *electricity* component.
- **Parent instrument:** the **12 May 1994 Upper Yamuna agreement**, which provided that a *separate agreement* would be signed for each storage project. An **in-principle** agreement among the six States was reached on **16 June 2026**.
- **Government claim:** about **25% of the Yamuna's water** would become available as **environmental flow**; the MHA describes this as the **tenth** water dispute taken up for resolution in the present tenure.
- **The discrepancies (excellent Mains material):** installed capacity listed as **422 MW** in the Environment Ministry's **47th Expert Appraisal Committee** document (January 2026) but **660 MW** in the 2018 HPPCL record and the **29th EAC** agenda (June 2022). Submergence is constant at **2,950 ha**, but the 2018 record showed **2,438 ha of forest** while the 2026 EAC document lists only **689 ha** — the status of the missing **1,749 ha** is unexplained.
- **Displacement:** **5,498 people from 701 families**, a figure derived from a **2014 field study and a 2018 report**, not re-verified (Himdhara Environmental Research and Action Collective).
- **The objection:** **SANDRP** (Himanshu Thakkar) argues the Himalaya is seismically unstable and that a dam of this height carries disproportionate risk; he calls the Yamuna-cleaning claim misleading, since regulating flow *dilutes* pollution rather than removing it.
- **Still pending:** Union Cabinet approval; **forest land diversion** under the **Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam** (the Forest Conservation Act, 1980 as amended in 2023); **environmental clearance** under the **EIA Notification, 2006**; and a rehabilitation plan under the **LARR Act, 2013**.

**Destination notebook:** GS2 — Polity & Governance (federalism, inter-State relations); cross-tag GS3 — Environment & Infrastructure.

**Static anchor:** **Laxmikanth — "Inter-State Relations" chapter**, the section on Inter-State Water Disputes (Article 262, ISRWD Act 1956, River Boards Act 1956). *Nudge:* on that page, add a margin line — "**2026: Kishau — six-State negotiated agreement, no tribunal**" — next to your list of tribunals, and cross-link to the **Yamuna drainage system** page in your NCERT/GC Leong physical geography notes (Tons as largest Yamuna tributary; Lakhwar–Vyasi–Dakpathar–Asan cascade downstream).

### 2. India's first soil carbon payments: ₹2.9 crore to 2,550 farmers, for carbon held in their own fields — Agriculture

**The headline.** At **Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana on 17 September 2026**, over **₹2.9 crore** was transferred by DBT to **2,550 smallholder farmers in Punjab and Haryana** for verified greenhouse-gas reductions and soil-carbon gains — the first reported instance in India of farmers being paid for carbon stored in their soil.

**The exam angle.** This sits at the junction of three syllabus areas — agriculture, climate finance and environmental regulation — and it is the kind of "first" that Prelims likes to test through the *mechanism*, not the number: which standard, which practice, which market. The Mains value is sharper still, because the programme fails two of the three classical tests for a carbon credit (permanence and, arguably, additionality) and exposes a regulatory gap: **agriculture is not an obligated sector under India's own Carbon Credit Trading Scheme**, so these credits have no domestic compliance buyer and must be sold abroad.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **Programme:** **'Aadi'**, run by **Grow Indigo** (a private agri-venture) with technical guidance from **ICAR**; launched **2019**. DBT initiated by **Dr M. L. Jat**, Secretary, **DARE** and Director General, ICAR.
- **Credited practices, 2019–2022:** **Direct Seeded Rice (DSR)**, **reduced tillage**, and **crop-residue management**.
- **Methodology:** **Verra VM0042** — the *improved agricultural land management* methodology under the **Verified Carbon Standard**; credits issued only after independent third-party verification.
- **Footprint:** more than **2 million acres** and over **1,00,000 farmers** across **seven States**. **First issuance:** about **30,000 acres** and more than **50,000 carbon credits**.
- **Payment:** average about **₹11,478** per farmer, most in the **₹3,000–15,000** band. Farmers chose between an **assured upfront payment** and **75% of net carbon revenue** after sale; the aggregator advanced the money from its own funds.
- **Co-benefits claimed:** about **45 billion litres** of irrigation water saved; over **2 lakh tonnes** of crop residue kept out of field fires; roughly **1,000 tonnes of PM2.5** avoided.
- **Context figure:** Punjab recorded **5,114 farm-fire incidents** in the **2025 paddy season** — a **93% fall from 2021**.
- **The three tests every credit must pass:** **additionality**, **permanence**, and absence of **leakage**. **MRV** — Measurement, Reporting and Verification — is what makes a credit credible.
- **The critical points:** soil carbon is **reversible** (one season of deep ploughing undoes years of accumulation); **additionality is contested in Punjab**, where DSR and residue management are already pushed by State subsidy, NGT orders and the **Commission for Air Quality Management**; transaction costs are largely fixed, so smallholders depend on an aggregator; and India has **no settled legal position on who owns soil carbon** where the cultivator is a tenant.
- **Regulatory frame:** **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS), 2023**, notified under the **Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022**, administered with the **Bureau of Energy Efficiency** — it succeeds the **PAT** and **REC** schemes. Distinguish from the **Green Credit Programme (2023)** of the Environment Ministry, which rewards actions like plantation and water conservation and is *not* a carbon instrument.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Agriculture; cross-tag GS3 — Environment (carbon markets, climate finance).

**Static anchor:** **Shankar IAS — Climate Change chapter**, the carbon-market section (Kyoto mechanisms → Paris Article 6 → CCTS 2023). *Nudge:* under CCTS, write a one-line note — "**offset mechanism: agriculture NOT an obligated sector; Sept 2026 soil-carbon credits sold on the voluntary market (Verra VM0042)**" — and cross-link to the DSR/residue-burning entry in your **Mrunal Agriculture pillar** notes.

### 3. Human noise is pushing the Himalayan monal to call at a higher pitch — Environment

**The headline.** Researchers at **Hemvati Nandan Bahuguna Garhwal University** have found that the **Himalayan monal** (*Lophophorus impejanus*) calls at **higher frequencies, with greater amplitude and longer duration**, in areas made noisier by roads, tourism and pilgrim traffic — evidence of **adaptive vocal plasticity**, with consequences for breeding that are suspected but not yet established.

**The exam angle.** Two things make this worth a card. First, the **species** — a State bird, a neighbouring country's national bird, and a Himalayan pheasant — is exactly the profile Prelims uses for a species question. Second, the **concept**: *acoustic masking* is a form of pollution India's law does not address inside protected areas at all. The Noise Pollution Rules were written for human health in settled areas, with categories like "silence zone"; there is no equivalent standard for a wildlife sanctuary or a pilgrimage route. That gap is the answer-worthy point.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **The bird:** *Lophophorus impejanus*, a **pheasant** (ground-dwelling landfowl). **State bird of Uttarakhand**; **national bird of Nepal**, where it is the **danphe**. Ranges across the Himalaya — **Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim**. **Not threatened** on the IUCN Red List (Least Concern), but **protected under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972**.
- **The study:** Rajeev Lochan, Dinesh Kumar Sharma and Anand Kumar, HNB Garhwal University, using **passive acoustic monitoring**.
- **The comparison:** two valleys inside the **Kedarnath Wildlife (Musk Deer) Sanctuary**, Uttarakhand — **Madmaheshwar Valley** (little human activity, **no road**) against **Chopta Valley** (road, tourists, heavy pilgrim footfall on the route to **Tungnath** and **Chandrashila**).
- **The finding:** higher-frequency calls with greater amplitude in the noisy valley, the difference strongest at the **upper end** of the call range; call duration also increased.
- **The concept — acoustic masking:** traffic and crowd noise is concentrated at **low frequencies**, so shifting a call **upward** escapes the masking band. The related idea is the **Lombard effect** — raising vocal effort in noise, seen in animals and humans alike. The field is **bioacoustics**, now a low-cost biodiversity-monitoring tool.
- **The regulatory gap:** noise is regulated under the **Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000**, framed under the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, with ambient standards by area category — industrial, commercial, residential and **silence zone**. None of these categories contemplates a protected area.
- **The link to carry forward:** **carrying capacity** of Himalayan pilgrimage and tourism circuits — the same argument that runs through Chardham, Joshimath and the Char Dham road project.

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Environment (biodiversity, pollution); cross-tag GS1 — Geography (Himalayan tourism pressure).

**Static anchor:** **Shankar IAS — "Pollution" chapter**, the noise-pollution section, and the **Protected Area Network / Wildlife Conservation** chapter. *Nudge:* against the Noise Rules table of ambient standards, add "**gap: no noise standard for protected areas / pilgrimage routes — Himalayan monal study, Kedarnath WLS, Sept 2026**", and add **Kedarnath WLS (Musk Deer Sanctuary), Uttarakhand — Chopta, Madmaheshwar** to your protected-areas map list.

### 4. The Centre draws a line around stem cell therapy — approved indications only, and autism goes back to clinical trials — Science & Technology

**The headline.** The **Union Health Ministry**, by an advisory dated **16 September 2026** addressed to all States and UTs that have adopted the **Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010**, has reiterated that stem cell therapy may be offered as **standard clinical care only for indications on a list approved by the Ministry** — and that its therapeutic use in **Autism Spectrum Disorder** is restricted to **duly approved clinical trials**.

**The exam angle.** This is a governance-of-science item, and those are consistently under-prepared. The substance is that in India — apart from **haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** for defined blood disorders — stem cell therapies remain **investigational**, yet a commercial market in unproven treatments has grown around desperate families. The advisory follows a **Supreme Court judgment of 30 January 2026** and an **NMC advisory of 5 September 2026** which classifies unauthorised administration, prescription, promotion or advertisement of such therapy as **professional misconduct**. Note the regulatory architecture: guidelines from a *research* body, enforcement through a *professional* regulator and a *drugs* regulator, and the delivery net cast through a *clinical establishments* statute that not every State has adopted.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **The advisory:** MoHFW, **16 September 2026**, to States/UTs that have adopted the **Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010**.
- **The rule restated:** stem cell therapy as standard care **only for MoHFW-approved indications**; for **Autism Spectrum Disorder**, use of **any type of stem cell** is confined to approved clinical trials.
- **The governing guidelines:** the **National Guidelines for Stem Cell Research, 2017**, issued **jointly by the ICMR and the Department of Biotechnology (DBT)**.
- **The professional regulator:** the **National Medical Commission**, advisory of **5 September 2026** — offering, prescribing, promoting or advertising stem cell therapy beyond approved indications amounts to **professional misconduct**.
- **The drugs regulator:** **CDSCO**, under the **New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019**, framed under the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**. Stem cell derived products are regulated as new drugs.
- **The trigger:** a **Supreme Court judgment dated 30 January 2026**.
- **The one accepted use:** **haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** (bone marrow / peripheral blood / cord blood) for defined haematological conditions. Everything else is investigational.
- **Terms to have straight:** **totipotent** → **pluripotent** (embryonic stem cells; **iPSCs** — induced pluripotent stem cells, reprogrammed adult cells) → **multipotent** (adult/somatic, e.g. haematopoietic, mesenchymal). **Autologous** (from the patient) versus **allogeneic** (from a donor).

**Destination notebook:** GS3 — Science & Technology (biotechnology, health); cross-tag GS2 — Governance (regulation, health).

**Static anchor:** **Shankar IAS / your Sci-Tech notes — Biotechnology chapter**, the stem cells and regenerative medicine section. *Nudge:* under the stem-cell types table, add the regulatory stack in one line — "**ICMR–DBT National Guidelines for Stem Cell Research 2017 · CDSCO under NDCT Rules 2019 · NMC (professional misconduct) · Clinical Establishments Act 2010 for enforcement; only HSCT is standard care**" — and cross-link to your GS2 health-governance page on medical regulation.

### 5. *Gondhal* goes to the Oscars — and a Maharashtrian ritual performance becomes the year's Art & Culture card — Art & Culture

**The headline.** On **18 September 2026** the **Film Federation of India** selected the Marathi film ***Gondhal***, written and directed by **Santosh Davakhar**, as India's official entry for **Best International Feature Film** at the **99th Academy Awards (Oscars 2027)** — chosen from **33 films** by a **14-member selection committee** chaired by filmmaker **P. Sheshadri**.

**The exam angle.** Ignore the award; learn the **folk form**. *Gondhal* is a ritual devotional performance of Maharashtra, and UPSC has a settled habit of asking which State a named folk or tribal performance belongs to. The film is built around a night-long Gondhal, which is why the form is suddenly in every newspaper — a free opportunity to fix a card that would otherwise sit unlearned in the Art & Culture backlog.

**Key facts to memorise.**

- **Gondhal** is a **ritual devotional folk performance of Maharashtra** (and parts of northern Karnataka), performed by hereditary performers called **gondhalis**, combining **invocation, devotional song, storytelling, rhythmic movement and firelight**.
- **Deities invoked:** the goddesses **Renuka** (Mahur) and **Tulja Bhavani** (Tuljapur), and in parts of the region **Khandoba**. It is performed at **weddings and other auspicious family occasions**, often running **through the night**.
- **Instruments:** the **sambal** — twin wooden drums slung at the waist and struck with two sticks, one with a curved end — and the **tuntune**, a single-string plucked drone; plus metal idiophones and a **torch or lamp**, which gives the form its visual signature.
- **The film:** Marathi, released **November 2025**; uses the Gondhal performance as the frame for a woman's attempt to leave a stifling relationship.
- **Cinema facts worth one line each:** *Gondhal* is the **fourth Marathi film** chosen as India's entry — after ***Shwaas* (2005)**, ***Harishchandrachi Factory* (2009)** and ***Court* (2015)**. India has **never won** this category; only **three Indian films have been nominated** — ***Mother India*** (1957), ***Salaam Bombay!*** (1988) and ***Lagaan*** (2001). The **99th Academy Awards** fall in **March 2027**.
- **Place it among Maharashtra's folk forms:** **Tamasha** (folk theatre, incorporating **Lavani**), **Powada** (heroic ballad sung by *shahirs*, above all on Shivaji), **Bharud** (allegorical devotional song associated with **Eknath**), **Dashavatara** (folk theatre of the **Konkan** and Goa), **Keertan**, **Jagran**.

**Destination notebook:** GS1 — Art & Culture (folk and performing arts).

**Static anchor:** **Nitin Singhania — "Performing Arts: Folk Dance / Folk Theatre" chapter**, the Maharashtra entry. *Nudge:* add **Gondhal** beside Tamasha, Lavani, Powada, Bharud and Dashavatara in your State-wise folk-forms table, with the two instruments (**sambal, tuntune**) and the two goddesses (**Renuka, Tulja Bhavani**) — that pairing is what a Prelims question would hang on.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS3 — Environment / Agriculture, 250 words, 15 marks)

> India's first farm-level soil carbon payments have linked smallholder agricultural practice to the global voluntary carbon market. **Critically examine** whether carbon finance can become a dependable income stream for Indian smallholders, and identify the institutional and regulatory preconditions this would require.

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Set the fact and the mechanism (intro, ~40 words).** September 2026: ₹2.9 crore to 2,550 farmers in Punjab and Haryana for practices adopted 2019–2022, verified under Verra VM0042 on the voluntary market. State the chain in one sentence — practice → measurement → third-party verification → issuance → payment — and note the **four-year lag** between practice and payment as the structural feature everything else follows from.

2. **The case for (~60 words).** A genuinely additional income stream that pays for *ecosystem services* rather than output, so it does not distort the MSP–procurement system. Strong **co-benefits**: ~45 billion litres of water saved, >2 lakh tonnes of residue diverted from burning, ~1,000 tonnes of PM2.5 avoided — i.e. the same rupee buys soil health, groundwater and Delhi's winter air. Aligns with the National Mission on Sustainable Agriculture and India's NDC.

3. **The case against — four fault-lines (~90 words).** **Permanence:** soil carbon is reversible; one season of deep ploughing releases years of accumulation, and no contract binds a smallholder for two decades. **Additionality:** DSR and residue management are already mandated or subsidised by the State, NGT and CAQM — paying for legally-nudged behaviour is methodologically weak. **Transaction costs and dependence:** MRV costs are largely fixed, so a two-hectare farmer cannot participate except through an aggregator whose margin is undisclosed; and the "75% of net revenue" option transfers **price risk** to the farmer. **Ownership:** India has no settled law on who owns soil carbon where the cultivator is a tenant or sharecropper.

4. **Preconditions and way forward (~60 words).** (i) A statutory position on **carbon rights in agricultural land**, tied to records of rights and to tenancy. (ii) **Bring agriculture into the CCTS offset mechanism** so credits have a domestic buyer and are not exposed solely to a volatile voluntary market. (iii) A **public, low-cost MRV backbone** — ICAR protocols, remote sensing, a national soil-carbon baseline — to collapse transaction costs. (iv) **Aggregator regulation**: disclosure of margins, model contracts, grievance redress. (v) Clarity under **Paris Article 6** on whose NDC the reduction counts towards. Conclude: carbon finance is a credible **supplement** to farm income and a poor substitute for it; its value lies in financing a transition, not in replacing crop revenue.

### D. PYQ Mirror — how UPSC has asked these themes before

**Theme 1 — Inter-State river water disputes (Item 1).**
**UPSC CSE Mains 2013, GS Paper II** *(verified)*: *"Constitutional mechanisms to resolve the inter-state water disputes have failed to address and solve the problems. Is the failure due to structural or process inadequacy or both? Discuss."*
**Read the trend:** UPSC asks about the *mechanism*, not the individual dispute. Kishau is therefore most useful to you as **evidence of a shift away from that failing mechanism** — a negotiated six-State agreement that never went to a tribunal at all. Keep it as a one-line example in that answer, not as a topic in itself.

**Theme 2 — Carbon credits and carbon markets (Item 2).**
**UPSC CSE Prelims 2011** *(verified)*: *"Regarding 'carbon credits', which one of the following statements is NOT correct?"* — the incorrect option being the claim that carbon credits are **traded at a price fixed from time to time by UNEP**. The other three (origin ratified in conjunction with the **Kyoto Protocol**; awarded for reductions below an emission quota; aim of limiting the increase in CO₂ emissions) are correct.
**Read the trend:** the examiner tests **who sets the price and under what instrument**. Update that card from Kyoto to the present stack — **Paris Article 6 → CCTS 2023 (compliance) versus Verra/Gold Standard (voluntary)** — because that is where a 2027 version of this question would sit.

**Theme 3 — Stem cells (Item 4).**
**UPSC CSE Prelims 2012** *(approximate wording — flagged; verify against the original paper)*: a question of the form *"With reference to 'stem cells', frequently in the news, which of the following statements is/are correct?"*
**Read the trend:** Prelims has tested stem cells on **biology** (sources, potency, therapeutic status). The 2026 advisory adds a **regulatory** layer that has not yet been asked — ICMR–DBT guidelines, CDSCO, NMC — which is exactly the kind of overlay UPSC has been adding to science questions recently. Prepare both halves.
