### 1. The FCRA Amendment Bill gets its 31-member JPC — and the fight is now about property, not funding — Polity & Governance

**Headline.** On **3 September 2026** the Lok Sabha Secretariat notified the **31-member Joint Parliamentary Committee** to examine the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026**, with BJP MP **Sanjay Jaiswal** (Bettiah) as Chairperson. The Bill had been referred to a JPC during the Monsoon Session (**12–13 August 2026**) after sustained Opposition objection; the committee must report by the **last day of the first week of the Winter Session** (Nov–Dec 2026).

**Exam angle.** Two separate examinable things are happening, and you should not blur them. The **first** is procedural: this is a live, dated example of the *ad hoc* committee route — a Bill sent not to a Department-Related Standing Committee but to a specially constituted joint committee of both Houses, with members nominated by the Speaker (21 from Lok Sabha) and the Chairman (10 from Rajya Sabha). That is exactly the distinction UPSC likes to test between standing and ad hoc committees. The **second** is substantive and more interesting: the 2026 Bill shifts FCRA regulation from *controlling the flow of money* to *controlling the assets that money built*. A proposed new **Chapter IIIA** creates a Centre-appointed **"designated authority"** in which foreign contribution — and everything created out of it — provisionally vests when a certificate is cancelled, surrendered, or simply **ceases** (including where renewal was refused, not applied for, or not granted before expiry). A building only *partly* financed with foreign money vests in full, and the organisation must separately apply to recover the domestically funded share. The appeal to a district judge lies only against what the authority subsequently does with the property, **not** against the refusal to renew, and there is no pre-decisional hearing. That is the constitutional pressure point: **Article 300A** (no deprivation of property save by authority of law) read with **Article 14** (reasoned, non-arbitrary discretion) and **Article 19(1)(c)** (right to form associations). The Mains line to keep: *the objection is not to regulation of foreign funding, which no serious party disputes, but to a design that visits the consequences of proven wrongdoing on a mere lapse of a certificate.*

**Facts to memorise.**

- JPC notified **3 September 2026**; **31 members** = **21 Lok Sabha** (nominated by the Speaker) + **10 Rajya Sabha** (nominated by the Chairman); Chairperson **Sanjay Jaiswal**. Report due by the **first week of the Winter Session 2026**.
- Parent statute: **FCRA, 2010**, which replaced the **FCRA, 1976**; administered by the **Ministry of Home Affairs (Foreigners Division)**. Registration is valid **five years** and must be renewed.
- **FCRA (Amendment) Act, 2020** — the previous tightening: barred **sub-granting** of foreign funds to any other person (even another FCRA-registered body); cut the administrative-expenses cap from **50% → 20%**; mandated a designated **FCRA account at SBI, New Delhi Main Branch**.
- What the **2026 Bill** adds: a **"designated authority"** (new Chapter IIIA) with **automatic provisional vesting** of foreign contribution and assets on cancellation/surrender/cessation; full vesting of part-foreign-funded property; a **Section 48** amendment barring initiation of any investigation without **prior approval of the Central Government**.
- Constitutional hooks: **Art. 19(1)(c)**, **19(1)(a)**, **14**, **300A**; **Entry 10, Union List** (foreign affairs) is the competence basis.
- Category of persons barred from accepting foreign contribution under **Section 3**: election candidates, judges, government servants, members of legislatures, political parties, and media/news organisations.
- Judicial context: the **Kerala High Court** set aside the Centre's refusal to renew certificates of two NGOs (**Save A Family Plan**; **Kerala Social Service Forum**), holding that reasons must be specified in every order.

**Notebook.** GS2 — Polity (Parliament and its committees) *and* GS2 — Governance (role of NGOs, civil society, development industry).

### 2. Lakhpati Didi target doubled to 6 crore — and a ₹11 lakh crore credit roadmap to get there — Society

**Headline.** At a review of **SHG–bank linkages** under **DAY-NRLM** on **3–4 September 2026**, the Ministry of Rural Development set out a five-year credit roadmap to take the **Lakhpati Didi** target from **3 crore to 6 crore women**, backed by **₹10 lakh crore** of bank credit to Self-Help Groups and a further **₹1 lakh crore** earmarked for **individual women entrepreneurs**.

**Exam angle.** The number that matters is not the headline target but the *instrument*. India's rural women's-collectives programme has, for fifteen years, run on the SHG–bank linkage model: mobilise women into groups, build a credit history through internal lending and revolving funds, then leverage bank credit at scale. What this roadmap does is (a) shift a chunk of lending from the **group** to the **individual woman entrepreneur** — a graduation step that acknowledges some Lakhpati Didis have outgrown group-liability lending; and (b) attack the **regional skew** in SHG credit, with banks directed to prioritise underserved and high-poverty districts and private banks specifically told to show up at **District Level Consultative Committee** meetings. The governance element is quietly the most examinable: **quarterly virtual and half-yearly physical reviews** with State Rural Livelihood Missions, **RBI**, **NABARD** and commercial banks, plus a dedicated SHG loan helpline — i.e. a monitoring architecture bolted onto a credit target. For Mains, this is your best current example of the argument that *financial inclusion has moved from account-opening (Jan Dhan) to credit-deepening*, and of the critique that follows: rising SHG indebtedness without commensurate market linkage risks converting a livelihoods programme into a debt programme.

**Facts to memorise.**

- New target: **6 crore Lakhpati Didis** (up from **3 crore**), against roughly **3.01 crore** already certified by early 2026; horizon **2029-30**.
- Five-year credit plan: **₹10 lakh crore** to SHGs + **₹1 lakh crore** to individual women entrepreneurs.
- Scale of the base: about **10 crore rural women** mobilised into roughly **91–93 lakh SHGs** under DAY-NRLM.
- **Lakhpati Didi** = an SHG household with an **annual household income of ₹1 lakh or more** (sustained, not one-off). Announced in the **Independence Day 2023** address (2 crore), raised to **3 crore** in the **Interim Budget 2024-25**, now to 6 crore.
- **DAY-NRLM**: launched as **NRLM (Aajeevika) in June 2011**, restructuring the earlier **SGSY**; renamed **Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana–NRLM in 2015**; **Ministry of Rural Development**; centrally sponsored, cost-shared **60:40** (90:10 for NE and Himalayan States).
- Monitoring: **quarterly virtual + half-yearly physical** reviews with SRLMs, **RBI**, **NABARD** and banks; private banks mandated into **DLCC** participation; dedicated SHG credit helpline.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Indian Society (role of women, women's organisations); GS2 — Governance (welfare schemes and their performance); GS3 — Inclusive growth and financial inclusion.

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* → **Pillar 1D (Financial Inclusion)** and **Pillar 2 (Poverty & Rural Schemes)**. **Nudge:** build a single **SHG credit-ladder diagram** — SHG formation → Revolving Fund → Community Investment Fund → SHG–bank linkage (cash-credit limit) → individual enterprise loan / Mudra → producer collective (FPO/SHG federation) — and hang the Lakhpati Didi target off the last two rungs. ### 3. NFRA sets up a nine-member advisory panel on audit quality — with AI risk written into the mandate — Economy

**Headline.** On **4 September 2026** the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** constituted a **nine-member Advisory Committee on Audit Quality, Assurance and Technology**, under **Rules 15 and 16 of the NFRA Rules, 2018**. Its distinguishing feature: an explicit mandate to advise on risks arising from **data analytics, automation, artificial intelligence and machine learning** in the conduct of statutory audits.

**Exam angle.** Two exam uses. First, **institutional**: NFRA is the standard "which body, under which section, under which ministry" trap — a body **constituted in October 2018 under Section 132(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, under the **Ministry of Corporate Affairs**, which *recommends* accounting and auditing standards to the Central Government, *monitors* compliance, oversees the quality of service of the professions, and can *investigate* professional misconduct of chartered accountants and audit firms — with appeals lying to the **NCLAT**, and with jurisdiction over listed and large unlisted companies while ICAI retains the rest. Second, **thematic**: this is a clean, citable instance of regulators formally absorbing **AI risk** into an existing supervisory mandate rather than legislating a new AI law — the committee is asked to look at cybersecurity and cloud risks to audit platforms, integrity and confidentiality of audit documentation, and security of client data, alongside consistent application of the Standards on Auditing and Quality Management. For a GS3 answer on AI governance, that is a much better example than another paragraph about the EU AI Act.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Committee constituted **4 September 2026**; **nine members**; drawn from regulators, CFOs, audit committees, independent directors, technology experts and the accounting profession.
- Legal basis: **Rules 15 and 16 of the NFRA Rules, 2018** (power to constitute study groups, committees and task forces).
- It is NFRA's **third** major advisory body — after the **Technical Advisory Committee (2020, term ended December 2022)** and the **Advisory Group on Auditor–Audit Committee Resources (July 2024)**.
- **NFRA static:** constituted **October 2018**; statutory basis **Section 132(1), Companies Act, 2013**; **Ministry of Corporate Affairs**; chairperson plus a maximum of **15 members**; appeal against its orders lies to the **National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)**.
- Mandate of the new panel: emerging risks warranting thematic/sectoral focus; AI, ML, analytics and automation in audit; cybersecurity and cloud risk; consistent application of **Standards on Auditing (SAs)** and **Standards on Quality Management**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Indian Economy (corporate governance, regulatory bodies); GS2 — statutory and regulatory bodies.

**Static anchor.** *Mrunal* → **Pillar 4A (financial-sector regulators)** / your **corporate governance** page. **Nudge:** make a **regulator-comparison strip** for SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, IBBI, CCI and NFRA — parent statute + year + parent ministry + appellate forum (SAT / SAT / SAT / NCLAT / NCLAT / NCLAT). Add a second column, "AI/tech supervision hook", and enter this committee against NFRA; you will be able to reuse that column for any GS3 answer on regulating emerging technology through existing regulators.

### 4. BIO-NIVESH — a matchmaking platform aimed squarely at biotech's 'valley of death' — Science & Technology

**Headline.** On **3 September 2026**, Union MoS (Independent Charge) for Science & Technology **Dr Jitendra Singh** launched **BIO-NIVESH**, a **Department of Biotechnology (DBT)/BIRAC** platform to connect biotechnology start-ups with private investors. The inaugural edition brought together about **20 high-potential biotech and deep-tech start-ups** and roughly **50 investors**.

**Exam angle.** The concept to learn from this item is the **"valley of death"** — the stage between laboratory proof-of-concept and commercially viable scale-up, where deep-science ventures fail for want of patient capital. Grants get you to proof-of-concept; venture capital arrives at commercial traction; the gap in between is where biotech dies. BIO-NIVESH is designed as a **two-way engagement** rather than a pitch event — start-ups learn what investors underwrite, investors learn how biotech's scientific, regulatory and development timelines differ from software's. Selection criteria are worth remembering because they double as an answer framework: **innovation, stage of development, market potential, scalability and regulatory readiness**. Fit it into the policy stack you already have: **BIRAC** (2012) does grants and equity; **BioE3 (2024)** is the industrial policy; the **RDI Fund** supplies patient capital; BIO-NIVESH is the market-linkage layer. The honest critique for Mains: a networking platform cannot by itself fix a thin domestic deep-science VC pool, slow **CDSCO/GEAC** clearances, or start-ups' reluctance to disclose before IP is secured — so treat it as necessary but plainly insufficient.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **BIO-NIVESH** launched **3 September 2026**, New Delhi, by **Dr Jitendra Singh**; implemented through **DBT/BIRAC**; inaugural edition **~20 start-ups, ~50 investors**.
- **India's BioEconomy Report (IBER) 2026**, released by **BIRAC on 19 March 2026**: bioeconomy at **US$195.3 billion in 2025**, growing **~18% year-on-year**, contributing about **4.8% of GDP**; up from roughly **US$10 billion in 2014**.
- Target: **US$300 billion by 2030**. Biotech start-ups grew from **5,365 (2021)** to about **13,000 (2025)**.
- **BIRAC** — Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council, set up **2012** as a not-for-profit PSU under **DBT**; programmes include **BIG, SPARSH, PACE, LEAP**.
- **BioE3 Policy, 2024** — *Biotechnology for Economy, Environment and Employment*; **National Biopharma Mission, 2017** (₹1,500 crore, implemented by BIRAC); **RDI Fund** (₹1 lakh crore) with BIRAC as a second-level fund manager for biotech.
- Key term: **"valley of death"** — the funding gap between proof-of-concept and commercial scale-up.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Science & Technology (biotechnology; indigenisation of technology and developing new technology) and GS3 — Economy (start-up ecosystem, industrial policy).

**Static anchor.** *Shankar IAS / your Sci-Tech notes* → **Biotechnology chapter**. **Nudge:** add a **"biotech policy stack" ladder** — DBT (1986, department) → BIRAC (2012, funding) → National Biopharma Mission (2017) → BioE3 Policy (2024) → RDI Fund → BIO-NIVESH (2026, investor linkage) — with one line each on *what problem it solves*. ### 5. Ports told to fast-track green fuel — and India's three hydrogen-hub ports move to bunkering — Environment

**Headline.** On **3 September 2026**, Union Minister for Ports, Shipping and Waterways **Sarbananda Sonowal** reviewed progress on **green shipping corridors** and port readiness for alternative fuels at **TERI's Gwal Pahari campus, Gurugram**, and directed major ports to fast-track **green fuel readiness**. Three ports — **Deendayal (Kandla)**, **Paradip** and **V.O. Chidambaranar (Tuticorin)** — presented progress on their **Green Fuel Bunkering Hubs**.

**Exam angle.** The chain to hold in your head is: *green hydrogen production → conversion to green ammonia/methanol → bunkering at a port → a zero-emission corridor between two such ports.* The same three ports that were recognised as **Green Hydrogen Hubs under the National Green Hydrogen Mission** are now being built out as **bunkering** hubs — production and refuelling converging on the same geography, which is exactly why those three names keep recurring. Two institutions to name: the **National Centre of Excellence for Green Ports and Shipping (NCoEGPS)**, set up in **March 2023** by the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways and **hosted by TERI**, which drafts the regulatory framework and technology-adoption roadmap; and the **Rocky Mountain Institute (RMI)**, which is doing the corridor modelling. The external driver is the **IMO's 2023 GHG Strategy**, which sets an indicative goal of **net-zero GHG from international shipping by or around 2050** — a deadline that turns port decarbonisation from an environmental nicety into a trade-competitiveness question, since ships will route to where clean fuel is available. India-specific frame for Mains: green shipping is the **demand anchor** the National Green Hydrogen Mission needs — without bunkering demand, India's green-hydrogen capacity has no domestic offtake and must chase an export market it does not control.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Review held **3 September 2026** at **TERI, Gwal Pahari (Gurugram)**; presentations by **NCoEGPS**, **RMI**, and the three port authorities.
- **Green Hydrogen Hub Ports under the National Green Hydrogen Mission**: **Deendayal Port Authority** (Kandla, Gujarat), **Paradip Port Authority** (Odisha), **V.O. Chidambaranar Port Authority** (Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu) — the same three now developing **Green Fuel Bunkering Hubs**.
- **NCoEGPS** — National Centre of Excellence for Green Ports and Shipping, established **March 2023** under the **Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways**, hosted by **TERI**; the three port authorities signed the enabling MoU with TERI.
- **IMO** — specialised agency of the UN (HQ **London**); **2023 IMO GHG Strategy**: net-zero GHG from international shipping **by or around 2050**, with indicative checkpoints for 2030 and 2040. **MARPOL** (and its **Annex VI** on air pollution) is the principal anti-pollution convention.
- Indian policy stack: **Harit Sagar — Green Port Guidelines, 2023**; **Maritime India Vision 2030**; **Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047**; **Green Tug Transition Programme**; **National Green Hydrogen Mission (January 2023, ₹19,744 crore)**.
- Candidate green marine fuels: **green hydrogen, green ammonia, green methanol, LNG (transitional)**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Environment (climate change mitigation, conservation) and GS3 — Infrastructure (ports, shipping).

**Static anchor.** *Shankar IAS* → **Climate Change / Mitigation** chapter, plus *Mrunal* **Pillar 5 (Infrastructure — ports)**. **Nudge:** add a **maritime-decarbonisation page**: IMO 2023 GHG Strategy (with the 2030/2040 checkpoints) → MARPOL Annex VI → Harit Sagar Guidelines 2023 → Green Tug Transition Programme → Green Hydrogen Hub Ports (the three names) → green shipping corridors. Cross-link it to your **National Green Hydrogen Mission** entry with a single arrow labelled *"domestic offtake"* — that arrow is the argument, and it is worth a mark on its own.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS2 — Governance / Polity, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"Regulation of foreign funding of voluntary organisations is a legitimate state function; the difficulty lies in ensuring that the consequences of a procedural lapse are not made indistinguishable from the consequences of proven wrongdoing." Critically examine this proposition in the light of the proposed amendments to the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010.**

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Frame the legitimacy of regulation.** Foreign funding of domestic civil society engages the Union's exclusive competence over foreign affairs (Entry 10, Union List); the courts have held that the right to receive foreign contribution is not itself a fundamental right, and comparable regimes exist in other democracies. Trace the tightening: FCRA 1976 → FCRA 2010 → the 2020 amendments (no sub-granting, administrative-expense cap cut to 20%, designated SBI account) → the 2026 Bill.

2. **Identify what is new, and why it is different in kind.** The 2026 Bill moves from regulating the *flow* of funds to vesting the *assets* built with them in a Centre-appointed designated authority — triggered not only by cancellation but by mere cessation of a certificate, with full vesting of part-foreign-funded property, no pre-decisional hearing, and an appeal that lies only against subsequent dealings, not against the refusal itself. This is where proportionality bites: Article 300A read with Article 14, and the chilling effect on Article 19(1)(c).

3. **Weigh the counter-arguments honestly.** The state's case: diversion of funds, opacity in end-use, and the difficulty of recovering assets once a certificate is revoked; a vesting mechanism is the natural remedy for that enforcement gap. Also note the genuine compliance failures in the sector, and the fact that referral to a 31-member JPC is itself the constitutional system working — deliberation before enactment rather than litigation after it.

4. **Way forward and close.** Distinguish technical lapses from substantive violations before any asset consequence attaches; mandate a pre-decisional hearing and a statutory appeal against refusal to renew; require reasoned, disclosable orders (as the Kerala High Court insisted), with security-sensitive material redacted but reviewable; restrict vesting to the demonstrably foreign-funded share, independently valued. Conclude: legitimacy of purpose does not cure disproportionality of means; the test of the JPC will be whether it restores due process without diluting oversight.

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**Q1 — (c).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the Bill's proposed Chapter IIIA creates a Centre-appointed "designated authority" with provisional vesting of foreign contribution and assets on cancellation, surrender or cessation of a certificate; the JPC notified on 3 September 2026 has 31 members (21 Lok Sabha + 10 Rajya Sabha), chaired by Sanjay Jaiswal. Statement 3 is wrong — the proposed Section 48 amendment requires prior approval of the **Central Government**, not of the Supreme Court.

**Q2 — (b).** The target has been raised from 3 crore to 6 crore Lakhpati Didis, backed by roughly ₹10 lakh crore of SHG credit and ₹1 lakh crore for individual women entrepreneurs over five years. Statement 3 is wrong — **DAY-NRLM is administered by the Ministry of Rural Development**, not the Ministry of Women and Child Development.

**Q3 — (b).** The nine-member Advisory Committee on Audit Quality, Assurance and Technology was constituted by the **NFRA on 4 September 2026 under Rules 15 and 16 of the NFRA Rules, 2018**. NFRA itself is constituted under Section 132(1) of the Companies Act, 2013, under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, with appeals to the NCLAT.

**Q4 — (c).** BIO-NIVESH is a **DBT/BIRAC platform**, launched on 3 September 2026, that connects biotechnology and deep-tech start-ups with private investors to bridge the "valley of death" between proof-of-concept and commercial scale-up.

**Q5 — (b).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Statement 3 is wrong — the **NCoEGPS (established March 2023, hosted by TERI) functions under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways**, not the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy. (The Green Hydrogen Hub recognition for the three ports comes under the National Green Hydrogen Mission.)

**Q6 — (c).** A JPC is an **ad hoc** committee that dissolves on submitting its report, and its members are nominated by the Speaker (Lok Sabha) and the Chairman (Rajya Sabha). Statement 3 is wrong — **JPC recommendations have persuasive, not binding, force**; the Government must place an action-taken statement but is not obliged to accept them.

**Q7 — (d).** All three are correct. The IMO is the UN specialised agency for shipping safety, security and marine-pollution prevention (HQ London); MARPOL is the principal convention on ship-source pollution; and the **2023 IMO GHG Strategy** sets the indicative goal of net-zero GHG from international shipping **by or around 2050**, with checkpoints for 2030 and 2040.
