### 1. Belgian PM Bart De Wever in India — the first such visit in two decades, and a defence-plus-diamonds package — International Relations

**Headline.** Belgian Prime Minister **Bart De Wever** was in India on an official visit from **2–4 September 2026**; he and PM Modi held delegation-level talks on **3 September 2026** and issued a **India–Belgium Joint Statement**. The two sides set a target of **doubling bilateral trade within five years**, signed defence-cooperation arrangements including work on **joint weapons production**, and cleared a set of company-to-company defence agreements. It is the **first visit by a Belgian Prime Minister to India in about two decades**.

**Exam angle.** Read this as the *bilateral wing of the India–EU relationship*, not as a standalone friendship story. The India–EU FTA was concluded on **27 January 2026**, and individual member-state visits now do the ratification-era spadework: locking in supply-chain niches (semiconductors, critical minerals, green hydrogen/ammonia), and converting a legacy commodity relationship into a technology one. Belgium's leverage over India is unusually concentrated — **Antwerp** is the world's rough-diamond clearing house and India (Surat/Mumbai) is its largest customer, so the diamond channel is a genuine structural interest for both, not decoration. The defence element is the newer part: co-production and Belgian participation around platforms such as the **Zorawar** light tank moves Belgium from a trade partner to a defence-industrial one, which is the same template India has used with France, Spain and now Japan. For Mains, this is clean evidence for the argument that India's Europe policy has shifted from *bloc-level engagement* to *bloc plus a lattice of bilaterals*.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Visit **2–4 September 2026**; talks and Joint Statement **3 September 2026**; Belgian PM **Bart De Wever**; first Belgian PM visit to India in ~20 years.
- Bilateral trade **US$13.01 billion in FY2025-26**; agreed target: **double in five years**. Belgium is India's **fifth-largest trading partner within the EU**.
- Belgian **FDI into India ≈ US$4.2 billion** (April 2000 – December 2025).
- Inaugural **India–Belgium Strategic Dialogue** was held in **Brussels on 15 July 2026** — the standing mechanism this visit built on.
- Sectors in the package: defence-industrial co-operation (incl. **Zorawar** light tank), semiconductors, critical minerals, renewables and a **green-ammonia project in Andhra Pradesh**, human mobility; joint language on terrorism.
- Diamonds: **India took ~58% of Belgium's unworked-diamond exports in 2025**; under the India–EU FTA, duty on EU-origin natural polished diamonds falls from **5.5% → 2.5%**.

### 2. 'RAKSHA' — India puts its defence diplomacy on a ten-year written roadmap — Internal Security

**Headline.** On **3 September 2026**, Raksha Mantri **Rajnath Singh** released **RAKSHA**, a document setting out India's **strategic roadmap and vision for defence diplomacy over the next ten years**. It is India's first consolidated, publicly issued statement of defence-diplomacy doctrine, built on **four pillars**.

**Exam angle.** Defence diplomacy is the use of military instruments — exercises, training slots, defence attachés, port calls, equipment transfers, HADR — for *foreign-policy* ends short of the use of force. India has done all of this for decades without writing it down; the significance of RAKSHA is the codification, because a published doctrine creates benchmarks against which Parliament, partners and analysts can measure delivery. Note how the four pillars map onto existing policy stacks you already know: **strategic partnerships** (2+2 dialogues, logistics agreements such as LEMOA/ACSA/RELOS), **capacity building** (training vacancies at IMA/NDC, exercises like Dustlik, Dharma Guardian, Malabar), **indigenous defence exports** (the ₹50,000-crore-by-2029 export ambition, iDEX, DPEPP 2020, the Positive Indigenisation Lists), and **maritime security** (the Indian Navy's 2015 maritime strategy commitment to be a **"net security provider"**, MAHASAGAR/SAGAR, IFC-IOR at Gurugram, first-responder HADR). The Mains line to keep: *defence diplomacy is where Atmanirbharta and foreign policy meet — exports are simultaneously an industrial-policy instrument and a strategic-influence instrument.*

**Facts to memorise.**

- **RAKSHA** released **3 September 2026** at Kartavya Bhawan, New Delhi, by Raksha Mantri Rajnath Singh; horizon **10 years**.
- **Four pillars**: (i) Strategic Partnerships; (ii) Capacity Building (joint exercises, training, exchange of best practices); (iii) Indigenous Defence Manufacturing and Exports under Make in India; (iv) Maritime Security — India as **net security provider and first responder** in the Indian Ocean Region.
- Related same-week announcement: **Aero India 2027** scheduled for **8–12 February** (Bengaluru), registrations opened 3 September 2026.
- Vocabulary: "net security provider" entered Indian usage after US Defence Secretary **Robert Gates** used it of India at the **Shangri-La Dialogue, 2009**; the Indian Navy adopted it in its **2015 maritime security strategy**.

### 3. UNEP's first dedicated overshoot report: 1.5 °C will be crossed, and the argument moves to peak-and-return — Environment

**Headline.** On **2 September 2026** UNEP released **"Limiting Overshoot: Navigating exceedance of 1.5 °C and pathways towards return"** — its first report devoted specifically to *overshoot*, i.e. exceeding the Paris temperature limit and then coming back down. Its headline finding: global warming is set to **cross 1.5 °C within the next few years**, but a return below it remains physically possible under an **"overshoot, peak and decline"** pathway.

**Exam angle.** The conceptual shift is the exam-worthy part. For a decade the framing was *avoid 1.5 °C*; UNEP has now formally moved the policy question to *how high does the peak go, how long does it last, and what is irreversible on the way through*. Three distinctions to be able to make on demand: (a) **peak warming vs end-of-century warming** — the peak determines the damage, the end-of-century number determines whether you "returned"; (b) **reversible vs irreversible impacts** — temperature can come down, but ice-sheet loss, glacier mass, coral mortality and species extinctions do not simply un-happen, which is why UNEP insists overshoot is not a safe detour; (c) **emissions reduction vs carbon dioxide removal (CDR)** — UNEP is deliberately restrictive, saying CDR can credibly return the world below 1.5 °C **only if peak warming stays well below 2 °C and residual emissions are cut**, which is a direct guard against using CDR as an excuse to delay mitigation. India-relevance: this raises the salience of *adaptation and loss-and-damage* finance in India's negotiating position, and hands you a fresh, citable data anchor for any climate answer.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **UNEP, "Limiting Overshoot"**, released **2 September 2026**; first UNEP report dedicated to overshoot.
- **Full NDC + net-zero implementation** → peak warming ≈ **1.8 °C** (range **1.7–2.2 °C**).
- **Current policies** → median ≈ **2.6 °C by 2100** (range **1.9–3.6 °C**).
- Benchmark carried over from the **Emissions Gap Report 2025**: global GHG emissions must fall **55% from 2019 levels by 2035** for 1.5 °C with limited exceedance.
- Recommended pathway: **overshoot → peak → decline**, requiring net-negative emissions eventually; CDR is credible for return **only if** peak stays well below 2 °C.
- Adjacent, same week: India and Germany signed a **Joint Declaration of Intent on the conservation and wise use of wetlands of international importance** at the **4th Indo-German Environment Forum** (theme: "Together for More Resilience"), New Delhi — IGEF was established in **2008**.

**Notebook.** GS3 — Environment (climate change, international agreements).

**Static anchor.** Shankar IAS *Environment* → "Climate change: UNFCCC, Kyoto, Paris". Update the Paris Agreement page with a new sub-heading, **"Overshoot"**, holding the three numbers above, and add a cross-link to your **carbon-sequestration/CDR** page distinguishing *nature-based removal* (afforestation, soil carbon, blue carbon) from *engineered removal* (BECCS, DACCS, enhanced weathering). Also add one line to the UNEP flagship-reports list: Emissions Gap Report, Adaptation Gap Report, Global Resources Outlook, Frontiers, and now *Limiting Overshoot*.

### 4. Supreme Court: the Bar Council of India has no disciplinary power over law students — Polity & Governance

**Headline.** A Bench of **CJI Surya Kant** with **Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana** held that the **Bar Council of India has no statutory power to discipline or punish law students**, and **set aside two BCI notifications** issued in the row over **NALSAR University of Law** students' objections to the CJI's proposed participation in the university's convocation — even though the BCI had withdrawn both within hours. Discipline over students, the Court said, belongs to the **educational institutions** under their own regulatory norms.

**Exam angle.** This is a clean statutory-jurisdiction case and therefore excellent Prelims material. The **Advocates Act, 1961** creates a self-regulating profession: the BCI's disciplinary machinery under **Sections 35–37** is triggered by "professional or other misconduct" of an **advocate**, i.e. a person already **enrolled on a State Bar Council roll under Section 24**. A law student is not an advocate; the BCI's writ over legal education flows from a *different* head — **Section 7(1)(h) and (i)** (promoting legal education, laying down standards in consultation with universities) and the rule-making power in **Section 49(1)(af)** — which lets it regulate *curricula and institutional recognition*, not individual student conduct. The Court's reasoning is the standard administrative-law point that a statutory body's powers are only those conferred, expressly or by necessary implication; acting beyond them is *ultra vires*. Note also the free-speech undertone — the trigger was student protest — and the procedural point that withdrawal of an impugned order does not bar the Court from setting it aside where the question of power itself needs settling.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Order of **September 2026**; Bench: **CJI Surya Kant, Joymalya Bagchi J., V. Mohana J.**; two BCI notifications set aside; trigger — **NALSAR University of Law, Hyderabad**.
- Holding: BCI's regulatory/disciplinary control attaches **only after enrolment as an advocate**; student conduct is for the university/institution.
- **Advocates Act, 1961**: BCI constituted under **Section 4**; functions under **Section 7**; admission as advocate **Section 24**; **AIBE** as a post-enrolment practice requirement; disciplinary committees and misconduct **Sections 35–37**; appeal to the Supreme Court under **Section 38**.
- Composition of the BCI: **Attorney General of India** and **Solicitor General of India** as *ex officio* members, plus one member elected by each State Bar Council.
- Companion current-affairs cross-link (already in your notes, 22 August 2026): the Supreme Court's modification of the **All India Judges Association** Bar-practice requirement for entry into the judicial service.

**Notebook.** GS2 — Polity & Governance (statutory bodies; judiciary and the legal profession).

**Static anchor.** Laxmikanth → the chapter on the Judiciary, plus your "statutory vs constitutional vs regulatory bodies" comparison sheet. Add a **BCI row**: parent Act (Advocates Act, 1961), nature (statutory), composition (AG + SG *ex officio* + State Bar Council electees), core functions, and now this **limit** on jurisdiction. Cross-link to the *ultra vires* doctrine entry in your administrative-law notes.

### 5. CARA launches its 2026 adoption-awareness theme — and moves the campaign out of November — Society

**Headline.** In **September 2026** the **Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA)**, under the **Ministry of Women and Child Development**, launched its Adoption Awareness 2026 theme — **"Legal Adoption. Safe Childhood. Secure Future. Say No to Illegal Adoption!"** — announced by CARA CEO **Bhavna Saxena**. The campaign is being run through State/UT-level workshops **from September onwards**, rather than being concentrated in November as before, with a first workshop at the Delhi Secretariat on **10 September 2026** and tightened coordination with the **Ministry of Health and Family Welfare** and the **Ministry of Home Affairs** to choke off illegal adoption.

**Exam angle.** The policy problem behind the slogan is a **supply–demand inversion**: prospective adoptive parents on CARA's registry outnumber legally adoptable children in the pool by roughly an order of magnitude, and the resulting waiting periods push desperate families toward extra-legal routes — direct "handover" from hospitals or maternity homes, informal relative arrangements never regularised, and outright trafficking dressed up as adoption. That is exactly why the coordination partners are **MoHFW** (hospitals and maternity homes as the point of leakage) and **MHA** (trafficking and inter-country movement). The legal architecture is the part UPSC tests: adoption of orphaned, abandoned and surrendered children runs through the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**, which gave CARA statutory status; India is a party to the **Hague Convention on Intercountry Adoption, 1993**, with CARA as the designated **Central Authority**; and the **JJ (Amendment) Act, 2021** shifted the power to issue adoption orders from civil courts to **District Magistrates**. Running parallel and separate is the **Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956** — a personal-law route, available only to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, whereas the JJ Act is secular and open to all.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **CARA** is a **statutory body under Section 68 of the JJ Act, 2015**, under the **Ministry of Women and Child Development**; nodal body for in-country and inter-country adoption; operates the **CARINGS** portal.
- Designated **Central Authority** under the **Hague Convention on Protection of Children and Co-operation in Respect of Intercountry Adoption, 1993**; India **ratified in 2003**.
- **Adoption Regulations, 2022** are framed under **Section 68(c)** read with Section 2(3) of the JJ Act, 2015 — they replaced the 2017 Regulations.
- **JJ (Amendment) Act, 2021** amended **Section 61** to substitute "District Magistrate" for "court" in adoption proceedings; brought into force **1 September 2022**; DMs/ADMs also monitor JJ agencies district-wise.
- Two parallel legal routes: **JJ Act, 2015** (secular, for OAS children, via CARA) vs **HAMA, 1956** (personal law; Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, Sikhs; no CARA route).
- 2026 theme: **"Legal Adoption. Safe Childhood. Secure Future. Say No to Illegal Adoption!"**; workshops from **September 2026**; Delhi workshop **10 September 2026**.

**Notebook.** GS2 — Social Justice / Governance (welfare of children; statutory bodies); GS1 — Indian Society (family, vulnerable sections).

**Static anchor.** Your Social Justice notes → "Children: constitutional and statutory framework". Build a **child-rights instrument ladder** on one page: **Articles 15(3), 21A, 23, 24, 39(e)–(f), 45, 51A(k)** → **UNCRC (1989, India ratified 1992)** → **JJ Act 2015 + Amendment 2021** → **POCSO 2012** → **CPCR Act 2005 (NCPCR)** → **Hague Convention 1993 / CARA**. Add HAMA 1956 as a side-branch so the personal-law vs secular-law distinction is visible at a glance.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS2 — International Relations, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"Defence diplomacy has moved from being an adjunct of India's foreign policy to one of its principal instruments." Examine this statement in the light of India's recently articulated ten-year defence-diplomacy roadmap.**

**Model-answer skeleton (4 points):**

1. **Define and frame.** Defence diplomacy = peacetime use of military instruments (exercises, training, attachés, port calls, equipment transfers, HADR, defence-industrial co-production) for foreign-policy ends. Note the shift: from *episodic and reactive* to *codified*, with a published ten-year roadmap resting on four pillars — strategic partnerships, capacity building, indigenous manufacturing and exports, and maritime security.

2. **Evidence that it has become a principal instrument.** Logistics and enabling agreements (LEMOA, ACSA with Japan, RELOS with Russia) that convert access into reach; 2+2 ministerial formats; a widening exercise calendar (Malabar, Dustlik, Dharma Guardian, Varuna); training slots as long-horizon influence in Africa, ASEAN and the IOR; defence exports as strategic instrument (BrahMos to the Philippines, coastal-surveillance radar chains, LoC-funded platforms for neighbours); India as first responder in HADR — the operational content of "net security provider".

3. **Counter-view — the limits.** Capacity and delivery gaps between announcement and induction; import dependence still constraining the credibility of an export pitch; competition from China's cheaper, faster defence-export offer in South Asia and Africa; institutional thinness (defence attaché cadre, absence of a standing defence-diplomacy budget line); and the risk that a doctrine document substitutes vocabulary for capability. Strategic autonomy also caps how far partnerships can be deepened into alliances.

4. **Way forward and close.** Tie pillars to measurable benchmarks (export value, training slots, exercise tempo, MDA coverage); institutionalise the defence-attaché stream and a dedicated financing window; align exports with the Indigenisation Lists and iDEX pipeline so influence and industrial policy reinforce each other. Conclude: the roadmap makes defence diplomacy *legible and accountable* — its promotion from adjunct to principal instrument will be judged by delivery, not by the document.

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**Q1 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the two sides announced a five-year doubling target, and the inaugural India–Belgium Strategic Dialogue was held in Brussels on 15 July 2026. Statement 3 is wrong — Belgium is India's **fifth-largest** trading partner within the EU, with bilateral trade of about **US$13.01 billion in FY2025-26**.

**Q2 — (a).** RAKSHA, released by the Raksha Mantri on 3 September 2026, is a **ten-year defence-diplomacy roadmap** built on four pillars: strategic partnerships, capacity building, indigenous defence manufacturing and exports, and maritime security.

**Q3 — (c).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: UNEP finds 1.5 °C likely to be crossed within the next few years, and puts peak warming at about **1.8 °C (1.7–2.2 °C)** even with full NDC and net-zero implementation. Statement 3 is wrong — the report is explicitly conditional: CDR can credibly deliver a return below 1.5 °C **only if peak warming stays well below 2 °C** and residual emissions are cut.

**Q4 — (b).** The Bench held that the BCI's disciplinary jurisdiction under the Advocates Act, 1961 attaches only to persons **enrolled as advocates**; law students are governed by their own institutions. Two BCI notifications arising from the NALSAR row were set aside.

**Q5 — (b).** Statements 1 and 3 are correct. Statement 2 is wrong: CARA functions under the **Ministry of Women and Child Development**, not the Ministry of Home Affairs — though it coordinates with MHA and MoHFW on preventing illegal adoption.

**Q6 — (d).** All three are correct. Under the Advocates Act, 1961 the **Attorney General and Solicitor General are *ex officio* members** of the BCI; disciplinary jurisdiction for professional or other misconduct runs against **enrolled advocates** (Sections 35–37); and an appeal from a BCI disciplinary committee order lies to the **Supreme Court** under Section 38.

**Q7 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the Paris Agreement's NDC cycle requires successive contributions reflecting **progression**, and the **Global Stocktake** occurs every five years (first concluded at COP28, 2023). Statement 3 is wrong — that annexed, country-specific binding-target design belongs to the **Kyoto Protocol** (Annex B); Paris is bottom-up and nationally determined.
