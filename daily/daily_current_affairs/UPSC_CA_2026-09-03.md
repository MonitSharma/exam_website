### 1. GSLV-F17 to place EOS-05 in geostationary orbit — India's first Earth-observation satellite parked at 36,000 km — Science & Technology

**Headline.** ISRO has scheduled the GSLV-F17 mission from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota, in the early hours of **4 September 2026** (lift-off around **02:55 IST**), carrying **EOS-05**, also designated **GISAT-1A** — an imaging satellite meant for **geostationary/geosynchronous orbit** rather than the polar sun-synchronous orbit that almost all Indian remote-sensing satellites use.

**Exam angle.** The whole point of the mission is the orbit, not the camera. A satellite at ~36,000 km above the equator has an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation, so it hovers over one longitude — giving **near-continuous staring coverage of the entire Indian subcontinent** with a revisit interval reported at roughly **five minutes**, against the days-long revisit of a low-Earth-orbit satellite. That converts Earth observation from periodic mapping into real-time monitoring: cyclone and flood tracking, forest-fire and cloud-burst detection, border and coastal surveillance, crop-stress watch. The trade-off is resolution — distance costs you ground detail, which is why GEO imaging supplements rather than replaces Cartosat/RISAT/EOS satellites in LEO. Note also the programme history: the first attempt at this capability, **EOS-03/GISAT-1, was lost when GSLV-F10 failed in August 2021** because the cryogenic upper stage did not ignite.

**Facts to memorise.**

- Mission **GSLV-F17**; payload **EOS-05 (GISAT-1A)**; mass ~**2,367 kg**; launch **4 September 2026** from Sriharikota.
- Target orbit: geostationary/geosynchronous, ~**36,000 km**; revisit of the same region reported at ~**5 minutes**.
- GSLV = three-stage (solid S139 → liquid L40 strap-ons and second stage → indigenous **CE-7.5 cryogenic upper stage**); GEO payload class ~2–2.5 t. LVM3 (GSLV Mk-III) is the heavier 4 t-to-GTO vehicle.
- Contrast: **PSLV** → sun-synchronous polar LEO (Earth-resources, EOS-04/06/08/09); **GSLV/LVM3** → GTO/GEO (INSAT, GSAT, NVS).
- Precedent failure: **GSLV-F10 / EOS-03, 12 August 2021** — cryogenic stage ignition failure.

### 2. Japan Credit Rating Agency lifts India to 'A−' from 'BBB+' — Economy

**Headline.** On **2 September 2026** the **Japan Credit Rating Agency, Ltd. (JCR)** upgraded India's **long-term issuer ratings in both foreign and local currency to 'A−' from 'BBB+'**, assigned a **Stable** outlook, and raised India's **country ceiling to 'A'**.

**Exam angle.** Two concepts do the heavy lifting here. First, a **sovereign credit rating** is an assessment of a government's *ability and willingness* to service debt — it is not a growth forecast, and it feeds directly into the risk premium Indian borrowers pay abroad and into index-inclusion and institutional-investor mandates. Second, the **country ceiling** is the cap on the foreign-currency rating that can normally be assigned to *any* issuer domiciled in that country — so lifting the ceiling to 'A' mechanically widens headroom for Indian corporates and banks raising external commercial borrowings. JCR's cited reasons — growth, a clean banking system, GST and digital public infrastructure — are exactly the fundamentals-versus-ratings argument the **Economic Survey 2020-21** made in its chapter *"Does India's Sovereign Credit Rating reflect its Fundamentals? No!"*, which remains the standard Mains citation on rating-agency methodology bias against emerging economies. The rating is Japanese, not one of the Big Three (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) — worth flagging, since agency identity is a classic Prelims trap.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **JCR**, 2 September 2026: FC and LC long-term issuer rating **BBB+ → A−**; outlook **Stable**; **country ceiling raised to A**.
- Cited: real GDP growth of **7.7% in FY2026**; banking-sector **non-performing loan ratio below 2%**; structural reforms — **GST** (in force 1 July 2017) and **digital public infrastructure** (Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker).
- Forward view: real GDP growth projected **above 6% in FY2027**; risks flagged — **high general government debt** and **complex intergovernmental fiscal relations** keeping deficits elevated.
- Vocabulary: *issuer rating* vs *outlook* vs *country ceiling*; investment grade begins at BBB−/Baa3.

### 3. Index of Services Production: 8 of 19 sub-sectors in double digits in June 2026 — Reports & Indices

**Headline.** MoSPI released the **Index of Services Production (ISP)** for **June 2026** on **31 August 2026** — the third release of what is still a **trial** series — showing **8 of 19 sub-sectors growing in double digits**, and every sub-sector except **air transport (−6%)** in positive territory year-on-year.

**Exam angle.** This closes India's oldest statistical gap. Services contribute roughly **55% of GVA**, yet until 2026 the only monthly output indicator India published was the **IIP**, which covers mining, manufacturing and electricity alone — so more than half the economy had no high-frequency volume measure between quarterly GDP prints. The ISP fills that hole using three data streams, and the choice of streams is itself examinable: **GST returns on outward supplies** for most market services, **administrative records** for railways, air transport, banking and insurance, and the **Annual Survey of Incorporated Services Sector Enterprises (ASISSE)** for health and education. Base year **2024-25** — note this is *not* the IIP's 2022-23 base, a deliberate design choice given the newness of the series. Caveat for Mains: the ISP measures the **formal** services sector; India's large informal services economy remains outside it.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **ISP**, released **31 August 2026** for **June 2026**; **19 sub-sectors**; base year **2024-25**; trial phase; MoSPI.
- Top growth in June 2026: **Real estate 24.7%**, **Retail trade 18.0%**, **Wholesale trade 15.1%**, **Administrative & support services 14.4%**, **IT and computer-related services 13.5%**, **Warehousing & support to transport 11.8%**, **Banking 11.4%**, **Accommodation & food 10.2%**.
- Only contraction: **air transport, −6%**.
- Sources: **GST outward-supply data + administrative records (railways, air transport, banking, insurance) + ASISSE**.
- Guided by the **Technical Advisory Committee on ISP (TAC-ISP)**, chaired by **Debjani Ghosh** (as reported).
- Place in the indicator stack: **IIP** (industry) · **ISP** (services) · **CPI/WPI** (prices) · **PLFS** (labour) · quarterly **GDP/GVA** (MoSPI).

### 4. Gollala Gudi at Palampet becomes a Monument of National Importance — Art & Culture

**Headline.** The **Gollalagudi (Gollala Gudi) temple** at **Palampet village, Mulugu district, Telangana** was declared a **Monument of National Importance** by gazette notification of the Union Ministry of Culture on **1 September 2026**, taking the number of **ASI-protected monuments in Telangana to 10**.

**Exam angle.** Two threads. Architecturally, Gollalagudi is a **trikutalaya** — a three-shrine temple in which an east-facing **mandapa** connects three **garbhagrihas** — a plan strongly associated with **Kakatiya-era temple building of the 12th–13th centuries CE** in the Telangana region. Its village, **Palampet**, is the same village that holds the **Ramappa (Rudreshwara) Temple**, inscribed on the **UNESCO World Heritage List in 2021** — so the new declaration effectively extends statutory protection across a Kakatiya temple cluster rather than a single monument. Procedurally, it is a clean illustration of how the **AMASR Act, 1958** works: a **preliminary notification** was issued on **7 March 2026** for Gollala Gudi and a neighbouring Shiva temple, opening a **two-month window for public objections**, followed by the final gazette notification. ASI's **Hyderabad Circle** now takes over conservation. Exact construction date and original dedication remain open to epigraphic study — a useful honesty note in a Mains answer.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **Gollalagudi**, Palampet, **Mulugu district, Telangana**; declared MNI by gazette notification, **1 September 2026**; preliminary notification **7 March 2026**; Telangana's ASI-protected count rises to **10**.
- **Trikutalaya** = three sanctums sharing one mandapa; attributed to the **Kakatiya period, 12th–13th centuries CE**.
- Same village as the **Ramappa / Rudreshwara Temple**, **UNESCO World Heritage Site, 2021** — built 1213 CE under **Kakatiya ruler Ganapati Deva**, by general Recharla Rudra; named after its sculptor **Ramappa**, uniquely for an Indian temple.
- Legal frame: **Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958**; the **AMASR (Amendment and Validation) Act, 2010** created the **100 m prohibited area** and further **200 m regulated area**, and set up the **National Monuments Authority (NMA)**; the **2017 amendment** allowed "public works" in prohibited areas.
- Kakatiya essentials: capital **Warangal (Orugallu)**; **Ganapati Deva → Rudramadevi → Prataparudra**; seaport **Motupalli**; **Thousand Pillar Temple**, Hanamkonda; **Kakatiya Kala Thoranam** (the Warangal Gate) is on the Telangana state emblem.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Art & Culture (Temple architecture) with a GS2 governance tail (AMASR/NMA).

### 5. Places in News: Vizhinjam ships its first export consignment — Geography

**Headline.** The **366-metre container vessel MSC Beryl** sailed from **Vizhinjam International Seaport, Kerala**, on **1 September 2026** for **Valencia, Spain**, carrying the port's **first-ever export consignment** — eight containers of packaged food from Thiruvananthapuram and seafood from Kochi — on MSC's **Himalaya Express** India–Europe service.

**Exam angle.** Vizhinjam matters for one geographic reason: it is the rare Indian site where a container ship can berth without a dredging bill. Natural water depth close to shore (~**18–20 m** draught), negligible littoral drift and siltation, and a location roughly **10 nautical miles** from the main **east–west international shipping route** connecting Suez to the Malacca Strait — the combination that made Colombo, Singapore and Salalah India's default transhipment hubs. India has historically leaked a large share of its transhipment cargo to those foreign ports; a domestic deep-water transhipment terminal is the policy answer, and Vizhinjam is India's **first dedicated deep-water container transhipment port**. This first export sailing marks the shift from pure transhipment to **EXIM (export–import) operations** — i.e. the port now serves Kerala's own hinterland cargo, not just boxes changing ships. Model: a **landlord-port PPP** between the Government of Kerala (through Vizhinjam International Seaport Ltd.) and **Adani Ports & SEZ**.

**Facts to memorise.**

- **MSC Beryl**, 366 m, departed Vizhinjam **1 September 2026** for **Valencia, Spain**; **8 containers**; **MSC Himalaya Express** service.
- **EXIM operations** flagged off at Vizhinjam on **20 August 2026**; a further **~₹16,000 crore** expansion was announced on 19 August 2026 to raise capacity from **1.6 million TEU to 5.7 million TEU**.
- **TEU** = twenty-foot equivalent unit. Vizhinjam is near **Thiruvananthapuram**, on the **Arabian Sea**; India's **first dedicated deep-water container transhipment port**; developed by **Adani Ports & SEZ** on a landlord-PPP basis with the Kerala government.
- Geographic edge: natural draught ~18–20 m, minimal dredging, ~10 nautical miles off the east–west shipping lane.
- Policy frame: **Sagarmala** (2015), **Maritime India Vision 2030**, **Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047**; rival transhipment hubs **Colombo, Singapore, Salalah, Jebel Ali**.

**Notebook.** GS1 — Geography (Transport & communication) with a GS3 infrastructure tail.

**Static anchor.** **NCERT Class 12 Geography, *India: People and Economy*, Ch. 10 (Transport and Communication)** → ports section; and Mrunal's infrastructure pillar. Add Vizhinjam to your major-ports map alongside Kochi, Tuticorin and Chennai, and cross-link a one-line note on *why* transhipment leakage to Colombo is an economic-cost question, not just a map question.

### C. Mains-Style Question (GS3 — Economy, 250 words, 15 marks)

> **"India's macro-statistical system has long measured its factories better than its offices."** Examine this proposition in the light of the recent introduction of the Index of Services Production, and discuss the challenges that remain in measuring services-sector output in India. *(15 marks)*

**Model-answer skeleton.**

1. **Frame the asymmetry.** Services contribute roughly 55% of GVA, yet the only high-frequency output indicator India published until 2026 was the IIP, covering mining, manufacturing and electricity. Between quarterly GDP releases, more than half the economy was unobserved at monthly frequency — a gap that weakened nowcasting, monetary-policy inputs and counter-cyclical fiscal timing.
2. **What the ISP changes.** Monthly volume index for the formal services sector, 19 sub-sectors, base 2024-25; built on GST outward-supply data, administrative records (railways, air transport, banking, insurance) and ASISSE. It completes the indicator stack — IIP for industry, ISP for services, CPI/WPI for prices, PLFS for labour. June 2026 illustrated its diagnostic value: 8 of 19 sub-sectors in double digits, with air transport the sole contraction — heterogeneity a single GDP number hides.
3. **Challenges that remain.** (i) *Coverage* — the informal services economy, a large employer, sits largely outside GST and ASISSE. (ii) *Measurement* — services output is deflation-hard: quality change in IT, health and education makes real-versus-nominal separation contentious. (iii) *Data dependence* — GST data reflects tax compliance behaviour as much as production, so policy changes can masquerade as output shocks. (iv) *Base and revision* — the series is still on trial, with a base year different from the IIP's, complicating composite readings.
4. **Way forward.** Stabilise and formally adopt the series after the trial phase; build services-specific deflators; extend coverage to the unincorporated sector via periodic surveys (ASUSE); and align base years across IIP, ISP and national accounts. Statistical capacity is itself infrastructure — the argument to close on.

### D. PYQ Mirror — how UPSC has asked these themes before

- **Launch vehicles and orbits (Item 1) — UPSC Prelims 2018.** *"With reference to India's satellite launch vehicles, consider the following statements: 1. PSLVs launch satellites useful for Earth resources monitoring whereas GSLVs are designed mainly to launch communication satellites. 2. Satellites launched by PSLV appear to remain permanently fixed in the same position in the sky, as viewed from a particular location on Earth. 3. GSLV Mk III is a four-staged launch vehicle..."* — answer: **statement 1 only**. Statement 2 fails precisely because geostationary satellites are launched by GSLV, not PSLV. EOS-05 is the interesting exception that proves the rule: an *Earth-observation* payload going up on a *GSLV*, because the orbit — not the payload type — dictates the vehicle.
- **Kakatiyas (Item 4) — UPSC Prelims 2017.** *"Which one of the following was a very important seaport in the Kakatiya kingdom?"* — options Kakinada / Motupalli / Machilipatnam / Nellore; answer **Motupalli**, the port covered by Ganapati Deva's *Abhaya Shasanam* charter guaranteeing merchant security and fixed customs duties. UPSC has repeatedly attacked the Kakatiyas through geography-of-power rather than dynastic lists.
- **Statistical indices (Item 3) — UPSC Prelims 2015.** *"In the 'Index of Eight Core Industries', which one of the following is given the highest weight?"* — answer as keyed: **Electricity generation** (note that refinery products carry the largest weight in the current 2011-12 series; the keyed answer reflects the base then in use). The lesson for the ISP: UPSC asks about **composition and weights** of indices, not their monthly readings. Learn what the ISP covers and where its numbers come from, not the June 2026 growth rates.

## Part C — Answers & Explanations

**Q1 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: EOS-05/GISAT-1A is meant for geostationary/geosynchronous orbit, and its selling point is a revisit interval of the order of minutes over the same region. Statement 3 is wrong on two counts — the mission is **GSLV-F17**, and PSLV does not launch *all* Indian Earth-observation payloads.

**Q2 — (b).** A country ceiling is the highest rating ordinarily assignable to the foreign-currency obligations of issuers domiciled in a country; JCR raised India's to 'A'. (a) describes ECB limits, (c) the FRBM framework, (d) FPI investment limits — none is a country ceiling.

**Q3 — (b).** Statements 1 and 3 are correct — the ISP draws on GST outward-supply data, administrative records and ASISSE, and gives a monthly reading for the **formal** services sector. Statement 2 is wrong: the ISP's base year is **2024-25**, while the current IIP series is on **2022-23**.

**Q4 — (b).** Gollalagudi is at **Palampet, Mulugu district, Telangana** — the same village as the **Rudreshwara (Ramappa) Temple**, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2021. Hampi and Pattadakal are in Karnataka; the Great Living Chola Temples are in Tamil Nadu.

**Q5 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Statement 3 is wrong: Vizhinjam lies near Thiruvananthapuram on Kerala's **Arabian Sea** coast, not the Bay of Bengal.

**Q6 — (a).** Statements 1 and 2 are correct: a geostationary satellite must orbit in the equatorial plane with a period of one sidereal day (23 h 56 m), which from `T² = 4π²r³/GM` gives r ≈ 42,164 km from Earth's centre (≈ 35,786 km altitude). Statement 3 is wrong — orbital speed falls as radius rises (v = √(GM/r)); a GEO satellite moves at roughly 3.07 km/s against about 7.8 km/s in LEO.

**Q7 — (c).** All three are correct. Warangal (Orugallu) was the Kakatiya capital; Motupalli was its principal seaport (UPSC Prelims 2017); and the Ramappa Temple at Palampet was built in 1213 CE during the reign of **Ganapati Deva**, by his general Recharla Rudra.
