## Passage 1

Our ordinary moral thinking rests on a principle that seems beyond dispute: a person may be judged only for what lies within their control. Circumstances that no one chooses — parentage, temperament, the accidents of a given afternoon — are supposed to be morally inert. Yet the judgements we actually make betray this principle at every turn. Two drivers are equally negligent; one arrives home without incident, the other strikes a child who steps into the road. The difference between them is a matter of timing over which neither had any command, and yet we do not treat them alike. The second is prosecuted, reviled, and — more tellingly — regards himself as having done something the first did not. His remorse is not a confusion to be argued away; it answers to something real.

The difficulty deepens when we notice how far the contagion spreads. The courage a person never had occasion to display, the corruption a comfortable life never tempted them into, the convictions that were formed before they were old enough to examine them: each is a case where fortune supplies the raw material on which character is later judged. Strip away everything that luck contributed and there may be no agent left to assess.

Two responses are available, and neither is comfortable. We may hold the principle and abandon the practice, conceding that nearly all our moral judgements are unjust — an austere conclusion that no society has ever managed to live by. Or we may keep the practice and admit that the principle was always an idealisation, a picture of agency we find flattering rather than one we could act upon. The honest course, perhaps, is to hold both and refuse to resolve the tension.

### Questions

**1. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Moral judgement should be reformed so that people are held answerable only for what they genuinely control.
(b) The principle that we judge only what is controlled and our actual practice of judging cannot be reconciled, and the honest response is to acknowledge the conflict rather than dissolve it.
(c) Because luck shapes conduct so pervasively, nearly all moral judgement is unjust and ought to be withheld.
(d) The remorse of the unlucky driver shows that outcomes matter more to morality than intentions do.

**2. The author's remark that the unlucky driver's remorse "is not a confusion to be argued away" rests on which of the following assumptions?**

(a) A moral feeling that is widely shared cannot be mistaken.
(b) The moral responses people actually have carry evidential weight about what morality requires of us.
(c) The two drivers differed, on closer inspection, in the degree of their negligence.
(d) Remorse is proportionate to the harm a person's conduct has caused.

**3. The tone of the passage is best described as**

(a) detached amusement at a puzzle with no practical stakes.
(b) troubled and even-handed, pressing a difficulty the author does not claim to have solved.
(c) indignant at the injustice of existing moral practice and insistent on its reform.
(d) dismissive of the control principle as a philosophers' invention.

**4. The author would most likely DISAGREE with which of the following statements?**

(a) A person's character is partly the product of circumstances they did not choose.
(b) Once we recognise how much of conduct is owed to luck, the practice of blaming others can be discarded without real loss.
(c) A moral principle that no society has managed to live by is, for that reason, worth scrutinising.
(d) The remorse of a person whose negligence happened to cause harm is not simply irrational.

## Passage 2

International institutions are defended on two grounds that are usually run together but that pull apart under pressure. The first is effectiveness: a body is worth having because it solves problems states cannot solve alone — coordinating vaccine supply, policing trade disputes, financing recovery after a currency collapse. The second is legitimacy: a body deserves authority because those subject to its decisions have some claim on how they are made. Where these coincide, the institution flourishes. Where they diverge, the institution is asked to choose, and the choice is rarely announced.

Consider the design of bodies with weighted voting. Concentrating decision-making in a handful of large contributors produces speed and, often, competence; it also means the states most affected by a ruling may have had the least say in it. The arrangement is defended as a bargain: influence for those who supply the resources. But bargains struck in one distribution of power become awkward when power shifts, and an institution that resists renegotiation begins to look less like a solution to a collective problem and more like a monument to an earlier one.

The temptation is to treat compliance as evidence that all is well. It is not. States comply for many reasons — habit, dependence, the sheer cost of exit — and a record of obedience tells us little about whether an arrangement is regarded as rightful. The clearer indicator is what happens when compliance becomes expensive. Institutions that command genuine assent survive their own bad decisions; those that command only convenience are abandoned the moment a cheaper alternative appears. Judged by that test, the proliferation of regional and plurilateral arrangements is less an addition to the existing order than a verdict on it.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) International institutions should widen participation in decision-making so as to reflect current distributions of power.
(b) Effectiveness and legitimacy are distinct grounds of institutional authority, and the strain between them shows itself when compliance turns costly.
(c) Weighted voting is an indefensible arrangement that has outlived whatever usefulness it once had.
(d) Compliance with international rules has fallen as states have discovered cheaper alternatives.

**6. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**

(a) An institution with a long record of compliance may nonetheless lack legitimacy.
(b) An institution that is effective for long enough will in time come to be seen as legitimate.
(c) Weighted voting invariably undermines the legitimacy of the body that adopts it.
(d) States that abandon an international institution do so because they have judged it ineffective.

**7. The author's claim that the spread of regional and plurilateral arrangements is "a verdict" on the existing order assumes that**

(a) regional arrangements handle collective problems more effectively than global ones do.
(b) states would not be building alternatives if the existing institutions commanded their genuine assent.
(c) the number of institutions in a system is a reliable measure of that system's health.
(d) exit from an international institution carries no appreciable cost.

**8. The author would most likely AGREE with which of the following?**

(a) An institution that survives a decision widely thought to have been mistaken has some claim to legitimacy.
(b) The resources a state contributes are the fairest available basis for allocating influence within an institution.
(c) Legitimacy becomes a concern only where an institution has failed to deliver results.
(d) A shift in the distribution of power is by itself sufficient reason to dissolve an institution.

---

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The closing paragraph names both horns and refuses to pick: "hold both and refuse to resolve the tension."
*Closest wrong — (c):* this is only the first horn, which the author explicitly sets aside as one "no society has ever managed to live by."

**2 → (b).** The remark treats the driver's felt response as data about morality rather than as an error to be corrected.
*Closest wrong — (a):* the author never claims widely shared feelings are infallible — only that this particular one answers to something real.

**3 → (b).** The passage calls both available responses "uncomfortable" and offers its resolution with a hedging "perhaps."
*Closest wrong — (c):* the injustice charge belongs to one horn the author declines to endorse; the register is puzzled, not indignant.

**4 → (b).** The author insists the unlucky driver's remorse is "not a confusion to be argued away" — blame is not discardable without loss.
*Closest wrong — (c):* tempting because the author does not call for discarding unlivable principles, but he treats unlivability as precisely the reason to scrutinise the control principle, so he would agree with (c).

**5 → (b).** The thesis is set out in the first paragraph (two grounds that "pull apart under pressure") and tested in the last (what happens "when compliance becomes expensive").
*Closest wrong — (a):* a reform the passage implies but never advances; the argument is diagnostic, not prescriptive.

**6 → (a).** "A record of obedience tells us little about whether an arrangement is regarded as rightful" licenses exactly this.
*Closest wrong — (d):* the passage attributes abandonment to the appearance of a cheaper alternative, i.e. convenience, not to a judgement about effectiveness.

**7 → (b).** The inference from "states are building alternatives" to "a verdict on the order" only works if genuine assent would have kept them in.
*Closest wrong — (a):* the passage nowhere compares regional and global bodies on performance; its criterion throughout is assent, not competence.

**8 → (a).** "Institutions that command genuine assent survive their own bad decisions" — surviving a bad decision is the passage's own test of legitimacy.
*Closest wrong — (b):* this is the defence *offered* for weighted voting, which the author immediately proceeds to question; a reported position, not the author's.

## 10-second reading tip

On **assumption** stems, don't look for something the passage said — look for the unstated bridge between the evidence given and the conclusion drawn. Find the conclusion's verb ("is a verdict on it"), find the fact offered for it ("regional arrangements are proliferating"), and ask: *what must be true for that fact to support that claim?* Options that merely restate the passage are decoys; the answer is always the sentence the author needed but never wrote.
