**Theme:** Passage 1 - Environment (the incoherence of 'naturalness' as a conservation baseline) - Passage 2 - Polity/Governance (rules, discretion and the meaning of accountability) - **Target time:** ~16-18 min (weekly double set) - Read once, then attack the stems.

## Passage 1

> Few ideas have shaped environmental policy as quietly as the notion that conservation means restoring nature to the state it would have occupied had we not interfered. The appeal is obvious: it seems to supply an objective standard, one that settles disputes by appeal to fact rather than to preference. Yet the standard dissolves under examination. Ecosystems do not possess a single state to which they tend; they are historical accidents, shaped by contingencies of climate, migration and catastrophe, and they would have gone on changing had no human ever arrived. To designate one moment in that sequence as the authentic condition is to make a choice, not to discover a fact - and the moment usually chosen turns out to be the one just before the arrival of whoever is currently doing the choosing.
>
> Nor does the difficulty end there. Species now move, hybridise and assemble themselves into communities that have no historical precedent, and many of these novel assemblages are productive, resilient and rich. The restorationist must either condemn them as counterfeit or concede that the criterion of authenticity has quietly been abandoned.
>
> None of this licenses the conclusion that conservation is arbitrary, or that one landscape is as good as another. It shows only that the work of justification cannot be evaded by an appeal to nature. If we protect a wetland, it must be because we value what the wetland does, or what it means to those who live beside it, and we must be prepared to say so and to defend that claim against competitors. The appeal to naturalness is not an argument but a way of declining to make one - and arguments that are never made are also never won.

## Passage 2

> Every scandal in public administration produces the same remedy: another rule. The reasoning is intuitive. An official exercised judgement, the judgement proved disastrous, and the obvious way to prevent a repetition is to remove the judgement - to specify in advance what shall be done, so that the next official in that chair has nothing left to decide. Repeated over decades, this logic produces the modern manual: thousands of provisions, each the fossil of some earlier failure.
>
> The cost of this accumulation is rarely counted, because it never appears as a scandal. A rule written to prevent one kind of error is necessarily blind to the particulars of cases its author never imagined, and the official who applies it to such a case does no wrong that any inquiry will recognise. He has complied. If the outcome is absurd, the absurdity is attributed to the system, which is to say to nobody. Discretion, by contrast, leaves fingerprints: the officer who decides is the officer who can be blamed. Faced with that asymmetry, a prudent official will prefer the defensible error to the risky judgement, and will do so more often as the manual thickens.
>
> What has gone wrong is not the desire for accountability but its impoverished form. To hold someone accountable for compliance is to ask only whether a box was ticked; to hold them accountable for their reasons is to ask whether the decision made sense in the circumstances they actually faced, and to require that those circumstances be recorded and that reasoning disclosed. The second is harder to administer and harder to audit. It is also the only version that leaves an administrator with anything worth being accountable for.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Administrative rules should be replaced by official discretion wherever this is practically possible.

(b) Accountability understood as procedural compliance pushes officials towards defensible but poor decisions; accountability for reasons is the sounder standard.

(c) Public scandals are an unreliable guide to what is actually wrong with an administrative system.

(d) Administrative rules multiply chiefly because officials are afraid of being blamed for their own judgements.

**6. The author's argument rests on which one of the following assumptions?**

(a) Officials adjust their conduct in response to the incentives created by the way that conduct will later be reviewed.

(b) Most administrative rules were poorly drafted when they were first written.

(c) Officials allowed discretion generally reach better outcomes than the application of rules would produce.

(d) Public inquiries into administrative failure are usually conducted in bad faith.

**7. The tone of the passage is best described as**

(a) analytical and critical, yet not dismissive of the purpose the rules are meant to serve

(b) indignant and polemical, treating administrative rules as an unmitigated evil

(c) detached and neutral, setting out both positions without evaluating either

(d) resigned, presenting the problem as one beyond any practical remedy

**8. The author would most likely DISAGREE with which one of the following statements?**

(a) An official who has followed the applicable rule has fully discharged his responsibility even where the result is plainly perverse.

(b) Rules tend to multiply because each recognised failure invites the drafting of a further provision.

(c) Auditing the reasons behind decisions is more burdensome than auditing compliance with rules.

(d) An official who exercises judgement is more exposed to blame than one who merely applies a rule.

## Answers

**1. (b)** - The passage dismantles the appeal to naturalness and closes by insisting that protection must be justified by what we value and defended against competing claims.

*Closest trap:* (d) fastens on a subsidiary jibe and prescribes 'better science', but the author's point is that no scientific baseline can do normative work at all.

**2. (a)** - The whole case turns on the move from 'the baseline must be chosen' to 'the baseline cannot settle the dispute' - which holds only on this assumption.

*Closest trap:* (d) is never claimed; the author disputes not our ability to reconstruct past states but the idea that knowing them tells us which to prefer.

**3. (a)** - If the baseline is a choice rather than a fact, factual agreement leaves the normative question open - exactly the author's thesis.

*Closest trap:* (b) converts a claim about justification into a claim about practical failure; the author never says restoration cannot succeed technically.

**4. (a)** - This is the passage's closing claim: the appeal to naturalness 'is not an argument but a way of declining to make one'.

*Closest trap:* (b) is explicitly disowned - 'None of this licenses the conclusion that conservation is arbitrary'.

**5. (b)** - The passage traces the pathology of rule-accumulation and ends by proposing accountability for reasons as the corrective.

*Closest trap:* (d) states a consequence the passage describes - blame-avoidance by officials - and mistakes it for the cause of rule-making, which the passage attributes to the reflex response to scandal.

**6. (a)** - The claim that a prudent official 'will prefer the defensible error to the risky judgement' makes sense only if review shapes behaviour.

*Closest trap:* (c) is stronger than the argument needs: the author claims rules cannot anticipate every case, not that discretion outperforms rules on average.

**7. (a)** - The author diagnoses sharply but concedes that 'what has gone wrong is not the desire for accountability' and offers a remedy.

*Closest trap:* (b) overreads the sharpness; the passage defends the underlying aim of accountability rather than condemning rules outright.

**8. (a)** - The passage's target is precisely the regime in which 'He has complied' settles the matter while the absurdity is charged to nobody.

*Closest trap:* (c) is asserted by the author himself - 'harder to administer and harder to audit' - so it cannot be the point of disagreement.

---

**10-second reading tip:** Before you look at the options, compress the passage into one sentence of your own - and if the author is correcting a common view, force it into the shape *"not X, but Y"*. Half the traps in a central-idea question are accurate statements of the X half alone.
