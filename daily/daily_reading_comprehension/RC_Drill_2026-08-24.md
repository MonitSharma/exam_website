## Passage 1

For most of the twentieth century, what an economy produced could, in principle, be weighed. National accounts grew up around factories, and their instruments were calibrated to a world in which value arrived in crates. Today the most valuable assets of the largest firms are of a different kind: software, brands, protocols, tacit organisational know-how. The familiar complaint is that our statistics have not caught up — that the measuring apparatus lags the thing measured, and that with better surveys and bolder imputations the gap would close. This diagnosis is comforting because it makes the difficulty technical.

It is, I think, mistaken. The trouble with intangible capital is not that it hides from the statistician but that it behaves differently. A lathe can be in one factory at a time; a piece of code can be everywhere at once, at almost no additional cost. Assets of the second sort do not wear out in any ordinary way, cannot be reliably valued by what they cost to produce, and yield returns that depend less on the asset itself than on the scale of the network attached to it. Where price no longer tracks the cost of producing one more unit, the arithmetic on which national accounting rests quietly stops meaning what it once did. Aggregate output can then rise while measured productivity stagnates, and both figures can be correct.

None of this implies that measurement should be abandoned, or that the statistics are worthless. It implies something less consoling: that part of what we want the numbers to tell us — how much better off we are than we were last year — was always parasitic on an economy of things, and cannot be recovered by refining the survey.

## Passage 2

Consider a policy whose benefits are enjoyed now and whose costs fall two centuries hence. It seems obvious that we wrong those who will bear the costs. The obvious becomes slippery under inspection. Large policies do not merely affect future people; they determine which future people exist. Alter the pattern of a society's work, travel and settlement and, within a few generations, a wholly different set of individuals is born — different meetings, different marriages, different moments of conception. The people who inherit our depleted world are not people who would otherwise have inherited a better one. They are people who, had we chosen otherwise, would never have existed at all.

From this a disturbing argument follows. To wrong someone, it is usually said, is to make them worse off than they would otherwise have been. But there is no available state of affairs in which these particular individuals are better off; the alternative to their diminished lives is not better lives but no lives. If their existence is on balance worth having, they have no complaint to bring. And if nobody is wronged, it is hard to see what our obligation consists in.

The conclusion is intolerable, and rightly so; but the correct response is not to patch the argument. It is to notice what the argument reveals. Our duties regarding the future were never owed to a set of identifiable claimants waiting to be harmed. They concern the quality of the lives that will exist, whoever turns out to live them. An ethics of this kind is impersonal — and it is uncomfortable precisely because we are accustomed to thinking that a wrong requires someone in particular to have been wronged.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Because our choices determine who will exist, we cannot meaningfully be said to wrong future generations.
(b) Future people have no legitimate complaint against us so long as their lives are on balance worth living.
(c) The non-identity puzzle should lead us to reconceive our duties to the future as impersonal rather than to abandon them.
(d) Our intuitions about harm are unreliable and should be set aside when reasoning about the distant future.

**6. The argument set out in the second paragraph rests on which one of the following assumptions?**

(a) The identity of a person is fixed by the particular moment of their conception.
(b) An act wrongs a person only if it leaves that person worse off than an available alternative would have.
(c) A life worth living is always preferable to no life at all.
(d) Future generations will in fact live worse lives than the present one.

**7. The tone of the passage is best described as**

(a) analytical, willing to follow a troubling argument and then reinterpret what it shows.
(b) sceptical of the very idea that we owe anything to those not yet born.
(c) alarmed and exhortatory about the depletion of resources.
(d) detached, declining to take a position on how the puzzle should be resolved.

**8. With which one of the following statements would the author be most likely to AGREE?**

(a) If a life is worth living, the circumstances into which it is born raise no moral question.
(b) Our obligations to the future are owed to specific individuals who will one day be in a position to complain.
(c) The non-identity argument shows that our intuitions about harming the future are simply mistaken.
(d) A generation can act wrongly even though no particular person has been made worse off by what it did.

## Answers

**1. (b)** — The passage explicitly rejects the 'lag' diagnosis as one that 'makes the difficulty technical' and argues instead that intangibles behave differently, so the arithmetic itself stops meaning what it did.

*Closest trap:* (d) fails because the author says aggregate output rising while measured productivity stagnates can leave *both* figures correct — not that one is an illusion.

**2. (d)** — The pivotal sentence — 'Where price no longer tracks the cost of producing one more unit, the arithmetic ... quietly stops meaning what it once did' — is an inference only if that relation is what made the arithmetic meaningful.

*Closest trap:* (b) is tempting because it sounds like the background of the piece, but the argument bites at whatever the current share is; it needs no claim about the future trend.

**3. (a)** — If output can rise while measured productivity stagnates with both figures correct, the stagnant figure cannot on its own settle what is happening to innovation.

*Closest trap:* (c) overshoots a position the author disowns in the final paragraph — 'None of this implies that measurement should be abandoned'.

**4. (c)** — This is precisely the 'comforting' technical diagnosis the second paragraph sets out to refute, and the closing line says the loss 'cannot be recovered by refining the survey'.

*Closest trap:* (b) reads like a concession the author might resist, but he grants it outright: the statistics are not 'worthless'.

**5. (c)** — The third paragraph rejects the sceptical conclusion as 'intolerable' and relocates the duty: it concerns 'the quality of the lives that will exist, whoever turns out to live them'.

*Closest trap:* (b) is a faithful restatement of one step inside the argument the author is dismantling, not of the passage's own position.

**6. (b)** — The paragraph opens by stating the counterfactual account of wronging and then observes that no better alternative exists for these very individuals; without that account the inference collapses.

*Closest trap:* (a) is genuinely relied on — but by the FIRST paragraph, to establish that different policies yield different people; the stem asks what the second paragraph's inference needs.

**7. (a)** — The author develops the sceptical argument in its strongest form, calls its conclusion intolerable, and then offers a positive reconstruction — analysis followed by commitment.

*Closest trap:* (d) mistakes the even temper of the prose for neutrality; the closing paragraph plainly commits to an impersonal ethics.

**8. (d)** — This is the impersonal conclusion the passage arrives at: a wrong need not have 'someone in particular to have been wronged'.

*Closest trap:* (c) inverts the author's verdict — he treats the intolerable conclusion, not the intuition, as the thing that must give way.

---

**10-second reading tip:** When a passage says *"the usual explanation is X"*, underline X and expect the author to reject it — then check whether the rejection is "X is false" or the subtler "X is the wrong kind of answer." Central-idea traps almost always offer you a tidy "X is false" when the author actually said the question was misframed.
