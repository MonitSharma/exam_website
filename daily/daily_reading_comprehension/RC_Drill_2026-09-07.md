## Passage 1

The claim that a society is meritocratic is usually offered as praise, and it is worth asking what the praise amounts to. A meritocracy distributes positions according to ability and effort rather than birth, and set against the alternative of inherited rank this is plainly an improvement. But the word carries a second, quieter claim: that those who arrive at the top deserve what they find there. The second claim does not follow from the first. Ability is itself distributed by processes — genetic endowment, early nutrition, the accident of an attentive teacher — over which no one exercised any choice; and effort, the supposedly virtuous residue, is at least partly a function of the confidence and security that make sustained effort possible. A sorting mechanism can be efficient without being just. It can put the right people in the right jobs while leaving entirely open the question of what those people are owed.

The confusion matters most for what it does to those who lose. Under an aristocracy, the man at the bottom could regard his position as an accident of birth and keep his self-respect intact; under a meritocracy he is invited to read the same position as a verdict on his worth. The system thus manufactures a humiliation that the older order, for all its cruelties, did not require. Nor does the winner escape unharmed. Convinced that his advantages are earned, he loses the sense of indebtedness that might otherwise temper his claims upon the common stock. What began as a rule for allocating jobs hardens into a theory of desert, and a society that cannot tell the two apart will find its inequalities both wider and harder to question.

### Questions

- (c) Inequality is wider in a meritocracy than under aristocracy because the successful refuse to contribute to the common stock.

## Passage 2

Environmental policy is routinely defended in the language of risk, and the language flatters us. To speak of risk is to imply that the possible outcomes can be listed and probabilities attached to them, so that the expected cost of acting may be weighed against the expected cost of delay. Much of what we face is not like this. The behaviour of ice sheets, the circulation of the ocean, the threshold at which a forest ceases to absorb carbon and begins to release it — these are systems whose responses are not merely unmeasured but, in the present state of knowledge, unmeasurable in the probabilistic sense. To call such situations risky is to import a false precision, and the precision is not neutral: a number, once produced, tends to be believed, while the outcomes to which no number can be attached drop quietly out of the calculation.

The honest description is uncertainty, and uncertainty demands a different logic. Where the range of outcomes is unknown but is known to include states from which there is no return, the reasonable policy is not the one with the best expected value but the one that keeps the irreversible options open — an insurance principle rather than an investment principle. This is not a licence for alarm. Insurance is bought in proportion to what is at stake, and against catastrophic and irreversible loss it is rational to pay premiums that no expected-value calculation would sanction. The objection that such caution is expensive is true and beside the point: the relevant comparison is not with the cheapest available course, but with the cost of discovering, too late, that the estimate on which we relied was never an estimate at all.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

- (a) Since the costs of caution cannot be compared with the costs of inaction, cost-benefit reasoning has no legitimate place in environmental policy.
- (b) Environmental policy should follow the precautionary principle because scientific estimates of environmental outcomes are usually inaccurate.
- (c) Because key environmental systems present uncertainty rather than quantifiable risk, policy should be designed to guard against irreversible loss rather than to maximise expected value.
- (d) The vocabulary of risk is used by policymakers as a device for postponing action on environmental threats.

**6. The passage's objection to describing environmental threats in the language of risk rests on the assumption that**

- (a) decision-makers are, as a rule, incapable of interpreting statistical evidence correctly
- (b) scientific understanding of ice sheets and ocean circulation is unlikely ever to improve
- (c) probabilities assigned to environmental outcomes are generally miscalculated
- (d) the outcomes that resist quantification are consequential enough that leaving them out distorts the decision

**7. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**

- (a) Expected-value reasoning is an inappropriate guide in every area of public policy.
- (b) A policy that is optimal by expected value may still be unreasonable if the outcomes it omits are irreversible.
- (c) Insurance against environmental catastrophe should be purchased irrespective of its cost.
- (d) Environmental systems will in time be understood well enough for probabilistic estimates to be reliable.

**8. The author would most likely agree that**

- (a) an estimate that wears the appearance of precision may mislead more than an open admission of ignorance
- (b) alarm is the appropriate public response to environmental uncertainty
- (c) insurance is worth buying only where the probability of loss can be established
- (d) the expense of precautionary policy is a decisive objection to adopting it

## Answers

**1 → (a).** The passage separates two claims bundled into the word 'meritocracy' — an efficient rule of allocation and a theory of desert — and its whole argument is that the second does not follow from the first and does the damage.

*Closest wrong — (d): true of the passage's opening concession, but the passage grants this in one line in order to attack what comes after it; a concession is not the central idea.*

**2 → (c).** The contrast turns entirely on the story each order tells the loser — 'accident of birth' versus 'verdict on his worth'. Without the assumption that such accounts reach self-respect, the two positions would be indistinguishable and no humiliation would be manufactured.

*Closest wrong — (b): the passage explicitly refuses this, conceding the older order's 'cruelties'; it claims only that humiliation was not required, not that aristocracy was kinder.*

**3 → (d).** This is precisely the inference the passage is written to block: the second, 'quieter claim' of desert, which the author says 'does not follow' because ability and effort are themselves unchosen.

*Closest wrong — (c): tempting because it sounds like a concession the author might resist, but the passage endorses it — the winner's error is losing 'the sense of indebtedness'.*

**4 → (b).** The author concedes the case for meritocracy ('plainly an improvement'), reasons in the register of 'does not follow' and 'at least partly', yet is plainly unsettled by what the doctrine does to winners and losers alike.

*Closest wrong — (d): the subject matter invites indignation, but the prose argues rather than denounces; there is no invective and no call to arms.*

**5 → (c).** The passage's structure is a distinction (risk versus uncertainty) followed by its policy consequence (an insurance principle rather than an investment principle, aimed at keeping irreversible options open).

*Closest wrong — (b): the complaint is not that the numbers come out wrong but that in these systems no probability can properly be produced at all — a different and stronger objection.*

**6 → (d).** The charge that unquantified outcomes 'drop quietly out of the calculation' is damning only if those outcomes matter; the author's insistence that they include states 'from which there is no return' shows the assumption at work.

*Closest wrong — (c): this concedes that probabilities are the right instrument and merely disputes their accuracy, whereas the passage denies that the probabilistic frame applies to these systems in the first place.*

**7 → (b).** This follows directly from the two moves made in the second paragraph: unquantifiable outcomes are excluded from the calculation, and where those outcomes are irreversible the expected-value criterion is the wrong one.

*Closest wrong — (a): overgeneralises. The passage restricts its argument to situations of deep uncertainty with irreversible states, and its own analogy — investment versus insurance — presumes expected-value reasoning is fine elsewhere.*

**8 → (a).** The closing sentence makes exactly this point: the danger is discovering too late that 'the estimate on which we relied was never an estimate at all' — false precision is worse than acknowledged uncertainty.

*Closest wrong — (b): the passage does warn of catastrophe, but explicitly disowns alarm — 'This is not a licence for alarm' — and pitches its case in the sober language of insurance.*

## 10-second reading tip

**When a passage concedes something in its first two lines, that concession is never the answer.** A UPSC author who opens with "this is plainly an improvement" or "the language flatters us" is clearing ground for the real thesis, which arrives immediately after the pivot word — *but*, *however*, *and yet*. Mark the pivot on your first read; the central-idea option almost always paraphrases the sentence just after it, while the most tempting distractor paraphrases the sentence just before.
