## Passage 1

Sovereignty is usually described as a possession: a state either has it or does not. The description is convenient and misleading. What we call sovereignty is a bundle of distinct entitlements — to make law within a territory, to be free of external coercion, to represent one's population abroad — and history shows these entitlements coming apart far more often than they travel together. States have leased ports, accepted foreign judges, submitted their budgets to external auditors, and remained states. The bundle is habitually unpacked and redistributed, and the vocabulary of possession conceals this.

The concealment matters when the question turns to intervention. Debate is conducted as though the choice lay between respecting sovereignty and overriding it, with the first presumptively lawful and the second requiring extraordinary justification. But if sovereignty is a bundle, the interesting question is never whether to intervene but which entitlement is being suspended, on whose authority, and for how long. A trade sanction, a conditional loan, an arms embargo and an air campaign all abridge some strand of the bundle; they differ in which strand, and in how visibly. Treating only the last as intervention flatters the others by leaving them unnamed.

Nothing in this argument makes intervention easier to justify. If anything it makes justification harder, since it denies interveners the comfort of a bright line to point at — the claim that what they are doing is categorically different from war because no soldier has crossed a border. It requires them to say what they are taking, and to accept that the taking is a matter of degree rather than of kind. That is an uncomfortable position for great powers and a clarifying one for everyone else.

## Passage 2

There is an old assumption in the sciences that to predict a phenomenon reliably is to understand it, and that the two capacities grow together. A theory earns its keep by telling us what will happen; a theory that tells us correctly must have got hold of something real. For most of the history of physics the assumption was harmless, because the models that predicted well were also the models one could read — a few equations, a handful of terms, each answering to something in the world.

Recent practice has driven a wedge between the two. Systems trained on very large bodies of data now forecast protein structures, storm tracks and equipment failures with an accuracy older methods could not approach, while offering nothing that resembles an account of why. Their internal parameters number in the millions and correspond to nothing one can name. The predictions are excellent; the explanation is absent. Faced with this, some argue that understanding was always an aesthetic preference — a human demand for a story, satisfied by simplicity rather than by truth — and that a science which predicts everything and explains nothing has lost only a comfort.

The response is too quick. Understanding is not merely pleasant; it is what allows us to say when a model will fail. A theory whose terms refer tells us the conditions under which it applies, and therefore warns us at the boundary. A system that has only absorbed regularities has no such boundary to report, and will extrapolate past it with the same confidence it had inside. The demand for explanation is not nostalgia for narrative. It is the practical question of how much weight an instrument will bear.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

**6. The author's argument in the final paragraph rests on the assumption that**

(b) models with fewer parameters are more likely to be true than models with many.
(c) the accuracy of data-driven systems declines as the data on which they were trained grows older.

**7. Which one of the following is a valid inference from the passage?**

(a) Predictive success within a familiar range is not, by itself, a guide to performance outside it.
(b) Older physical theories never failed within the circumstances they were designed to describe.
(c) The parameters of large trained systems could be made interpretable given sufficient effort.
(d) Phenomena such as storm tracks and protein structures resist explanation in principle.

**8. The author's attitude towards the claim that understanding is merely an aesthetic preference is best described as**

(a) dismissive of it as self-evidently absurd.
(b) reasoned disagreement with a position the author has first stated fairly.
(c) cautious sympathy tempered by practical misgivings.
(d) neutral exposition of a position on which the author takes no side.

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The passage's whole move is from "possession" to "bundle," and the payoff is that intervention becomes a question of *which* strand is abridged, "a matter of degree rather than of kind."
*Closest wrong — (c):* tempting because sanctions and air campaigns are listed together, but the passage says they "differ in which strand, and in how visibly" — parallel in kind of act, not equivalent in weight.

**2 → (b).** "Flatters… by leaving them unnamed" only works if being unnamed buys an exemption from scrutiny; that is the unstated bridge.
*Closest wrong — (a):* "flatters" carries a hint of undeserved leniency, so magnitude feels relevant — but the passage never compares harms, and the argument survives even if sanctions hurt far less.

**3 → (b).** The author explicitly mocks "the claim that what they are doing is categorically different from war because no soldier has crossed a border."
*Closest wrong — (a):* directly contradicted — states that accepted foreign judges "remained states"; the bundle can be unpacked without sovereignty vanishing.

**4 → (b).** "Nothing in this argument makes intervention easier to justify. If anything it makes justification harder."
*Closest wrong — (a):* superficially attractive if one reads the author as defending conventional vocabulary, but he says the opposite — the vocabulary of possession "conceals."

**5 → (c).** The final paragraph converts explanation from a taste into a safety instrument: it tells you "when a model will fail."
*Closest wrong — (b):* half-right and therefore dangerous — the author concedes the old assumption was *harmless* in early physics because readable models happened to predict well, not that the link was always illusory.

**6 → (a).** The argument only bites if the absence of a self-reported boundary leaves the user genuinely without warning; if failure could be detected by other means, losing explanation would cost little.
*Closest wrong — (b):* the passage praises "a few equations, a handful of terms," so parsimony seems to be the virtue — but the stated virtue is *reference* ("terms refer"), not fewness.

**7 → (a).** A system with no boundary to report "will extrapolate past it with the same confidence it had inside" — so interior accuracy carries no information about exterior performance.
*Closest wrong — (d):* the passage says current systems *offer* no explanation, which is a fact about the method, not a claim that the phenomena are inexplicable.

**8 → (b).** "The response is too quick" is a judgement of insufficiency, and the author then supplies a counter-argument rather than a rebuke; he also states the opposing view in its strongest form first.
*Closest wrong — (a):* "too quick" reads as impatience, but impatience with an argument's speed is not the claim that it is absurd — the author takes it seriously enough to answer it.

## 10-second reading tip

When a stem asks what the author **assumes**, don't hunt for a sentence in the passage — assumptions are by definition unwritten. Find the evidence and the conclusion, then ask what silent link must hold for one to deliver the other. Any option that merely restates the evidence, or restates the conclusion, is a distractor.
