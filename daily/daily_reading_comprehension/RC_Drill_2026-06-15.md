## Passage

Tolerance is often praised as a civic virtue, yet the praise frequently rests on a misunderstanding of what tolerance demands. To tolerate is not merely to refrain from interfering with what one dislikes; mere non-interference may spring from indifference, fatigue, or an inability to act, none of which carry moral weight. Genuine tolerance presupposes disapproval: one cannot be said to tolerate what one welcomes or regards as a matter of no consequence. It is precisely the coexistence of a settled objection with a deliberate decision not to suppress that gives the virtue its peculiar tension. A society that has ceased to care about how its members live has not become more tolerant; it has merely become more apathetic, and apathy, masquerading as openness, erodes the very convictions that make tolerance meaningful.

This account has an uncomfortable implication. If tolerance requires sustained disapproval, then a thoroughly relativist culture — one persuaded that no way of life is better or worse than another — cannot be tolerant at all, for it has nothing to tolerate. The relativist's celebrated broad-mindedness is, on this view, a counterfeit. What looks like generosity of spirit is the absence of any standard against which generosity could be measured. The truly tolerant person, by contrast, holds firm beliefs and yet restrains the impulse to impose them, recognising that the cost of coercion — to dignity, to autonomy, to social peace — outweighs the satisfaction of being proved right.

Tolerance, then, is not the lowering of conviction but its disciplined accompaniment. It flourishes not where belief is weakest, but where belief is strong enough to be exercised with restraint.

## Questions

**1. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Tolerance is a civic virtue best secured by a policy of non-interference in others' lives.
(b) Genuine tolerance involves holding a real objection to something while deliberately choosing not to suppress it.
(c) Relativism offers the most secure foundation for a peaceful and broad-minded society.
(d) Strong moral convictions inevitably drive people to coerce those who disagree with them.

**2. The author's claim that a thoroughly relativist culture "cannot be tolerant at all" rests primarily on the assumption that:**

(a) relativists are secretly indifferent to all questions of morality.
(b) tolerance necessarily requires an object of which one disapproves.
(c) every society contains people with sharply conflicting ways of life.
(d) apathy poses a greater danger to a society than coercion does.

**3. With which one of the following statements would the author most likely AGREE?**

(a) A person who feels no disapproval whatever toward a practice is not, strictly speaking, tolerating it.
(b) The more practices a society tolerates, the weaker its moral convictions are bound to become.
(c) Coercing others is justified whenever one is genuinely certain that one's own beliefs are correct.
(d) Indifference and tolerance, whatever their motives, are indistinguishable in their effects on society.

**4. The author's attitude toward "the relativist's celebrated broad-mindedness" is best described as:**

(a) admiring
(b) neutral and purely descriptive
(c) skeptical and critical
(d) hostile and contemptuous

---

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The passage defines true tolerance as the tension between a "settled objection" and "a deliberate decision not to suppress."
*Closest wrong — (a): non-interference is explicitly rejected as possibly mere indifference, "none of which carry moral weight."*

**2 → (b).** The relativist "has nothing to tolerate" only because the author has assumed tolerance must have an object one disapproves of.
*Closest wrong — (a): the author calls relativist broad-mindedness a "counterfeit," not a secret indifference; the argument turns on the definition of tolerance, not on relativists' hidden motives.*

**3 → (a).** Directly supported: "one cannot be said to tolerate what one welcomes or regards as a matter of no consequence."
*Closest wrong — (d): the author treats apathy and tolerance as crucially different — apathy "erodes the very convictions that make tolerance meaningful" — not indistinguishable.*

**4 → (c).** "Counterfeit" and "absence of any standard" signal disapproval, but the tone stays measured and analytical.
*Closest wrong — (d): "hostile and contemptuous" is too extreme — the author critiques the position calmly, without scorn for the people holding it.*

## 10-second reading tip

On "most likely AGREE/DISAGREE" stems, don't hunt for a sentence you *like* — eliminate options with extreme words (*inevitably, always, indistinguishable, whenever, never*). UPSC's trap answers usually overstate a claim the passage only made cautiously; the surviving option is almost always the modest one.
