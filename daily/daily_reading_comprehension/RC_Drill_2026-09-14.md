## Passage 1

The transfer of authority from a central government to local bodies is routinely described as a deepening of democracy, and the description is allowed to pass without much scrutiny. It rests on a spatial intuition: that power exercised nearer to those it affects is power more easily answered to. The intuition is not foolish, but it conflates two distinct things — the distance at which a decision is made, and the capacity of those affected to contest it. Proximity multiplies the occasions for contact between the ruler and the ruled; whether those occasions become moments of accountability depends on what else is true of the locality. Where landholding is concentrated, where caste or clan supplies a ready instrument of coordination, and where the poor depend for credit, work and protection on the very people they would have to challenge, the shortening of distance chiefly shortens the reach of the dominant. A grievance that might once have travelled anonymously to a distant office must now be voiced across a courtyard to a neighbour who knows one's name. Nor is the centre reliably the villain of the story: much of what has historically protected the weak was precisely the remoteness and impersonality of an authority with no stake in local hierarchies. None of this is an argument for centralisation, which carries its own well-catalogued pathologies. It is an argument that devolution redistributes power among existing claimants rather than creating new power in the hands of the many, and that its democratic value is therefore contingent — dependent on whether the local arena has been made contestable first. To devolve into an unreformed locality is not to democratise; it is to change the address at which the same authority is exercised.

**3. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**

(b) Local bodies in unequal societies are invariably captured by dominant groups.

## Passage 2

Economists long treated the informal economy as a waiting room. Enterprises too small to register, workers without contracts, transactions that leave no paper trail — these were read as the residue of an incomplete transition, a sector that would shrink as growth pulled labour into factories and offices. The expectation was not unreasonable; it was simply not borne out. Decades of rapid growth in several economies have left informal employment roughly where it was, and in some the informal share has risen alongside output. That persistence forces a second thought, and the second thought is more uncomfortable than the first. If informality does not dissolve with growth, it may not be a stage at all but a structure: not the part of the economy that modern production has yet to reach, but a condition that modern production actively finds useful. The firm that pushes its variable demand out to unregistered workshops is not failing to modernise; it is modernising in a manner that requires an unprotected periphery. On this reading the formal and the informal are not two sectors at different points on one road, but two faces of a single arrangement, each sustaining the other. The policy implications are awkward. If informality is a waiting room, the remedy is patience and perhaps a lighter registration burden. If it is a structure, then formalisation drives aimed at the small enterprise — the registration counter, the compliance form, the modest loan — leave the arrangement untouched and may simply load costs onto its weakest participants. What would have to change is the relation between the protected core and the unprotected margin, and that is a harder thing to legislate than a licence.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(c) Growth has failed to reduce informal employment in most developing economies, contradicting the predictions of economic theory.

**7. The author's move from the persistence of informality to the conclusion that it is "a structure" rests on the assumption that**

(a) an arrangement that survives the very conditions predicted to dissolve it is probably being sustained by something.
(b) informal enterprises are systematically less productive than formal ones.
(c) governments in these economies have consistently failed to enforce labour regulation.
(d) the informal sector will keep expanding for as long as the formal core remains protected.

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The closing sentences state the thesis outright: devolution redistributes power among existing claimants, so its democratic value is *contingent* on prior local contestability.
*Closest wrong:* (c) is true of the passage but is one supporting illustration (the courtyard), not the argument it serves.

**2 → (b).** The reach of the dominant lengthens only if voicing a grievance to a known neighbour carries a cost the poor cannot absorb — dependence for "credit, work and protection" is exactly that cost.
*Closest wrong:* (d) misreads "no stake in local hierarchies" as indifference to outcomes; the passage credits distant authority with *protecting* the weak, which is the opposite of indifference.

**3 → (a).** If democratic value is contingent on the locality being contestable, then reducing concentrated local economic power is precisely the condition that makes devolution work.
*Closest wrong:* (b) says "invariably." The passage argues contingency, not inevitability — a universal claim cannot be safely inferred from a conditional one.

**4 → (c).** This is the unexamined "spatial intuition" the whole passage is written to qualify.
*Closest wrong:* (a) is what the author in fact holds; test-takers in a hurry pick it because it also sounds critical of devolution.

**5 → (b).** The pivot is "it may not be a stage at all but a structure," and the final paragraph draws the policy consequence: enterprise-level formalisation misses the target.
*Closest wrong:* (c) is the *evidence* that prompts the argument, not the argument; it stops at the failed prediction and never reaches the structural claim.

**6 → (b).** If the formal core depends on an unprotected margin, registration changes the paperwork without changing the relation that produces the conditions.
*Closest wrong:* (d) restates the "waiting room" view the passage explicitly reports as not borne out.

**7 → (a).** The inference from persistence to structure only works if survival despite growth indicates active support rather than mere lag; without it, "not yet reached" remains available.
*Closest wrong:* (d) is a prediction the passage might endorse, not a premise the argument needs — assumptions sit *under* an argument, not after it.

**8 → (b).** The author calls the second thought "more uncomfortable" and the implications "awkward" — evaluative words inside an otherwise cool analysis.
*Closest wrong:* (d) fits the register but ignores those two words; "purely descriptive" is falsified by any evaluative adjective.

---

## 10-second reading tip

When a passage sets out a familiar view and then pivots — "the second thought," "on this reading," "it is an argument that" — the author's thesis is always on the far side of the pivot. Mark the pivot word on first read; the sentence immediately after it answers the central-idea question, and any option that paraphrases the *pre*-pivot view is a planted trap.
