# CSAT RC Drill — 2026-06-09

**Theme: Philosophy of Science / Ethics** · ~8 min

## Passage

It is commonly assumed that the authority of science rests on its freedom from values — that the scientist, unlike the politician or the priest, reports only what *is*, never what *ought* to be. This picture is comforting but misleading. Values enter scientific practice at every turn: in the questions deemed worth asking, in the threshold of evidence demanded before a claim is accepted, in the very decision about which errors are more tolerable than others. A researcher who insists on near-certainty before warning of an environmental hazard has not banished values; she has merely privileged the avoidance of false alarms over the avoidance of missed dangers. The pretence of value-neutrality, far from protecting objectivity, conceals the choices that shape it.

What distinguishes science, then, is not the absence of values but their disciplined exposure. The scientist does not escape judgment; she submits her judgments to a community trained to detect and contest them. Objectivity is thus a social achievement rather than a private virtue — the product of criticism, replication, and the willingness to be proven wrong, not of some impossible neutrality. To demand that science be value-free is to misunderstand both what science is and why we trust it. The trust is warranted precisely *because* the values are accountable, not because they are absent. A science that denied its own commitments would be less trustworthy, not more, for it would have hidden the very junctures at which reasonable people might disagree.

## Questions

**1. Which statement best captures the central idea of the passage?**
A) Science would be more trustworthy if scientists eliminated personal values from their work.
B) The credibility of science derives not from value-neutrality but from the accountable, communal scrutiny of the values it inevitably contains.
C) Scientists are no more objective than politicians or priests.
D) Environmental hazards are routinely underestimated because scientists demand too much certainty.

**2. The author's argument depends most heavily on which assumption?**
A) Scientific communities are generally capable of detecting and contesting hidden value-judgments.
B) Every individual scientist is fully aware of his or her own biases.
C) Politicians and priests never subject their judgments to any scrutiny.
D) Value-free inquiry is logically impossible in every field of human thought.

**3. From the passage, it can be inferred that a scientist who sets a very high evidence threshold before issuing a hazard warning:**
A) is, by that fact, being more objective than one who sets a lower threshold.
B) has made a value-laden choice to risk missing real dangers rather than raising false alarms.
C) is violating the established norms of the scientific community.
D) has succeeded in removing values from her decision.

**4. The author's attitude toward the ideal of "value-free science" is best described as:**
A) reverent
B) indifferent
C) critically skeptical
D) hostile and dismissive

---

## Answers

**1 — B.** The whole passage argues trust comes from values being *accountable*, not absent.
*Closest wrong (C):* the author grants values enter science as elsewhere, but insists science *manages* them — so C ignores the passage's key distinction.

**2 — A.** "Objectivity is a social achievement" only works if the community can actually expose and contest hidden judgments; remove this and the argument collapses.
*Closest wrong (D):* tempting, but the author defends trust in *science* via accountability — he never claims value-free inquiry is logically impossible everywhere.

**3 — B.** Directly stated: a high threshold "privileged the avoidance of false alarms over the avoidance of missed dangers" — a value choice.
*Closest wrong (A):* the passage explicitly denies that a stricter threshold equals greater objectivity.

**4 — C.** The author calls the ideal "comforting but misleading" and corrects it — measured criticism, not contempt.
*Closest wrong (D):* "hostile and dismissive" is too extreme; he engages the view seriously and explains why it fails, rather than dismissing it.

---

## 10-Second Tip

On **tone / attitude** questions, kill the extremes first. Words like *hostile, dismissive, contemptuous, reverent* almost never fit an author who is arguing carefully and conceding points along the way. The answer is usually the moderate option (*critical, skeptical, measured, appreciative*) — so eliminate the loud words before you even re-read.
