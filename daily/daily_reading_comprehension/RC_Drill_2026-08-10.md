## Passage 1

The popular history of science is a history of ideas — Copernicus reordering the heavens, Darwin reordering life. Yet a quieter account would make instruments, not insights, its protagonists. Before the telescope there was no observational problem that Copernican theory could be asked to solve; before the vacuum pump the air had no measurable properties to dispute. Instruments do not merely confirm what theory anticipates; they enlarge the domain of the arguable. Each new device converts some region of the world from the invisible into the contestable, and it is only within that contested region that theory can be tested at all. Scientific progress, on this reading, is less a sequence of intellectual leaps than a slow expansion of the perceptible.

The consequence is uncomfortable for those who prize the autonomy of thought. If what can be known depends on what can be detected, then the direction of inquiry is partly set by the accidents of engineering — by which materials could be ground into lenses, which currents could be stabilised, which crystals happened to fluoresce. The questions a discipline finds tractable are those its apparatus has made answerable, and the questions it neglects may be neglected for no better reason than that nothing yet registers them. Instruments therefore possess a kind of silent authority: they select the phenomena about which disagreement is possible, and thereby set the boundaries of a field's imagination.

None of this makes theory dispensable. An instrument without an interpretive framework produces readings, not results. But it does invert the usual hierarchy. The theorist works within a space of possible observations that someone else's craftsmanship has already delimited, and rarely notices the walls.

## Passage 2

It is often said that modern societies suffer from a decline of trust, and the evidence assembled is usually personal: fewer people report that their neighbours can be relied upon. But this framing mistakes a change of form for a loss of substance. A villager who trusts thirty people by name and distrusts everyone else is not more trusting than a city dweller who boards an aircraft assembled by strangers, eats food inspected by strangers, and moves money through institutions he could not describe. The second has extended trust much further; he has simply stopped locating it in persons.

What makes this possible is not virtue but architecture. Licences, audits, warranties and courts are devices for making strangers predictable, and where they function well the question of a stranger's character need never arise at all. Impersonal trust of this kind is cheap to extend and enormously productive: it permits cooperation among people who will never meet and have no reason to like one another. Its weakness is the mirror of its strength. Because it is invested in systems rather than individuals, it cannot be repaired by the ordinary human means — apology, restitution, long acquaintance. A betrayed friendship may be mended in an afternoon; a discredited inspectorate cannot.

This is why episodes of institutional failure do such disproportionate damage. The failure does not merely remove one relationship; it withdraws the guarantee that had made a whole class of relationships unnecessary. The citizen who learns that certifications were being sold does not thereafter fall back on trusting individuals instead, for he has not the acquaintance to do so. He is left holding neither form of trust, and the transactions that both once supported quietly cease to occur.

### Questions

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Modern societies are measurably less trusting than traditional ones.
(b) Trust has migrated from persons to systems, which vastly widens cooperation but makes institutional failure unusually costly.
(c) Institutions must be regulated more strictly so that public confidence in them is not betrayed.
(d) Impersonal trust is inferior to personal trust because it cannot be repaired once broken.

**6. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**

(a) Traditional societies possessed no mechanisms for dealing with strangers.
(b) Legal institutions have made personal friendship less valuable than it once was.
(c) Institutional failure eventually drives people back towards personal networks.
(d) The absence of visible neighbourly confidence is not by itself evidence that a society trusts less.

**7. The author would most likely DISAGREE with which one of the following statements?**

(a) Surveys of confidence in one's neighbours are an adequate measure of a society's trust.
(b) Trust in institutions functions as a substitute for knowledge about the people one deals with.
(c) Sustained cooperation is possible between people who feel no affection for one another.
(d) Recovering from an institutional scandal is harder than recovering from a personal betrayal.

**8. The argument of the passage depends on the assumption that**

(a) most institutions in modern societies in fact function reliably.
(b) human beings are naturally inclined to trust strangers.
(c) trust is better judged by the range of cooperation it makes possible than by the warmth of the relationships it involves.
(d) traditional communities were morally inferior to modern ones.

---

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The thesis is that each device “converts some region of the world from the invisible into the contestable,” so apparatus sets “the boundaries of a field's imagination.”
*Closest wrong — (d):* it reduces instruments to confirming theory, which the passage explicitly denies — they “do not merely confirm what theory anticipates.”

**2 → (d).** The whole argument runs from detectability to arguability: theory can be tested only inside the region an instrument has made contestable.
*Closest wrong — (a):* the author *states* this in the final line (“rarely notices the walls”), so it is a conclusion of the passage, not an unstated premise the argument needs.

**3 → (a).** Questions “may be neglected for no better reason than that nothing yet registers them” — neglect need not imply a judgement of unimportance.
*Closest wrong — (c):* the passage says theory is not dispensable; it inverts the hierarchy of priority, not of value.

**4 → (c).** Measured, argumentative exposition — but the author calls the consequence “uncomfortable” and speaks of instruments' “silent authority.”
*Closest wrong — (d):* not purely descriptive: the author advances a revisionist thesis and registers discomfort at it.

**5 → (b).** Trust has been “extended much further” but relocated from persons to systems; hence failure “withdraws the guarantee that had made a whole class of relationships unnecessary.”
*Closest wrong — (d):* it captures only the second half; the author insists impersonal trust is “enormously productive,” not inferior on balance.

**6 → (d).** The neighbourly measure “mistakes a change of form for a loss of substance,” so low neighbourly confidence cannot settle the question.
*Closest wrong — (c):* it inverts the passage — the citizen “does not thereafter fall back on trusting individuals,” for he lacks the acquaintance.

**7 → (a).** The passage's opening move is precisely to reject this measure as confusing a change of form with a loss of substance.
*Closest wrong — (b):* the author would in fact endorse it — where systems work, “the question of a stranger's character need never arise at all.”

**8 → (c).** The villager/city-dweller comparison only works if breadth of enabled cooperation, not intimacy, is the yardstick of trust.
*Closest wrong — (a):* the passage assumes only that institutions *can* work — “where they function well” is a conditional, not a claim about most institutions.

## 10-second reading tip

For **assumption** stems, negate the option and ask whether the argument collapses. If the passage survives the negation intact, the option is background colour, not an assumption; if the argument falls apart, that's your answer. This single test kills the “true but unnecessary” distractors UPSC loves to plant.
