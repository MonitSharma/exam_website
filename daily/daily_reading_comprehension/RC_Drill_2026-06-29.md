## Passage 1

It is often assumed that the authority of science rests on its capacity to deliver certainty, and that a claim earns the name "scientific" only when it has been conclusively proved. This picture, however congenial to the popular imagination, inverts the actual logic of inquiry. No finite body of observation can establish a universal law beyond all possibility of revision; the sun's having risen on every recorded morning does not prove that it must rise tomorrow. What distinguishes a scientific claim is not that it has been verified but that it has been exposed—framed precisely enough that some conceivable observation could overturn it. A proposition immune to refutation, however grand, purchases its safety at the cost of saying nothing about the world. The strength of science, then, lies not in the permanence of its conclusions but in the provisionality it openly embraces: every theory is held on sufferance, retained only until a better account displaces it. Those who demand that science speak with the finality of dogma misunderstand the source of its success, for it is precisely the willingness to be wrong, and to specify in advance the terms of being wrong, that has allowed knowledge to accumulate. To present a hypothesis as beyond question is therefore not to honour science but to abandon it. The public unease that greets scientific disagreement—the suspicion that if experts differ, none can be trusted—rests on the same error. Disagreement, far from betraying weakness, is the visible working of a method that treats no conclusion as sacred and no authority as exempt from the tribunal of evidence.

**1. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Science deserves its authority because, unlike dogma, it eventually arrives at certain and permanent conclusions.
(b) Because no universal law can ever be proved, scientific claims are ultimately no more reliable than any other beliefs about the world.
(c) The strength of science lies in framing claims so they can be refuted and in holding all conclusions as provisional, not in delivering certainty.
(d) Public distrust of science is the principal obstacle to the steady accumulation of knowledge.

**2. The author's claim that a proposition "immune to refutation… says nothing about the world" rests on the assumption that:**

(a) Scientists are generally more honest than other claimants to knowledge.
(b) A statement tells us something about the world only insofar as some possible observation could conflict with it.
(c) Every scientific theory now accepted will eventually be shown to be false.
(d) Certainty is psychologically undesirable even on the rare occasions when it can be had.

**3. Which one of the following is a valid inference from the passage?**

(a) A theory that has survived many attempts to refute it has thereby been proved true.
(b) Scientific progress would be faster if experts agreed with one another more often.
(c) The example of the rising sun shows that past observation is irrelevant to forming expectations about the future.
(d) A claim that no possible observation could contradict conveys no information about how the world actually is.

**4. The author would most likely agree that:**

(a) An expert who concedes that her theory could be overturned by future evidence is displaying the proper scientific attitude.
(b) A field in which specialists rarely disagree is, for that very reason, more scientific than one in which they often do.
(c) The more vigorously a hypothesis resists every conceivable refutation, the stronger it should be judged to be.
(d) Public confidence in science is best restored by presenting expert conclusions as settled and beyond dispute.

## Passage 2

Sovereignty remains the organizing fiction of the international order: the doctrine that within its borders each state is the final authority, answerable to no power above it. The language is everywhere—in charters, in the protests of governments against interference, in the very architecture of the assembly where each nation, however small, casts a single equal vote. Yet the practice has quietly drifted from the principle. A state may still command its army and mint its currency, but the value of that currency is set in markets it cannot govern, its industries depend on supply chains threaded through a dozen jurisdictions, and its citizens' data resides on servers beyond its reach. Capital crosses frontiers that armies no longer need to. To insist, under such conditions, that sovereignty means genuine self-determination is to mistake a legal status for a material capacity. This is not to say that the weak and the strong have become equal; on the contrary, formal equality has become the chief disguise of substantive dependence. The state that signs a trade agreement on terms it had no power to refuse has exercised its sovereignty precisely by surrendering it, and the ritual of consent obscures the absence of choice. Those who call for a retreat into national self-sufficiency imagine they are reclaiming a lost autonomy, but they mistake the cause of their predicament: interdependence is not an external imposition that withdrawal could undo, but the condition within which all modern power is now exercised. The sovereignty worth defending is not the right to stand alone—an option that no longer exists—but the capacity to bargain on terms one has had some hand in shaping.

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**

(a) Sovereignty should be abolished outright because it has become a meaningless legal fiction.
(b) Although states keep the formal status of sovereignty, interdependence has hollowed out its substance, so the autonomy worth defending is the capacity to bargain, not to stand alone.
(c) Globalization has made all states genuinely equal by subjecting each alike to the same impersonal markets.
(d) States should pursue national self-sufficiency in order to recover the autonomy that interdependence has taken from them.

**6. The claim that a state signing a trade agreement "on terms it had no power to refuse has exercised its sovereignty precisely by surrendering it" assumes that:**

(a) Trade agreements are always harmful to the weaker signatory.
(b) Sovereign states never enter agreements that genuinely benefit them.
(c) A formal act of consent can coexist with, and conceal, the absence of any real alternative.
(d) Legal authority and military power amount to the same thing.

**7. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**

(a) Formal legal equality among states can coexist with deep inequality in their actual power.
(b) A small state's equal vote in the assembly guarantees it an equal share of influence over outcomes.
(c) The author believes interdependence could be reversed if enough states chose self-sufficiency.
(d) Military strength has become wholly irrelevant to power in international affairs.

**8. The author's attitude toward the call for "a retreat into national self-sufficiency" is best described as:**

(a) sympathetic, though tempered by caution.
(b) openly contemptuous and dismissive.
(c) neutral and purely descriptive.
(d) critical, regarding it as resting on a misdiagnosis of the problem.

---

## Answer Key

**1 → (c).** The thesis is that a claim is scientific because it is "exposed" (refutable) and that science's strength is its openly embraced "provisionality," not certainty.
*Closest wrong — (b):* tempting because the author denies conclusive proof of laws, but he argues the method *succeeds*, so science is **not** "no more reliable than any other belief."

**2 → (b).** For "immune to refutation = says nothing" to hold, empirical content must come precisely from a claim's vulnerability to contrary observation—exactly the unstated premise.
*Closest wrong — (c):* the passage says theories are held "until a better account displaces it," not that *every* current theory *will* be falsified—an overstatement it never makes.

**3 → (d).** Direct restatement of "a proposition immune to refutation… says nothing about the world."
*Closest wrong — (a):* surviving refutation is not proof; the passage explicitly denies any conclusive proof of universal laws.

**4 → (a).** The author praises "the willingness to be wrong, and to specify in advance the terms of being wrong" as the proper scientific stance.
*Closest wrong — (c):* this inverts the passage—resisting all refutation is the mark of a claim that "says nothing," i.e., a weakness, not a strength.

**5 → (b).** The passage concedes states keep formal sovereignty while interdependence has drained its substance, closing on the "capacity to bargain… not the right to stand alone."
*Closest wrong — (d):* this is precisely the view the author rejects—he calls self-sufficiency a misdiagnosis, since interdependence is "the condition," not a removable imposition.

**6 → (c).** The point of "consent… obscures the absence of choice" is that a formal yes can mask the lack of any real alternative.
*Closest wrong — (a):* "always harmful" is an absolute the passage never asserts; the claim is about *coerced consent*, not about every agreement's outcome.

**7 → (a).** "Formal equality has become the chief disguise of substantive dependence" directly supports coexistence of legal equality with real inequality.
*Closest wrong — (c):* the passage states the opposite—interdependence "is not an external imposition that withdrawal could undo."

**8 → (d).** The author says such people "mistake the cause of their predicament"—a reasoned critique grounded in misdiagnosis.
*Closest wrong — (b):* "contemptuous and dismissive" overstates an analytical, measured tone; the author argues against the view, he does not sneer at it.

## 10-Second Reading Tip

On "author would most likely AGREE" stems, first strike out any option that *inverts the thesis while sounding sophisticated* (e.g., "the more a hypothesis resists refutation, the stronger it is"). UPSC's best trap restates the passage's key term but reverses its valence—so check the *direction* of each option against the thesis, not just whether its vocabulary matches.
