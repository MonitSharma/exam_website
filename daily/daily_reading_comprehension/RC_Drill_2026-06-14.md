## Passage

It is commonly assumed that tolerance flows from doubt — that we tolerate the beliefs of others only because we are unsure of our own. On this view, the tolerant person is one who has suspended judgement, while the intolerant person is the one burdened with conviction. Yet this account confuses tolerance with indifference. To be indifferent to a belief is to grant it no weight at all; one does not tolerate what one does not care about. Tolerance, properly understood, presupposes disagreement. It is precisely because I hold my own position to be true, and yours to be mistaken, that my decision to let you hold and express yours becomes a moral act rather than a mere absence of interest.

The paradox deepens when tolerance is mistaken for endorsement. A society that can only abide views it secretly shares has not learned tolerance; it has merely narrowed the range of what counts as acceptable. The genuine test arrives with the belief one finds repugnant, for only there does forbearance cost something. This is why a culture of easy agreement, however congenial, is a poor school for the virtue. Where nothing offends, nothing is tolerated.

It follows that tolerance is not the opposite of conviction but its companion. The sceptic who believes nothing has nothing to tolerate; the zealot who believes everything too fiercely refuses to. Between them stands the tolerant person, who holds firm beliefs and yet declines to impose them — restrained not by uncertainty, but by a regard for the freedom of others to be wrong.

## Questions

**1. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**
(a) Tolerance is a form of indifference that arises when people are unsure of their beliefs.
(b) Genuine tolerance requires holding firm convictions while declining to impose them on others.
(c) A harmonious society is one in which disagreement has been steadily minimised.
(d) Scepticism is the surest foundation on which a tolerant society can be built.

**2. The author would most likely agree with which one of the following statements?**
(a) We can tolerate only those beliefs toward which we are wholly indifferent.
(b) A society that accommodates only the views it already shares is not truly tolerant.
(c) To tolerate a belief one finds repugnant is, in effect, to endorse it.
(d) The zealot and the sceptic are, in different ways, both models of tolerance.

**3. Which one of the following is a valid inference from the passage?**
(a) Indifference and tolerance are practically identical in their social effects.
(b) Tolerance is most meaningfully exercised when the tolerated belief is one we find objectionable.
(c) Strong convictions inevitably lead a person toward intolerance.
(d) A genuinely tolerant person must eventually abandon their own convictions.

**4. The author's attitude toward "a culture of easy agreement" is best described as:**
(a) admiring
(b) indifferent
(c) gently critical
(d) openly hostile

---

## Answer Key

**1 — (b).** The passage builds to the claim that tolerance is conviction's *companion*: firm belief plus restraint from imposing it.
*Closest trap (a):* it states the exact view the author opens with in order to refute — "this account confuses tolerance with indifference."

**2 — (b).** Directly paraphrases "A society that can only abide views it secretly shares has not learned tolerance."
*Closest trap (c):* the author separates tolerance from endorsement ("the paradox deepens when tolerance is mistaken for endorsement"), so this reverses the author's point.

**3 — (b).** Supported by "The genuine test arrives with the belief one finds repugnant, for only there does forbearance cost something."
*Closest trap (c):* the author calls conviction the *companion* of tolerance, not the cause of intolerance — the word "inevitably" overshoots the text.

**4 — (c).** The author concedes the culture is "congenial" yet judges it "a poor school for the virtue" — measured disapproval, not outright attack.
*Closest trap (d):* "openly hostile" overstates a deliberately restrained tone ("however congenial"); extreme-wording options rarely fit a hedged passage.

---

## 10-Second Reading Tip

For **"the author would most likely agree"** stems, pick the option that restates the thesis in *weaker, hedged* language — and first strike out any option carrying absolutes like *only, inevitably, wholly, must, identical*. A passage built on nuance almost never supports an extreme claim, so extreme wording is usually your fastest elimination.
