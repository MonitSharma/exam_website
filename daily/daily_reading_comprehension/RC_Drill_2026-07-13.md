## Passage 1

Economists once treated incentives as universally additive: if a small reward encourages a behaviour, a larger one should encourage it more, and paying people for what they already do voluntarily should, at worst, leave their motivation untouched. Evidence has complicated this tidy picture. When a childcare centre began fining parents who arrived late to collect their children, late arrivals rose rather than fell; the fine converted a moral lapse into a purchasable service, and once purchasable, parents felt entitled to buy it. Blood donation, similarly, can decline when donors are paid, because payment reframes an act of civic generosity as a modest transaction that a self-respecting person might decline as beneath their time. The lesson is not that incentives never work—often they work precisely as intended—but that a price is never merely a price. It carries a signal about how an activity is to be understood, and that signal can displace the motives on which the activity previously rested. Where behaviour is sustained by identity, reciprocity, or a sense of duty, introducing money can erode the very disposition the designer hoped to harness, and the erosion may persist even after the incentive is withdrawn. This does not counsel a blanket suspicion of markets. It counsels attention to what economists have long ignored: the meanings that accompany transactions. A policymaker who assumes that motives are fixed quantities, to be topped up by cash, will be repeatedly surprised. The prudent designer asks first what already moves people, and only then whether a price will reinforce that motive or quietly corrode it.

**1. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**
(a) Monetary incentives are generally counterproductive and should be avoided in public policy.
(b) Incentives carry meanings that can strengthen or displace pre-existing motives, so designers must attend to those meanings rather than assume motives are fixed.
(c) People behave more responsibly when fined for their lapses than when merely asked to reform.
(d) Blood donation and childcare are activities where markets have no legitimate role.

**2. The author's argument rests most centrally on which assumption?**
(a) That non-monetary motives such as duty and reciprocity are real and can be altered by how an activity is framed.
(b) That people always act to maximise their financial self-interest.
(c) That fines and payments are economically equivalent instruments.
(d) That policymakers are generally indifferent to the consequences of their designs.

**3. Which one of the following is a valid inference from the passage?**
(a) Removing a poorly designed incentive automatically restores the original motivation.
(b) A fine can change not just the cost of a behaviour but the way people interpret it.
(c) Larger rewards always produce proportionally larger behavioural responses.
(d) Civic activities should never be organised through payment of any kind.

**4. With which statement would the author most likely AGREE?**
(a) Because motives are fixed, the only question is how large an incentive must be.
(b) A price reform should begin by asking what already motivates the behaviour in question.
(c) Markets corrupt every activity they touch and should be confined to trivial goods.
(d) Moral considerations are irrelevant to the technical design of incentives.

## Passage 2

A written constitution is a text, but a constitutional order is more than its text. Every constitution leaves gaps, uses words whose meaning shifts with circumstance, and confers powers that could, if exercised to their literal limit, defeat the ends the document was meant to serve. What prevents this is not a further clause—no clause can anticipate every abuse—but a disposition among those who wield power: a habit of self-restraint, a willingness to treat the spirit of an arrangement as binding even where its letter falls silent. This disposition is sometimes called constitutional morality, and it is what allows a constitution to function as a living framework rather than a set of loopholes. Where it decays, officials discover that much which is forbidden in spirit remains permitted in letter, and they govern accordingly, observing forms while hollowing out purposes. The danger is subtle because each such act may be individually defensible; it is the accumulation, the pattern of technically lawful evasion, that corrodes the order. A constitution defended only by courts is therefore fragile, for courts arrive late and cannot be everywhere. Its deeper defence lies in a shared understanding that certain things are simply not done, however available they may be. Such understandings cannot be legislated into existence; they are cultivated, transmitted by example, and lost through neglect. This is why the health of a constitutional democracy is measured less by the elegance of its text than by the forbearance of those entrusted with its powers—and why the erosion of that forbearance, though it violates no explicit rule, is among the gravest threats a free order can face.

**5. Which of the following best captures the central idea of the passage?**
(a) Constitutions fail primarily because their texts are poorly drafted.
(b) The stability of a constitutional order depends less on its text than on the self-restraint of those who exercise its powers.
(c) Courts are the sole reliable guardians of constitutional government.
(d) Constitutional morality can be secured by adding sufficiently detailed clauses.

**6. Which of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?**
(a) That the literal exercise of a granted power can sometimes defeat the purpose that power was meant to serve.
(b) That written constitutions contain no gaps or ambiguities.
(c) That officials always act in good faith.
(d) That courts can anticipate and prevent every abuse of power.

**7. Which one of the following can be most safely inferred from the passage?**
(a) Any action that is technically lawful is therefore consistent with constitutional morality.
(b) A pattern of individually defensible acts can nonetheless erode a constitutional order.
(c) A well-drafted text makes constitutional morality unnecessary.
(d) The strength of a democracy is best judged by the volume of its legislation.

**8. The author's attitude toward "technically lawful evasion" is best described as:**
(a) approving, since it respects the letter of the law.
(b) indifferent, treating it as a neutral feature of governance.
(c) wary, viewing its accumulation as a subtle but serious threat.
(d) dismissive, regarding it as too trivial to matter.

## Answers

**1 → (b).** The passage's thesis is that "a price is never merely a price"—incentives carry meanings that can reinforce or displace existing motives.
*Closest wrong — (a):* overstates; the author explicitly says incentives "often work precisely as intended," so a blanket condemnation misreads the balanced claim.

**2 → (a).** The whole argument presupposes that duty, identity, and reciprocity are genuine motives that framing can erode—otherwise crowding-out could not occur.
*Closest wrong — (b):* this is the fixed-self-interest view the passage is written to refute, not an assumption it relies on.

**3 → (b).** Directly supported: the fine "converted a moral lapse into a purchasable service"—it changed interpretation, not just cost.
*Closest wrong — (a):* the passage warns the opposite—erosion "may persist even after the incentive is withdrawn," so removal need not restore the motive.

**4 → (b).** The closing lines say the prudent designer "asks first what already moves people"—a near-restatement.
*Closest wrong — (c):* extreme wording ("corrupt every activity," "trivial goods") contradicts the author's stated refusal to counsel "blanket suspicion of markets."

**5 → (b).** The passage repeatedly contrasts the text with the "forbearance of those entrusted with its powers," locating stability in the latter.
*Closest wrong — (c):* the passage calls a constitution "defended only by courts" fragile—courts are necessary but not sufficient, so "sole reliable guardians" is precisely what it denies.

**6 → (a).** The argument opens by noting powers that "could... defeat the ends the document was meant to serve"—the gap between letter and purpose is the premise everything else builds on.
*Closest wrong — (d):* the passage says the reverse—no clause "can anticipate every abuse"—so this contradicts, rather than underlies, the argument.

**7 → (b).** Explicitly stated: "it is the accumulation, the pattern of technically lawful evasion, that corrodes the order."
*Closest wrong — (a):* the passage's central worry is that much "forbidden in spirit remains permitted in letter"—lawful is not the same as constitutionally proper.

**8 → (c).** The author calls such evasion a "subtle" danger and "among the gravest threats"—clearly wary.
*Closest wrong — (b):* "indifferent/neutral" misses the passage's evaluative alarm; the author is anything but neutral.

## 10-Second Reading Tip

On "central idea" stems, distrust any option containing an absolute (*never, sole, every, only, always*). Argumentative UPSC passages are built on hedged, balanced claims—both of today's passages explicitly refuse the extreme view ("incentives often work"; "courts are necessary but not sufficient"). The trap option almost always overstates; the correct one usually restates the thesis in the passage's own cautious register.
