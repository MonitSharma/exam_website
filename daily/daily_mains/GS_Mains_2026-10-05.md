## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> The Right to Information Act, 2005 and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 both flow from the same constitutional promise, yet Section 44(3) of the latter has reopened the debate on transparency versus privacy. **Critically examine** the changes made to Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act, and discuss whether the two rights can be reconciled without weakening democratic accountability. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction
The RTI Act, 2005 operationalises the right to know, read into **Article 19(1)(a)** by the Supreme Court (*State of UP v. Raj Narain*, 1975; *S.P. Gupta*, 1981). **Puttaswamy (2017)** declared privacy a fundamental right under **Article 21**. **Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act, 2023** amended **Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act** to exempt "personal information", and the change is now before the Supreme Court.

### Body

**1. What changed**
- *Earlier:* personal information was exempt only if unrelated to public activity or if its disclosure was an unwarranted invasion of privacy, and it could still be released if the larger public interest justified it. A proviso said information that could not be denied to Parliament or a State Legislature could not be denied to a citizen.
- *Now:* the clause covers personal information as such, and critics argue this removes the harm and public-interest tests.

**2. Case for the amendment**
- Privacy is a fundamental right (*Puttaswamy*), and a data law needs one consistent definition of personal data.
- It protects ordinary citizens (beneficiaries, patients, applicants) from being exposed through RTI queries or misused by "RTI-activism".
- Section 8(2) of the RTI Act, the general public-interest override, is still there. The Court has also not stayed the law, saying it will not by interim order replace a regime Parliament has chosen.

**3. Case against**
- It dilutes the balancing test. Names of officials, beneficiary lists, ration and pension records, and the antecedents of public servants all involve "personal information" and are the core of social audit.
- Earlier rulings accepted that public servants' official conduct is open to scrutiny (*Girish Deshpande v. CIC*, 2012, on service records, as a limit) while also recognising overriding public interest (*CPIO, Supreme Court v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal*, 2019).
- Transparency checks corruption. Exempting the information that exposes it weakens **Article 14** and **19(1)(a)** values, and could be seen as arbitrary.
- Institutional concerns: the Data Protection Board's independence from the executive (petitioners' challenge), and the use of an amending Act to alter a transparency law without consulting RTI stakeholders.

**4. Reconciling the two rights**
- Both rights are **complementary**, not hierarchical. The **proportionality test** from *Puttaswamy* (legality, legitimate aim, necessity, proportionality) can guide the balance.
- Restore a harm-based test, define "personal information" narrowly, and exclude information on public duties, official acts and use of public funds.
- Strengthen the independence of the Data Protection Board and the Information Commissions (appointments, tenure, vacancies).
- Offer a clear procedure for public-interest disclosure, and train PIOs to apply it.

### Conclusion
Privacy and transparency are two sides of constitutional democracy. The Constitution Bench should read Section 44(3) so that privacy shields the citizen without shielding the State's public conduct. As the Court itself noted, some "creases" need ironing, and a proportionate balance can do that without abandoning either right.

### Value additions
- **Cases:** *Raj Narain (1975)*, *S.P. Gupta (1981)*, *PUCL v. UoI (2003)* (right to know candidates' background), *Puttaswamy (2017)* (privacy and proportionality), *Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019)* (Supreme Court under RTI).
- **Articles:** 19(1)(a), 21, 14; RTI Act Sections 8(1)(j), 8(2); DPDP Act Sections 36 and 44(3).
- **Current:** the 16 Feb 2026 order (no stay; reference to a larger bench) and the petitions by NCPRI, Reporters Collective and Venkatesh Nayak.
- **Thinker/idea:** Justice Brandeis's idea that "sunlight is the best disinfectant", set against the privacy tradition of Warren and Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy" (1890).
