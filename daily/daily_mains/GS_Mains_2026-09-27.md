# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-27 (Sunday — Reflective / Essay-style GS question)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Sunday = one reflective, cross-cutting question spanning Industrial Policy & Growth (GS3), Employment & Social Structure (GS1) and Trade & the External Environment (GS2/GS3). Attempt it on paper first; the model answer is below the divider.*

> **This week's hook:** Make in India turned **12 on 25 September 2026**. The two scorecards published that week do not agree.
>
> **The government's stocktake** is a record of scale. It reports electronics production up from **₹1.9 lakh crore (2014-15) to ₹13.11 lakh crore (2025-26)**, with India now the **world's 2nd-largest mobile-phone manufacturer**. Defence production rose from **₹46,429 crore to ₹1.78 lakh crore** and crude steel from **81.7 MT to 170 MT**. Cumulative FDI is about **US$843 billion** since 2014-15. The **14 PLI schemes** report roughly **₹2.4 lakh crore of investment, ~₹22.66 lakh crore of production/sales and 14 lakh+ jobs**.
>
> **The structural scorecard** barely moved. The Government told the **Lok Sabha on 12 August 2026** that manufacturing was **14.7% of GVA in 2022-23 and 14.8% in 2025-26** (new 2022-23 base series). In its words, the data *"does not indicate any significant change in the share of manufacturing."* The target was **25%**, set by the **National Manufacturing Policy 2011** and adopted by Make in India. **PLFS 2025** puts manufacturing at just **12.1% of workers**, against **43.0% still in agriculture**. **The Hindu's 12-metric review (25 September 2026)** found manufacturing's share of growth, employment and global exports *"largely unchanged."* India's share of world merchandise exports is still about **1.7%**, and PLI investment is concentrated, with roughly **83% of it in five sectors**.
>
> So the policy has created sectors but has not changed the structure of the economy. That is today's reflective theme. It is deliberately distinct from this month's GS3 questions on **private capex concentration (15 September)**, **agricultural price policy (22 September)**, **Labour Codes (8 September)** and **carbon markets/CBAM (17 September)**. Each of those supplies one lever for today's answer. It is also distinct from the **demographic-dividend essay (28 June)**, which asked whether India can employ its young. Today's question asks whether the *particular route* that East Asia used to do so is still open.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> Twelve years after the launch of Make in India, several sectors have grown manifold, yet manufacturing's share in India's output, employment and world exports has barely moved. In an age of automation, rising protectionism and Chinese overcapacity, **critically examine** whether the East Asian path of manufacturing-led development remains open to India, and reflect on what a realistic industrial strategy should aim for.

## MODEL ANSWER (~300 words — trim a bullet or two to hit 250 in the exam)

**Introduction**
Japan, Korea and China grew rich by moving farm labour into factories. Make in India (2014) set out to follow that path with a **25%-of-GDP** target. Twelve years on, manufacturing is still about **15% of GVA** and **12% of jobs** (PLFS 2025).

**Why manufacturing matters**
- **Engine of growth (Kaldor):** manufacturing productivity converges towards the global frontier.
- **Absorbs labour:** it can move the **43% of workers** still in farming.
- **Tradable:** it is not limited by the size of the domestic market.

**The record: scale without structural change**
- **Gains:** electronics output grew **~7x** and defence production **~4x**, and phone assembly is now world-scale.
- **Stagnation:** manufacturing's share of GVA, jobs and world exports (**~1.7%**) is flat, and PLI benefits are concentrated in a few sectors that are capital-intensive and focused on assembly.

**Why the ladder is harder now**
1. **Premature deindustrialisation (Rodrik):** manufacturing now peaks at lower incomes.
2. **Automation** erodes labour-cost advantage.
3. **Protectionism and green barriers**, such as tariffs and **CBAM**.
4. **Chinese overcapacity** caps prices.
5. **Domestic frictions:** land, logistics, the "missing middle" in firm sizes, and high input tariffs.

**But the path is not closed**
Firms seeking **China-plus-one** suppliers, new FTAs (UK, EFTA, EU, New Zealand) and still-unclaimed **labour-intensive niches** (apparel, footwear, food processing, components) keep the window open.

**A realistic strategy**
- **Measure jobs per rupee of incentive and domestic value addition**, not share of GDP.
- **Support MSME scaling** through the **National Manufacturing Mission**.
- **Keep inputs cheap:** low input tariffs let firms join global value chains.
- **Pair manufacturing with services exports**, which complement factories but cannot replace them for low-skill workers.
- **Let States lead** on land, labour and power reforms.

**Conclusion**
The East Asian ladder has not vanished; its rungs are just fewer and further apart. India can still industrialise if it is **selective** (backing jobs, not just output) and **open** (to imported inputs, not only to export markets). Otherwise the demographic dividend risks becoming a liability.

## Value-additions (use 2–3)

1. **Dani Rodrik, "Premature Deindustrialization," *Journal of Economic Growth* 21 (2016).** Developing countries are running out of industrialisation opportunities sooner, and at much lower incomes, than early industrialisers did. This is the single most citable line for this question.
2. **Kaldor's growth laws.** Manufacturing output growth drives economy-wide productivity growth, which is why a flat manufacturing share is a *growth* problem and not only an *employment* problem.
3. **Target vs. outcome.** The **National Manufacturing Policy 2011** targeted **25% of GDP and 100 million jobs by 2022**, and Make in India carried the 25% goal forward. Compare that with **14.8% of GVA (2025-26)** from the **Lok Sabha reply of 12 August 2026** and **12.1% of employment (PLFS 2025)**. One clean sentence contrasting target and outcome carries more weight than a paragraph of scheme names.
4. **Use the data carefully.** PLI's reported **~14 lakh jobs** is small next to India's **~10 million-plus annual labour-force entrants**, a figure widely cited in policy debate. Use it only as an order of magnitude. Also, "production/sales" under PLI counts gross output, not domestic value added.

## Examiner's notes (how this is marked)

- **What earns marks:** separating *sectoral scale* from *structural share*; a named analytical frame (Rodrik or Kaldor); treating the "closed ladder" thesis fairly and then qualifying it; and a reframed goal in the conclusion.
- **What loses marks:** a list of schemes (PLI, GatiShakti, NSWS, Startup India) with no argument; treating the 25% miss as proof of "failure" without acknowledging the gains; or ignoring employment altogether. For GS1 and Essay overlap, *jobs* are the heart of this question.
