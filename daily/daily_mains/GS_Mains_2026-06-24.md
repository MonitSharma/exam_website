## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction (anchor + context):**
On 25 June 1975, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed proclaimed a national Emergency under **Article 352** on the advice of PM Indira Gandhi, citing "internal disturbance." For 21 months (until **21 March 1977**), fundamental rights stood suspended, the press was censored and political opponents were jailed. Fifty-one years on — now marked as **"Samvidhaan Hatya Diwas"** — it remains the sharpest stress-test of India's democratic consolidation.

**Body — I. The immediate trigger:**
The proximate spark was legal-political. On **12 June 1975, Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court** set aside Indira Gandhi's Rae Bareli election for corrupt practices under the Representation of the People Act, disqualifying her for six years. The Supreme Court's vacation judge granted only a **conditional stay (24 June 1975)**, and Emergency followed the next day — converting a personal political crisis into a national one.

**II. The deeper, structural strains:**
- *Economic distress*: the 1973–74 oil shock, double-digit inflation, food scarcity and the 1974 Railway strike.
- *A mass opposition*: the **JP (Jayaprakash Narayan) movement** and Navnirman agitation channelled mounting public anger.
- *Institutional erosion*: a "committed" judiciary/bureaucracy, the eclipse of intra-party democracy and centralisation of power in the PMO had hollowed out checks well before 1975.

**III. The character of the regime (excesses):**
Mass detentions under **MISA**, pre-censorship of the press, the **42nd Amendment (1976)** ("mini-Constitution") curbing judicial review, and coercive programmes — forced sterilisation and slum demolitions. In **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)**, the Supreme Court abdicated, holding that even the right to life (Art. 21) could be suspended.

**IV. Significance as a turning point:**
- *Democratic resilience*: the **1977 election** ousted the Congress and installed India's first non-Congress government — proof that the electorate, not institutions alone, was the ultimate safeguard.
- *Constitutional course-correction*: the **44th Amendment (1978)** replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion," required a *written Cabinet* recommendation, and made **Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable**.
- *Judicial redemption*: ADM Jabalpur was finally and explicitly overruled in **K.S. Puttaswamy (2017)**.
- *Political realignment*: it accelerated the rise of regional parties, coalition politics and an assertive civil society and press.

**Conclusion (balanced, forward-looking):**
The Emergency was thus less a freak event than a revelation of how quickly a democracy can be subverted when institutional guardrails weaken — and how decisively a vigilant citizenry can restore them. Its enduring lesson is not triumphalism but **eternal vigilance**: as Ambedkar warned, constitutional morality is "not a natural sentiment" and must be cultivated. India's democracy survived 1975 not by accident, but because its people chose liberty over order at the ballot box.

### Value-additions to deploy (pick 2–3, don't list all)
- **Shah Commission of Inquiry (1977, Justice J.C. Shah):** appointed by the Janata government to document Emergency excesses — the authoritative official record of abuses.
- **Granville Austin** (*Working a Democratic Constitution*) reads the Emergency as testing whether the Constitution could survive its own provisions — and concludes the system "bent but did not break."
- **ADM Jabalpur → Puttaswamy (2017):** the journey from judicial surrender to the right to privacy/dignity is a ready GS1+GS2 bridge on civil liberties.
- **Ambedkar's caution** in the Constituent Assembly against "bhakti" or hero-worship in politics as "a sure road to degradation and eventual dictatorship" — strikingly prescient.
- **Data/keyword anchors:** Art. 352 (national emergency), MISA, 42nd & 44th Amendments, 25 June 1975 – 21 March 1977.
