## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction (context).** India's Constitution makes the Governor a *nominal executive head* of a State who acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Art 163), yet Art 200 also gives the office a role in lawmaking. With the Governor being a Centre-appointee serving at the President's pleasure (Art 156), the assent power has become a flashpoint in Centre–State relations, especially where parties differ.

**1. Constitutional scheme**
- **Art 200:** On a Bill passed by the Legislature, the Governor may (a) assent, (b) withhold assent, (c) reserve it for the President, or (d) return a non-Money Bill with a message for reconsideration; if the House re-passes it, the Governor *shall not withhold assent*.
- **Mandatory reservation:** Where the Bill would derogate from the powers of the High Court so as to endanger its constitutional position (second proviso).
- **Art 201:** The President may assent or withhold, or direct return of a (non-Money) Bill; Art 254(2) gives repugnant State laws prevailing force on Concurrent subjects only with Presidential assent.
- **Art 163 and Art 361:** Aid-and-advice rule with limited discretion; personal immunity of the Governor from court proceedings.
- Silence on timelines ("as soon as possible" appears only in the first proviso) is the root of delay.

**2. Evolving judicial approach**
- *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974):* Governor acts on ministerial advice save in narrow constitutional discretion.
- *Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016):* Governor's discretion is limited and cannot be used to destabilise elected governments.
- *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor (Apr 2025):* Held prolonged inaction illegal, fixed outer time-limits, and used Art 142 for "deemed assent".
- *Presidential Reference (Nov 2025):* Court **declined fixed timelines and deemed assent** (separation of powers), recognised Governor's limited discretion, but kept a **limited mandamus** for unexplained, indefinite delay, and held that a Bill cannot be withheld without being returned to the House.

**3. Critical assessment**
- *Concerns about delay:* An elected legislature's mandate is held hostage to an unelected office; indefinite "sitting" on Bills amounts to a pocket veto, which the Constitution does not give. It chills policy-making in areas like higher education, health and public services.
- *Case for caution:* Governor is a constitutional check against hasty or unconstitutional legislation; courts rewriting the Constitution through timelines or deemed assent risks judicial overreach.
- *Gap in the post-2025 position:* Without time-limits, a reasoned-delay standard is vague; litigating each Bill takes time that often outlasts the legislative session.

**4. Way forward**
- Adopt guidelines through convention or a Constitution (or rules) amendment: **time-bound action** and **written reasons** for withholding or reserving. The *Punchhi Commission (2010)* recommended a six-month limit for Governor's decision on Bills; the *Sarkaria Commission (1988)* urged Governors to act as constitutional heads and to consult the Chief Minister on appointments and key decisions.
- Appointment reform: consult the Chief Minister / Inter-State Council (as recommended by Sarkaria and Punchhi); fixed tenure and non-political appointees.
- Strengthen the **Inter-State Council (Art 263)** as a forum for resolving Centre–State disputes before they reach courts.

**Conclusion.** A Governor's role should be that of a "friend, philosopher and guide", not a political gatekeeper. The solution lies less in judicial command than in constitutional morality: timely, reasoned action by the office, backed by the narrow judicial remedy that remains. This is what keeps Indian federalism "cooperative" in practice.

### Value-additions (use 2–3 in the exam)
1. **Constitutional morality / Dr B.R. Ambedkar:** In the Constituent Assembly, the Governor was envisaged as having no functions he can discharge on his own — an elected-government-first design.
2. **Committees:** Sarkaria (1988), Punchhi (2010), and the 2000 NCRWC (Venkatachaliah) on Governor's appointment and conduct.
3. **Case law:** *Shamsher Singh* (1974), *Nabam Rebia* (2016), *Tamil Nadu v. Governor* (2025), Presidential Reference (2025).
4. **Phrase to use:** "Cooperative federalism requires cooperation from constitutional offices, not just constitutional text."
