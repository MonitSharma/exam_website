> **This week's hook:** The ethics of *automated* administration is the fastest-growing GS4 frontier, and India now has both the technology and the cautionary record. Telangana's **Samagra Vedika** system — built by a private vendor, pulling citizen data from **30+ government databases** and using machine-learning "entity resolution" to build 360-degree profiles and flag ineligible beneficiaries — was linked to the cancellation of **over 18.6 lakh food security cards and the rejection of 1,42,086 fresh applications between 2014 and 2019**, in many cases **without notice**. An **Amnesty International technical investigation (April 2024)** found neither the vendor nor the State had released source code or accuracy data; a widely reported case involved a 67-year-old widow whose deceased rickshaw-puller husband was algorithmically tagged as a car owner, costing the family its subsidised grain. The policy scaffolding has only just begun to appear: **MeitY released the India AI Governance Guidelines on 5 November 2025**, built on seven *sutras* — **Trust, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness & Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and Safety, Resilience & Sustainability** — but these are **voluntary and sector-agnostic**, with sectoral regulators expected to fill the gap; and the **DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025** (gazetted 14 November) under a phased timeline running to **May 2027**. So an officer today inherits the machine without the rulebook. That is today's GS4 case, deliberately distinct from the last two Saturday cases — the **heatwave/red-alert SDM dilemma (20 June)** and the **DBT maternity-benefit fraud and the mentor (27 June)**. This one is not about corruption; it is about **accountability for a decision no human consciously took**.

## QUESTION (Case Study) — 15 marks · 250 words

You are the **District Collector** of a poor, largely rural district. Six weeks ago your State rolled out **"SAMPANN"**, an AI-based eligibility engine built by a private vendor, which cross-matches over thirty government databases to detect ineligible beneficiaries under the National Food Security Act. In your district it has flagged and **automatically suspended 48,000 ration cards**, days before the festival season. The State has publicised a **₹300 crore annual saving** and your district tops the "clean-up" leaderboard.

A sample field verification you quietly ordered through your SDMs finds that **roughly one in three suspensions is wrong** — a deceased husband recorded as a vehicle owner, a namesake's PAN attached to a landless widow, a migrant labourer flagged as "urban salaried" because of a stale address. **No suspension notice was issued; no hearing was offered.** Most of those affected are illiterate and do not know why the shop turned them away.

When you raise this, three responses arrive within a day:

- The **vendor** refuses to disclose the model's logic or error rates, citing **proprietary trade secrets**, and offers instead to "recalibrate the threshold quietly."
- The **Principal Secretary**, your reporting officer, tells you the State is about to scale SAMPANN to all districts and that "a 30% error rate reported on paper will kill the reform; fix it at your end without a file."
- A **respected local journalist**, who has independently spoken to three excluded families, calls and asks you to confirm the verification findings on record. The State's flagship image — and your own reputation as its best-performing Collector — rests on your reply.

**Answer the following:**

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved in this case.
**(b)** Evaluate the options available to you, and state, with justification, the course of action you would adopt.
**(c)** What institutional safeguards should govern the use of automated decision-making in welfare delivery?

**(Directive: this is a *case study* — do not write an essay on "AI and ethics". The marks lie in (i) naming the *competing values* precisely rather than listing virtues; (ii) giving *real, sequenced administrative options* with honest merits and demerits, including the ones you reject; (iii) a decision that is defensible, lawful and implementable by a Collector *this week*; and (iv) a forward-looking part (c) that converts the dilemma into systemic design. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Framing the dilemma

The case is not about detecting fraud — that is a legitimate public purpose — but about **who is accountable when an opaque system takes a decision that strips a citizen of a statutory entitlement without notice**. Efficiency has been purchased with due process.

### (a) Ethical issues involved

1. **Justice and the priority of the worst-off** — an *exclusion* error and an *inclusion* error are not morally equivalent. Wrongly retaining an ineligible card costs the exchequer; wrongly deleting a genuine one costs a family its food. Rawls' **difference principle** and Sen's **capability approach** both demand that the error be weighted by whom it falls on.
2. **Natural justice and human dignity** — suspension without notice or hearing violates *audi alteram partem* and the fairness standard of **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)**; the right to food is read into **Article 21** (*PUCL v. Union of India*, 2001).
3. **Accountability without authorship** — the Collector bears legal responsibility for orders generated by a model he cannot inspect. A trade-secret claim cannot extinguish a public duty.
4. **Transparency vs. institutional loyalty** — the instruction to "fix it without a file" asks for concealment of a material fact, and destroys the audit trail that accountability depends on.
5. **Integrity vs. career self-interest** — the Collector's own "best-performing district" ranking is itself a conflict of interest.
6. **Objectivity and non-partisanship** — the duty to give honest, unpalatable advice to political and administrative superiors.

### (b) Options and course of action

| Option | Merits | Demerits |
|---|---|---|
| Accept the Secretary's advice; silently recalibrate | Preserves relationships and the reform's momentum | Conceals a material fact; leaves 16,000 families excluded; no record; complicity |
| Publicly confirm to the journalist immediately | Fast, forces correction | Breaches **AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968, Rule 7** on unauthorised communication; pre-empts internal remedy; loses the ability to fix it |
| Suspend the algorithm's effect locally, record everything in writing, escalate formally | Restores entitlements at once; preserves lawfulness and the audit trail | Invites displeasure; slower than publicity |
| Resign / seek transfer | Protects personal conscience | Abdication — the families gain nothing |

**Course of action (sequenced, and defensible):**

- **Immediate relief first.** Order **restoration of all 48,000 suspended cards** pending individual verification, using the Collector's powers under the **NFSA, 2013**, and issue the required show-cause notice with a hearing before any deletion. Under uncertainty, the reversible harm must be preferred to the irreversible one — a hungry household cannot be compensated retrospectively.
- **Put everything on the file.** A written note to the Principal Secretary recording the sample-verification methodology, the ~33% false-positive rate, and the legal infirmity — refusing, politely and explicitly, the request to act "without a file". Ethical courage here is procedural, not theatrical.
- **Activate the statutory machinery, not the press.** Refer the matter to the **District Grievance Redressal Officer (Section 14, NFSA)** and the **State Food Commission (Section 15)**, and set up camp-mode redressal with panchayats, ASHAs and the vigilance committees.
- **Confront the vendor's claim institutionally.** Insist, through the State, on disclosure of **error rates, audit logs and a human-review layer** as a contractual condition — proprietary code may stay confidential, but **accuracy data affecting statutory rights cannot**.
- **On the journalist:** decline to comment in a personal capacity, which conduct rules bar; instead ensure the facts reach the public lawfully — through the **official spokesperson**, the grievance body and a record that is discoverable under the **RTI Act, 2005**. Whistleblowing to the press is a *last* resort, justified only after internal channels demonstrably fail; here they have not yet been tried.

### (c) Safeguards for automated decision-making in welfare

- **No adverse action without a human in the loop** — the algorithm may *flag*, never *decide*.
- **Statutory notice, hearing and appeal** before any deletion, with the **burden of proof on the State**, not the beneficiary.
- **Mandatory publication of accuracy and disaggregated error rates**; independent third-party algorithmic audit; procurement contracts that make explainability a condition of payment.
- **Design incentives around exclusion, not savings** — reward districts for zero wrongful exclusion, not for cards cancelled.
- Anchor all of this in the **India AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY, 5 November 2025)** principles of **Fairness & Equity, Accountability and "Understandable by Design"**, and give them statutory teeth through sectoral rules — as things stand they are voluntary.

### Conclusion

Technology can make the State faster, but only institutions can make it *just*. The administrator's duty, when an opaque system errs against the poorest, is neither silent compliance nor spectacular defiance, but **lawful, documented, on-the-record insistence** that efficiency does not get to override due process. As the **Second ARC's Fourth Report, "Ethics in Governance" (2007)**, put it, public office is a **trust** — and a trustee cannot outsource conscience to a vendor's black box.

### Value-additions to remember

1. **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles of Public Life (1995)** — *openness* and *accountability* directly cover the "fix it without a file" instruction.
2. **Kant's second formulation of the categorical imperative** — treating 48,000 households as *means* to a ₹300 crore savings headline is the core wrong; **Rawls' difference principle** and **Sen's capability approach** justify weighting exclusion errors more heavily.
3. **Legal anchors:** *PUCL v. Union of India* (2001) — right to food under Article 21; *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) — procedural fairness; **NFSA, 2013, Sections 14–15** — District Grievance Redressal Officer and State Food Commission; **AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968, Rule 7**; **Second ARC, 4th Report (2007)**.
4. **Contemporary anchors:** **India AI Governance Guidelines, MeitY, 5 November 2025** (seven *sutras*, voluntary, sector-agnostic); **DPDP Rules, 2025**, notified 13 November 2025 with phased enforcement to May 2027; **Amnesty International's April 2024 investigation** into entity-resolution systems in Indian welfare delivery.

### A note on technique for this paper

GS4 case studies are scored on **decision quality**, not vocabulary. Three habits worth drilling today:

- **State the decision in one line before justifying it.** Examiners look for a stakeholder-by-stakeholder resolution, not a meditation.
- **Reject options explicitly.** Saying *why you did not go to the press* earns more than saying why you restored the cards.
- **Keep part (c) systemic.** The difference between a 9 and a 13 is usually whether the candidate converted a personal dilemma into an institutional reform.
