## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction (text + anchor)

**Article 142(1)** authorises the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order "necessary for doing complete justice" in a cause or matter pending before it, enforceable throughout the territory of India. It is a **residuary equitable power**, unique to the Supreme Court — High Courts cannot read it into Article 226 (*Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain*, 2009), and must work through Article 226 or **Section 528, BNSS 2023**. Its reach was on display on **1 September 2026**, when the Court quashed all FIRs arising from the July 2026 NEET-UG protests — including those of non-parties in States that never appeared before it — and barred fresh FIRs on the same events.

### A. Why the power matters: justice where ordinary law falls short

- **Filling statutory gaps.** Codified law cannot anticipate every injustice. In ***Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan* (2023)** the Court used Article 142 to dissolve marriages on **irretrievable breakdown** — a ground the Hindu Marriage Act does not contain — sparing couples the mandatory cooling-off period.
- **Mass remedies no ordinary process can deliver.** In ***Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India* (1991)** the Court used Article 142 to sustain the Bhopal settlement; in ***Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat* (1991)** it held that ordinary statutory limits cannot curtail the power. The 2026 NEET order is of this family — thousands of scattered FIRs across many States could not realistically be undone case-by-case.
- **Protecting fundamental rights in practice.** Peaceful assembly is protected by **Article 19(1)(b)**, subject to Article 19(3); *Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary* (2012) and *Anita Thakur v. State of J&K* (2016) restrain excessive force against demonstrators. Ending a blanket criminalisation of protest vindicates a right that would otherwise be defeated by the process itself.
- **Enforcing accountability through continuing mandamus.** The direction to frame a compensation policy in three months follows the *Vishaka* (1997) template — the Court supplying an interim framework where the political executive has not acted.

### B. The separation-of-powers concerns

- **"Complete justice" is undefined.** The phrase supplies no standard, so its content varies with the Bench. This risks what *Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass* (2008) warned against: judges must "know their limits" and not run the country.
- **Binding non-parties.** Quashing FIRs in States that were never heard sits uneasily with **audi alteram partem** and federal comity; the *prospective bar* on registering FIRs is closer to legislating an immunity than adjudicating a dispute.
- **Encroachment on legislative space.** Creating a divorce ground, or mandating a compensation scheme, is policy-making. **Separation of powers is part of the basic structure** (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973; *I.R. Coelho*, 2007), even though India follows a *functional*, not rigid, model (*Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab*, 1955).
- **Internal limits already exist — and the Court itself invokes them.** ***Prem Chand Garg* (1963)** — Article 142 cannot override fundamental rights or substantive statutory provisions; ***Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India* (1998)** — it cannot supplant substantive law. Most tellingly, in the **Presidential Reference opinion of 20 November 2025** the Court held that Article 142 **cannot be used to "deem assent"** to Bills, since it cannot displace express constitutional provisions under Articles 200–201. The jurisprudence is therefore unsettled: expansive in criminal-justice and family matters, restrained in inter-branch matters.
- **Predictability cost.** Because Article 142 orders often expressly say they are "not to be treated as precedent," they generate relief without rule — sitting awkwardly with **Article 141**, under which the Court's declared law binds all courts.

### Conclusion

Article 142 is best understood as the Constitution's **safety valve, not its steering wheel** — the framers' recognition that legality and justice can diverge, and that the apex court must be able to close that gap. The remedy for its uneven use is **discipline, not deletion**: self-crafted, publicly stated guidelines on when the power will be exercised; a preference for Constitution Bench articulation of its outer limits; reasoned orders explaining why no ordinary remedy suffices; and, above all, a legislature and executive responsive enough that the Court is rarely the only institution left standing. As Cardozo cautioned, a judge "is not a knight-errant, roaming at will in pursuit of his own ideal of beauty or of goodness." Used sparingly, Article 142 is constitutional conscience; used routinely, it becomes constitutional substitution.

### Value-additions to deploy (pick 2–3, don't dump all)

1. **Case-law spine, four rows:** *enabling* — Union Carbide (1991), Delhi Judicial Service Association (1991); *limiting* — Prem Chand Garg (1963), SCBA v. UoI (1998); *expansive recent* — Shilpa Sailesh (2023), NEET FIRs (1 Sept 2026); *restrictive recent* — Presidential Reference advisory opinion (20 Nov 2025, no "deemed assent").
2. **Constitutional cross-links:** Article 141 (precedent) vs Article 142 (equity); Article 32 (rights enforcement); Article 145 (rules of court); the fact that Article 142(1) orders are enforceable "in such manner as may be prescribed by law made by Parliament" — Parliament has never legislated, leaving the Court to define its own reach.
3. **Comparative/thinker angle:** Montesquieu's tripartite separation vs Ambedkar's deliberately *functional* Indian model; contrast with the US Supreme Court, which has no equivalent textual "complete justice" clause and works through equitable remedies developed case by case.
4. **Institutional counter-point for balance:** judicial expansion typically tracks executive and legislative inaction — pendency of over five crore cases, and unaddressed policy vacuums, are the demand-side of judicial overreach.
