> **This week's hook:** The single most-watched number in Indian macroeconomics for a decade — *when does private investment come back?* — has finally moved. The **NSO's Q1 2026–27 estimates (released 31 August 2026)** put real **GDP growth at 7.8%** and **GVA at 8.2%**, but the striking line is on the expenditure side: **Gross Fixed Capital Formation grew 11.9% at constant prices**, against just **5.8%** in Q1 of 2025–26. The credit data tells the same story — **bank credit to industry grew 19.2% year-on-year in June 2026**, against **6.3%** a year earlier, with **non-food credit at 18.3%**; an **RBI article (August 2025)** had already projected bank-sanctioned private capex intentions rising **21.5% to ₹2.67 lakh crore** in FY26. Conditions are unusually supportive: the **repo rate is at 5.25%** with inflation near the lower half of the band, **GST 2.0's two-slab structure (5% / 18%, plus a 40% demerit rate) has been in force since 22 September 2025**, the **Union Budget 2026–27 (1 February 2026)** raised capital expenditure ~9% to **₹12.2 lakh crore** (effective capex **₹17.15 lakh crore**, 4.4% of GDP) while holding the fiscal deficit to **4.3% of GDP** on a glide path to a **50% (±1) debt-to-GDP ratio by FY31**, and the **India–US trade understanding of February 2026** cut reciprocal tariffs on Indian goods from **25% to 18%**.
>
> And yet the composition of the boom should give a careful student pause. **Private corporate capex was ₹30.3 lakh crore in FY25, up only ~7%** (MoSPI data reported September 2026). Within FY26 private investment, **Adani and Reliance alone accounted for roughly ₹3 lakh crore — about a quarter of the total** — with **Reliance committing ~$110 billion to AI and data centres over seven years** and **Adani ~$100 billion by 2035**. **Capacity utilisation was 75.6% (Q3 FY26)** — improving, but still not at the level that historically forces broad-based greenfield expansion. A revival powered by two conglomerates betting on one global theme is a different economic object from a revival powered by thousands of firms responding to demand.
>
> That gap — *between an investment cycle that has turned and one that is genuinely broad-based* — is today's GS3 theme. It is deliberately distinct from this month's GS3 questions on the **Labour Codes (8 September)** and the **SHANTI Act / nuclear energy (10 September)**, from the GS3 questions on **trade diversification and FTAs (11 August)** and **flexible inflation targeting (30 June)**, and from the Sunday essays on **urban governance and municipal finance (6 September)** and **delimitation (13 September)** — though the municipal-finance and land-titling (14 September) material is legitimately borrowable for *one* line on why investment stays where infrastructure and clear title already exist.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> India's investment cycle appears to have turned: gross fixed capital formation grew by 11.9% in the first quarter of 2026–27 and bank credit to industry by over 19%. Yet a disproportionate share of private capital expenditure remains concentrated in a few conglomerates and a few capital-intensive sectors. **Critically analyse** the factors driving this revival in private investment, and assess whether it is broad-based enough to sustain growth and employment.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (definition + anchor)

Gross Fixed Capital Formation — the addition to an economy's stock of productive assets — is the channel through which present saving becomes future capacity. Its **11.9% growth in Q1 FY27 (NSO, 31 August 2026)**, alongside **19.2% industrial credit growth (RBI, June 2026)**, signals that India's long-awaited private capex cycle has begun to turn. Whether it is a *cycle* or a *concentrated bet* is the real question.

### What is driving the revival

- **Balance-sheet repair:** the "twin balance sheet" problem of the 2010s has inverted into a **twin balance sheet advantage** — deleveraged corporates and banks with historically low NPAs, aided by the IBC's credit discipline.
- **Macro conditions:** a **repo rate of 5.25%** with inflation in the lower half of the tolerance band lowers the hurdle rate; **GST 2.0** (two slabs since 22 September 2025) and income-tax relief have lifted consumption, which the **accelerator principle** translates into investment demand.
- **Public capex crowding in:** **₹12.2 lakh crore** of Union capex in BE 2026–27 (effective capex 4.4% of GDP) has built the logistics and power backbone that private projects depend on.
- **Policy-created demand:** PLI schemes, the energy transition, semiconductors and — most of all — **AI and data-centre build-out**, plus supply-chain diversification aided by the **February 2026 tariff reduction from 25% to 18%**.

### Why it is not yet broad-based

- **Firm concentration:** two groups accounted for roughly **a quarter of FY26 private capex**; narrative-driven, concentrated confidence is more fragile than demand-driven, diffused confidence.
- **Sectoral concentration:** capex is clustered in data centres, energy and infrastructure — **capital-intensive, low employment-elasticity** sectors. Capital deepening is not the same as job creation, and India's problem is the latter.
- **The MSME gap:** aggregate private capex grew only **~7% in FY25 (₹30.3 lakh crore)**; **capacity utilisation at 75.6%** suggests the median firm is still using existing capacity rather than adding to it.
- **Geographic concentration:** investment flows to states with clear land title, reliable power and functioning urban infrastructure — deepening regional divergence.
- **The demand question:** private investment follows expected demand; with consumption still unevenly distributed and real wage growth modest, a durable cycle needs a mass consumption base, not only export and AI demand.

### Conclusion

The turn is real but narrow: India has an investment *upswing* rather than an investment *boom*. Broadening it depends on three conditions — deregulation and faster clearances so that small and mid-sized firms face the same ease as large ones; credit that reaches the MSME tail rather than the corporate top; and a deliberate tilt toward **labour-intensive** manufacturing alongside capital-intensive frontier sectors. As **Keynes** observed, investment ultimately rests on "animal spirits"; the task of policy is to make that confidence general rather than exceptional.

## VALUE ADDITIONS (use 2–3, not all)

1. **Data anchors (pick two, no more):** GFCF **+11.9%** in Q1 FY27 vs **5.8%** a year earlier; industrial credit **+19.2%** (June 2026) vs **6.3%**; private corporate capex **₹30.3 lakh crore** in FY25 (+7%); capacity utilisation **75.6%**; Union capex **₹12.2 lakh crore**, effective capex **4.4% of GDP**; fiscal deficit **4.3%**, debt-to-GDP **55.6%** (BE 2026–27) against a **50% ±1 by FY31** target.
2. **Concepts that lift the answer:** the **accelerator principle** (investment responds to the *rate of change* of demand); **ICOR** (a rising ICOR means each unit of growth costs more capital — the efficiency, not just the volume, of investment matters); **crowding in vs crowding out** of public capex; **employment elasticity of growth**; the **"twin balance sheet advantage"** (Economic Survey 2021–22's phrase, inverting Arvind Subramanian's original "twin balance sheet problem").
3. **Thinkers/frames:** **Keynes on "animal spirits"** for the confidence argument; **Kaldor's growth laws** (manufacturing as the engine of productivity growth) for why the *composition* of investment matters; **Dani Rodrik on premature deindustrialisation** for why capital-intensive investment cannot substitute for labour-absorbing manufacturing.
4. **Institutional anchors:** the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016** for balance-sheet repair; the **National Infrastructure Pipeline** and **Asset Monetisation Plan** for public capex; the **Vijay Kelkar Committee (2015) on revisiting the PPP model** — an excellent, under-used citation when arguing that private participation in infrastructure needs better risk-sharing, not just more announcements.
5. **One line on complementarities (borrow from this week):** investment concentrates where **land title is clear and municipal infrastructure works** — connecting to DILRMP 3.0 and municipal finance shows examiners you can think across GS2 and GS3. Use one line only.

## SELF-CHECK BEFORE YOU COMPARE

- Did you *analyse* (decompose and test) rather than *describe*? "Critically analyse" rewards separating cyclical from structural drivers — most scripts will simply list "reasons for investment revival".
- Did you make the **employment** argument explicitly? An answer that celebrates capex without asking *what kind of capex* misses the sharpest half of the question.
- Did you use at least **one number in the introduction** and **one concept (accelerator / ICOR / crowding-in)** in the body? Concepts, not adjectives, are what separate a 9 from a 12 in GS3.
- Is your conclusion **conditional and forward-looking** ("the cycle will broaden only if…") rather than a restatement?
- Did you avoid the two standard traps: writing a "public vs private investment" essay, and writing a generic "make in India / ease of doing business" list?
