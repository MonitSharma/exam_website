## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> *"International law is the only protection the weak have, and the first thing the strong set aside."*
>
> With the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize recognising the cause of international criminal justice at a time when the ICC and other bodies face political pressure, **critically examine** the tension between sovereignty and accountability for mass atrocities. **Reflect** on how a rising power like India, which is not a party to the Rome Statute, should position itself.

## MODEL ANSWER (≈250 words)

**Introduction**
After Nuremberg, the idea took root that some crimes, genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity, concern humanity and not only the State where they occur. The Rome Statute (1998; in force 2002) created the permanent ICC on that premise. Yet enforcement still depends on States, and the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize to jurist Navi Pillay comes as the Court faces open political attack.

**Why accountability matters**
- **Deterrence and dignity:** impunity invites repetition; the Rwanda tribunal's recognition of sexual violence as an instrument of genocide changed how such crimes are prosecuted.
- **Protection of the weak:** domestic courts often cannot try crimes in which the State is complicit; the ICC acts on *complementarity*, only when States are unwilling or unable.

**The sovereignty and selectivity objection**
- **Selective justice:** the Court's reach depends on membership and Security Council referral. Major powers (the US, Russia, China) are outside it, and the P5 veto can shield allies; this fuels the charge of "justice for the weak".
- **Politics of enforcement:** the Court has no police, relies on cooperation, and now faces sanctions and non-cooperation.
- **Sovereign equality:** Article 2(1) of the UN Charter; critics fear politically motivated prosecutions.

**India's position**
India is not a party, citing concerns over jurisdiction over non-parties, the Security Council's role and the risk of politicised use. Yet India's civilisational and constitutional commitments, **Article 51(c)** (respect for international law and treaty obligations) and its use of international norms in domestic law (*Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*, 1997), point to engagement, not indifference. A rising power wanting a rules-based order cannot ask for rules only when they favour it.

**Conclusion**
India can stay outside the Statute while championing reform: equal application, curbing the veto's reach over justice, strengthening domestic capacity to try grave crimes, and supporting ad hoc or hybrid mechanisms. Accountability delayed is not accountability denied, and a Global South voice on selectivity is a strength, not a retreat.

**Value-additions**
1. **Article 51(c), Constitution** and ***Vishaka* (1997)**: international norms informing domestic law.
2. **Principle of complementarity** (Rome Statute): the ICC as a court of last resort, which answers the sovereignty objection partly.
3. **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)**, 2005 World Summit: sovereignty as responsibility, and the debate over its selective use.
