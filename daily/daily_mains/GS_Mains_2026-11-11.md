## 📖 MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

**Introduction.** From the Kol (1831-32) and Santhal Hul (1855-56) to the Munda Ulgulan (1899-1900), tribal uprisings were triggered when colonial revenue, forest and legal regimes dissolved community control over land and forest. Birsa Munda's call to expel *dikus* (outsiders) shows the revolts were rooted in livelihood and identity, yet they also carried a political claim.

**Defence of a way of life**
- **Land:** Zamindari and rent regimes in Chhotanagpur eroded the *khuntkatti* (joint-clan) system; moneylenders, thikadars and traders turned Mundas into tenants and labourers.
- **Forest:** Colonial forest laws restricted shifting cultivation and customary access; forced labour (*veth begari*) added to the burden.
- **Culture:** Missionary activity and state courts undermined customary law; Birsa's *Birsait* faith reasserted tribal religion and moral order.

**But also political**
- Birsa spoke of *abua raj* ("our rule") and urged non-payment of rent; attacks targeted police posts, churches and landlords — a claim of self-rule, not only economic relief.
- Earlier petitioning (*Sardari Larai*) failed, so the revolt moved to the political arena.

**State response**
- *Colonial:* Santhal Parganas carved out after the Hul; Chotanagpur Tenancy Act 1908 restricted transfer of tribal land — a reactive, partial concession even as forest control tightened.
- *Post-colonial:* Fifth/Sixth Schedules, PESA 1996 and Forest Rights Act 2006, whose preamble acknowledges the "historical injustice" done to forest-dwellers; but land alienation, weak gram sabha implementation and displacement persist.

**Conclusion.** The revolts were both: a defence of habitat that became a politics of autonomy. Honouring Birsa on Janjatiya Gaurav Diwas is meaningful only if FRA titles, PESA consent and land-alienation safeguards are enforced on the ground.

1. **Legislative:** Chotanagpur Tenancy Act 1908 (post-Munda revolt); Santhal Parganas Act 1855; Forest Rights Act 2006 preamble — "historical injustice".
