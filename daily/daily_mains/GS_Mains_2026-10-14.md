## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> India's female labour force participation has risen in recent years, yet women's share in quality, paid employment remains low. **Critically analyse** the social factors that constrain women's participation in the workforce, and **suggest** measures to convert higher participation into real economic empowerment. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Directive: "Critically analyse"** — break the issue into parts, weigh the apparent success (rising LFPR) against its quality, and end with workable measures.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

**Introduction**
Female LFPR (15+) was **40.0% in PLFS 2025**, well above the levels of the late 2010s, but only about one-quarter of urban women are in the labour force (27.7%). Most working women (**64.2%**) are self-employed, often as unpaid helpers in family farms or enterprises. So the rise reflects *more work, not necessarily better work*.

**Social factors constraining participation**
- **Unpaid care burden:** Time Use Survey 2024 shows women spend about 289 min/day on unpaid domestic work against 88 min for men; 81.5% of women take part versus 27.1% of men.
- **Patriarchal norms and "status" effects:** Family honour and the idea of the male breadwinner discourage outside work, especially for married urban women.
- **Marriage, motherhood and safety:** Career breaks after childbirth; unsafe transport and workplaces; limited crèches.
- **Skill and occupational segregation:** Women cluster in low-productivity agriculture, home-based work and care jobs; the gender wage gap persists.
- **Measurement gap:** Unpaid and seasonal work is under-valued in national accounts, hiding women's contribution.
- **Distress-driven entry:** Much rural participation is from income shocks, not empowerment.

**Critical view**
Rising participation is welcome, but if it is distress-driven and unpaid it may add to women's "double burden" rather than reduce it. Regional variation (higher in hill and tribal areas, lower in the Hindi belt) shows that norms matter as much as economics.

**Way forward**
- Expand crèches and care infrastructure (Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act 2017: 26 weeks' leave and crèche in establishments with 50+ employees; National Creche Scheme).
- Safe mobility, hostels and flexible or remote work; skilling linked to growth sectors.
- SHG-to-enterprise pathways, credit and market linkage (DAY-NRLM, Mudra).
- Gender-responsive budgeting; recognise unpaid work through time-use data.
- Engage men and families through norm-change campaigns.

**Conclusion**
Participation is the entry ticket; *decent work, shared care and safety* are what convert it into empowerment. A "Viksit Bharat" cannot be built with half its human capital under-used.

### Value-additions (use 2–3 in the exam)
1. **Claudia Goldin (Nobel, Economics 2023):** the *U-shaped* female participation curve. Participation falls as incomes rise (stigma on women's work, shift out of farm work) and recovers only when education and service-sector jobs expand. India sits near the bottom of the "U".
2. **Time Use Survey 2024 (NSO):** the 289 vs 88 min/day gap, which makes the care economy visible. Cite it to argue for care infrastructure.
3. **Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017** and the **National Creche Scheme**, as policy levers. Also note the **Social Security Code 2020**'s expanded coverage. *(Verify the current implementation status before quoting.)*
4. **Thinker/concept:** Amartya Sen's "agency" and "capabilities" view, in which work matters for empowerment only when it gives women voice and bargaining power.

### Common mistakes to avoid
- Treating a higher LFPR as plain good news without discussing job quality.
- Listing only schemes; examiners reward analysis of *social norms and care work*.
- Quoting precise figures without the year or the source (always write "PLFS 2025", "TUS 2024").
