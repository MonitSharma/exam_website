# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-22 (GS3 — Economy: Agriculture, Prices & Farm Incomes)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Tuesday = GS3 (Economy). One question to write today; the model answer is below the divider — attempt it on paper first.*

> **This week's hook:** India has spent the last eighteen months defending the farmer on every front except the one that decides his income.
>
> **At the border**, the defence held. The **India–US interim trade agreement (announced 6 February 2026)** cut the US reciprocal tariff on Indian goods from **50% to 18%**, and Commerce Minister **Piyush Goyal told Parliament on 4 February 2026** that agriculture and dairy had been protected: **dairy, rice, wheat, maize, sugar, soybean, poultry, a wide range of spices** and several preserved vegetables were kept **outside India's tariff concessions** altogether. Agri-exports meanwhile held up better than feared — **$53.1 billion in 2025–26, up 2.3%**, just short of the **$53.2 billion** record of 2022–23, led by non-basmati rice, buffalo meat, marine products and coffee.
>
> **At the farm gate**, the price announcements kept coming. The CCEA cleared MSP for **14 kharif crops for marketing season 2026–27** with an estimated payout of about **₹2.60 lakh crore** — the sharpest hikes deliberately tilted towards diversification crops: **sunflower seed +₹622/quintal, cotton +₹557, sesamum +₹500**. For **rabi marketing season 2026–27** (approved October 2025), **wheat MSP is ₹2,585/quintal (+₹160)**, with **safflower +₹600, masur +₹300, rapeseed-mustard +₹250, gram +₹225**. The missions are real too: the **Mission for Aatmanirbharta in Pulses (launched 11 October 2025, ₹11,440 crore, 2025–26 to 2030–31)** targets **242 → 350 lakh tonnes**, and pulse production has already moved from **242.46 lakh tonnes (2023–24) to 274.09 lakh tonnes (2025–26, 3rd Advance Estimate)** while imports fell from **72.56 to 59.64 lakh tonnes**. **PM-DDKY**, approved for **100 low-productivity districts** over six years, saw its average composite score across six objectives rise from **22.54 to 38.85 between April and July 2026** — with irrigation (1.65 → 4.20) improving fastest and **post-harvest storage (2.61 → 3.82) still the weakest link**.
>
> **And yet the arithmetic of the sector has not moved.** Agriculture and allied activities grew just **3.6% in real GVA terms in Q1 FY27 (MoSPI, 31 August 2026)**, down from **4.4%** a year earlier, even as nominal GVA growth rose to 7.5% — a gap that tells you prices, not output, did the work. The sector still supports close to **half of India's workforce** while producing roughly **a sixth of GVA**, which is simply another way of saying that value added per worker in agriculture is a fraction of that outside it. The **Shanta Kumar High Level Committee (2015)** found that only about **6% of farmers actually sell at MSP**, with roughly **70% of procurement concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Telangana**. The scale of price support beyond cereals remains modest — under **PM-AASHA (₹7,200 crore in 2026–27)**, the Price Support Scheme sanction for the **summer 2026 season** came to **48,298 MT of moong, 97,970 MT of urad and 41,718 MT of groundnut**, against a PSS ceiling of **25% of a State's seasonal production**. And the **Budget 2026–27** numbers state the priority plainly: the **Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers' Welfare gets about ₹1.33 lakh crore** (of which **PM-KISAN alone is ₹63,500 crore — roughly 45%, unchanged from RE 2025–26**), while the **fertiliser subsidy alone is ₹1.71 lakh crore** — *more than the entire agriculture ministry's budget*.
>
> The market-side reform, meanwhile, has stalled where it always stalls. The **draft National Policy Framework on Agricultural Marketing (25 November 2024)** proposed an empowered committee of State marketing ministers, private markets and direct purchase, is **silent on a legal MSP guarantee**, and offers a **PMFBY-style price insurance scheme** instead; Punjab's farm unions read it as the repealed farm laws returning through the back door. Nor do we know, with any precision, what has happened to farm incomes since **2018–19**, when the **NSS 77th round Situation Assessment Survey** put the average agricultural household's monthly income at **₹10,218** (**₹8,337** on the imputed-cost basis) and — the number that should reframe this entire debate — showed **wages contributing about 40% of that income against roughly 37% from crop production**. The **next SAS is being conducted only now, July 2026 – June 2027**.
>
> That gap — *between a price policy that is generous, protected and well-publicised, and an income outcome that depends on things price policy does not touch* — is today's GS3 theme. It is deliberately distinct from this month's GS3 questions on **private capex (15 September)**, the **Labour Codes (8 September)** and **carbon markets/CBAM (17 September)**, from **GST 2.0 (23 June)**, **flexible inflation targeting (30 June)** and **trade diversification (11 August)** — though the Labour Codes material is legitimately borrowable for *one* line on the rural non-farm worker, and the land-titling answer (14 September) for *one* line on tenancy and credit access.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> India has raised minimum support prices year after year, shielded agriculture in its trade agreements, and expanded direct income support — yet agriculture still supports nearly half the workforce while producing about a sixth of gross value added. **Critically analyse** why a price-centred agricultural policy has failed to make farming remunerative, and assess what a shift towards markets, diversification and non-farm linkages would require.

**(Directive: *critically analyse* — break the policy into its components and test each against evidence, rather than defending or condemning it wholesale. Do **not** write a descriptive "problems of Indian agriculture: small holdings, monsoon dependence, low mechanisation" answer; that is a 4-mark answer. The marks lie in (a) separating **price policy** from **income policy** — showing that MSP reaches few farmers, few crops and few States, and that even for the average agricultural household crop income is *not* the largest income source; (b) taking the **fiscal composition** seriously — input subsidies and cereal procurement crowd out investment in irrigation, storage and research; (c) the **political economy** of why marketing reform stalls, treated as a legitimate trust problem rather than farmer irrationality; and (d) a forward-looking close that **sequences** the transition — what must be built *before* price support is diluted, not instead of it. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (frame + anchor)

A price is not an income. India's agricultural policy has been built largely around the first — MSP for 22 crops, tariff protection, and now ₹63,500 crore of PM-KISAN transfers — while the income problem lies in **low value added per worker**: agriculture supports close to half the workforce but generates about a sixth of GVA, and grew just **3.6% in real GVA in Q1 FY27 (MoSPI)**. The binding constraint is structural, not declaratory.

### Why price-centred policy under-delivers

- **Narrow reach:** the **Shanta Kumar Committee (2015)** found only ~**6% of farmers** sell at MSP; roughly **70% of procurement** is concentrated in five States. An announced price that most farmers cannot access is a signal, not an entitlement.
- **Crop and regional distortion:** assured cereal procurement locked Punjab–Haryana into a water-intensive paddy–wheat cycle, while pulses and oilseeds — where imports persist — lacked comparable assurance. Higher MSP hikes for sunflower, sesamum and masur try to correct this through prices alone, when the real deterrents are **marketing risk and missing post-harvest infrastructure**.
- **Income composition:** SAS 2018–19 showed **wages (~40%) exceeded crop income (~37%)** for the average agricultural household. Price policy therefore touches under two-fifths of farm-household income.
- **Fiscal crowding-out:** the **fertiliser subsidy (₹1.71 lakh crore, BE 2026–27) exceeds the entire agriculture ministry budget (~₹1.33 lakh crore)**. Subsidy substitutes for investment in irrigation, storage and research — where returns are demonstrably higher.
- **Marketing failure:** small marketable surplus, fragmented mandis and weak aggregation mean the farmer is a **price-taker twice over** — for inputs and for output.

### What a shift would require

- **Build before you dilute:** PM-DDKY's own scorecard identifies **post-harvest storage as the weakest objective (2.61 → 3.82, Apr–Jul 2026)**. Storage, cold chains, grading and market density must precede any withdrawal of price assurance.
- **Aggregation with teeth:** **10,000 FPOs** have been registered; the task now is turning registration into bargaining power, credit access and processing capacity.
- **Diversification with demand:** horticulture, livestock and fisheries already contribute disproportionately to farm income growth; they need cold chains and veterinary services, not MSP.
- **Rebuild trust before reform:** the draft **National Policy Framework on Agricultural Marketing (2024)** stalled because it was read as deregulation without assurance. Reform sequenced as *assurance first, liberalisation second* is the only politically survivable path.
- **Exit as policy:** with agriculture a **State subject (Entry 14, List II)**, the Centre's most useful lever is rural non-farm employment — the **Dalwai Committee's** insistence that income, not output, is the objective points to off-farm absorption as much as on-farm productivity.

### Conclusion

India has protected the farmer at the border and supported him at the till, but has not yet made farming a business. The transition is not from support to no support; it is from **price support to income and market support** — storage, aggregation, diversification and a rural non-farm economy that lets the marginal farmer leave agriculture without leaving prosperity behind. The **Situation Assessment Survey now under way (July 2026 – June 2027)** will, for the first time in seven years, tell us honestly whether that transition has begun.

2. **Committees (the two that matter here):** the **Shanta Kumar High Level Committee on FCI restructuring (2015)** — for the reach and concentration of MSP, and for the case for direct benefit transfer over physical procurement; the **Ashok Dalwai Committee on Doubling Farmers' Income (2016–18)** — for the decisive conceptual move from *output* to *income*, and its seven sources of income growth. The **National Commission on Farmers (Swaminathan, 2004–06)** and **C2+50%** should be cited once, precisely, not as a slogan.
3. **Constitutional/institutional anchor:** **agriculture is Entry 14 and markets & fairs Entry 28 of the State List (Seventh Schedule)** — which is *why* the 2020 farm laws were vulnerable and why the 2024 draft marketing framework proceeds by persuading States rather than legislating. One line on this signals GS2–GS3 integration.
4. **Concepts that lift the answer:** **marketable vs marketed surplus**; **terms of trade for agriculture**; **implicit taxation of agriculture through export restrictions** (OECD-ICRIER work found India's producer support persistently negative because of trade controls) — an excellent counterweight to "subsidy" framing; **value added per worker** as the real measure of agrarian distress; **Lewis-type structural transformation** and why India's has been incomplete.
5. **One line on complementarities (borrow from this week):** secure **tenancy and land title** determine whether a tenant farmer can access institutional credit at all — link back to the 14 September land-records answer in a single sentence, and to the Labour Codes answer (8 September) for the rural non-farm worker's social security. One line each, no more.

## SELF-CHECK BEFORE YOU COMPARE

- Did you *analyse* rather than *list problems*? "Critically analyse" rewards separating **price policy** from **income policy** — most scripts will write a general "agrarian distress" essay and score 5–6.
- Did you use the **income-composition** point (wages > crop income)? It is the single most powerful fact in this answer and almost nobody writes it.
- Did you treat farmer resistance to marketing reform as a **rational trust problem**, not as obstruction? Examiners mark down answers that are contemptuous of the constituency they describe.
- Did your body have at least **one fiscal argument** (subsidy vs investment) and **one institutional argument** (FPOs, mandis, State List)? GS3 economy answers that stay purely descriptive lose the analytical marks.
- Is the conclusion **sequenced and conditional** ("assurance first, liberalisation second") rather than a wish-list of "way forward" bullets?
- Did you avoid the three standard traps: writing a *"MSP: meaning, calculation, A2+FL vs C2"* answer; demanding a legal MSP guarantee without costing it; and treating "doubling farmers' income" as a slogan rather than a measurable target with a defined base year.
