## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction**
India backed the Republic (Salma Dam, Zaranj–Delaram highway, Afghan Parliament building, ~$3 bn aid) and shut its mission after the 2021 takeover. Since 2022 it has re-engaged step by step — a technical team in Kabul, then an upgrade to an embassy (2025) and the exchange of diplomats — all **without formal recognition**. This is pragmatic realism, but not a complete abandonment of values.

**Why the shift: interests**
- **Security:** prevent Afghan soil being used by anti-India groups (LeT, JeM); Taliban–Pakistan friction (border closure since Oct 2025) gives India room.
- **Strategic space:** avoid ceding Kabul to Pakistan, China or Russia; Russia has already recognised the regime.
- **Connectivity & economy:** Afghanistan as a gateway to Central Asia; Chabahar–INSTC route; Central Asia now carries ~48% of Afghan imports, Iran ~46%.
- **Humanitarian & people-to-people:** aid (wheat, medicines), protecting past investments, students and the Afghan diaspora.

**Why it is not purely interest-based (continuity)**
- No formal recognition; India backs an "inclusive and representative" government and engages through UN/regional platforms (Moscow Format, Doha-type talks).
- Aid is routed through humanitarian channels; traditional civilisational ties to the Afghan people, not the regime.

**Critical concerns**
- **Values dilution:** the Taliban's restrictions on women's education/work and rights; ICC arrest warrants (July 2025) against top Taliban leaders — engagement risks legitimising them.
- **Limited leverage:** no land access (Pakistan route shut), dependence on Iran — itself under sanctions and wary on Chabahar.
- **Security risk:** ISKP, TTP spillover and the Taliban's capacity to restrain anti-India groups are untested.
- **Bargaining weakness:** India enters late; China and Russia hold stronger cards.

**Way forward**
Calibrated engagement: conditional benchmarks (women's rights, counter-terror assurances), humanitarian-plus development projects, secure connectivity via Chabahar and Central Asia, coordinate with Russia, Iran and Central Asian states, and keep the people of Afghanistan as the primary partner.

**Conclusion**
India's Afghanistan policy is best read as **"principled pragmatism"** — engagement to protect enduring interests (security, connectivity, influence) while withholding recognition as leverage. Its success will depend on whether engagement yields tangible security and rights gains rather than mere access.
