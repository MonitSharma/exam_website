## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> Article 44 of the Constitution directs the State to "endeavour to secure for the citizens a Uniform Civil Code throughout the territory of India," yet for over seven decades this remained a dormant Directive Principle. With **Uttarakhand and Gujarat** now enacting their own state-level Uniform Civil Codes, **critically examine** the constitutional and social case for a Uniform Civil Code, and the concerns raised by its piecemeal, state-by-state implementation.

## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction

Article 44, a Directive Principle of State Policy under Part IV, enjoins the State to secure a Uniform Civil Code (UCC) for all citizens, envisaging one common law governing marriage, divorce, succession and maintenance in place of religion-based personal laws. Long treated as politically dormant since the Constituent Assembly debates — where Dr. B.R. Ambedkar clarified it was an aspirational goal, not an immediate compulsion — the UCC has now moved from theory to practice: **Uttarakhand (2024, in force January 2025)** and **Gujarat (Bill passed March 2026)** have each enacted their own codes, making this a live test of how a Directive Principle translates into sub-national law.

### The Constitutional and Social Case for a UCC

- **Gender justice and Article 14/15**: Personal laws across communities have historically diverged on inheritance, maintenance and marital rights, often disadvantaging women. The Supreme Court in *Shah Bano* (1985) and *Sarla Mudgal* (1995) urged the State to move towards a UCC precisely to end this discrimination and litigation churn.
- **National integration**: Ambedkar and the Constituent Assembly saw a common code as consolidating a shared citizenship transcending religious identity, reducing the scope for law itself to entrench communal difference.
- **Simplification and rule of law**: A single code would reduce the complexity of adjudicating multiple, often uncodified, personal law systems and curb forum-shopping between religious and civil provisions (as in conversion-linked remarriage cases, *John Vallamattom*, 2003).

### Concerns with a Piecemeal, State-wise Rollout

- **Federalism and uniformity-in-name-only**: Entry 5 of the Concurrent List allows states to legislate on marriage and personal law, but a UCC enacted state-by-state (Uttarakhand, then Gujarat, each with its own drafting choices) risks producing not one uniform code but several *state* codes — undermining the very idea of "uniform... throughout the territory of India."
- **Selective exclusion**: Both states have excluded Scheduled Tribes to protect customary rights under Article 371/Fifth-Sixth Schedule protections, which is constitutionally prudent but also concedes that a truly universal code is not yet politically or socially feasible.
- **Minority apprehension and Article 25-28**: Personal law reform touches freedom of religion; without wide consultation, top-down codification can be perceived as majoritarian imposition rather than a shared reform, deepening communal distrust even where the underlying provisions (bigamy ban, equal inheritance) are gender-progressive.
- **Implementation asymmetry**: Registration mandates, live-in disclosure requirements and penalties vary by state, creating unequal citizen experience and possible litigation on reasonableness and privacy (right to privacy under Article 21, *K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017, is already being invoked against live-in registration clauses).

### Conclusion

A Uniform Civil Code remains a legitimate constitutional aspiration rooted in gender equality and national integration, and the Uttarakhand-Gujarat enactments show the Directive Principle can move from aspiration to statute. But a code built state-by-state, without a wide-based national consultation of the kind the 21st Law Commission (2018) called for — which found a UCC "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage" absent broader consensus — risks producing fragmented uniformity rather than genuine national uniformity. The path forward lies in the 22nd Law Commission's ongoing public consultation feeding into a nationally deliberated, rights-respecting code, rather than a patchwork of state laws that could, ironically, multiply personal-law regimes instead of unifying them.

### Value Additions

1. **Law Commission of India, 21st Report (2018)**: Concluded a UCC was "neither necessary nor desirable" at that stage; recommended codifying and reforming personal laws within each community first. The 22nd Law Commission reopened public consultations on UCC in 2023.
2. **Case law**: *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985) and *Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) — both flagged the absence of a UCC as a source of injustice and urged legislative action under Article 44.
3. **Constituent Assembly Debates**: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's assurance that Article 44 was aspirational and that future Parliaments, not the Constitution itself, would decide the timing — relevant to the current debate on whether states can/should pre-empt Parliament.
