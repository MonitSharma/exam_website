## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction (≈40 words)
The Himalaya is young, tectonically active fold-mountain terrain, so it is naturally hazard-prone. Hazards become disasters when exposure and vulnerability rise. The 1976 Mishra Committee on Joshimath, the 2023 Sikkim glacial-lake flood and the Supreme Court's 2025 remarks on Himachal all point to a disaster that is part natural and part man-made.

### Body

**A. Physical (natural) factors**
- **Geology and tectonics.** The Indian plate is still pushing into Eurasia. The Himalaya has active thrusts such as the Main Central Thrust, steep slopes and weak, fractured rock, so it is landslide- and earthquake-prone. Towns like Joshimath sit on old landslide and glacial debris, not solid rock (Mishra Committee, 1976).
- **Climate and rainfall.** The monsoon meets the orographic barrier and dumps intense rain. A warmer atmosphere holds about 7% more moisture per degree of warming, which feeds cloudbursts (more than 100 mm/hr over a small area). Western Disturbances interacting with the monsoon can compound extremes.
- **Cryosphere change.** Glaciers retreat and permafrost thaws, forming moraine-dammed lakes. In Oct 2023 the South Lhonak lake in Sikkim burst; a study reported about 55 deaths and 70 missing, with climate change as a contributing factor.

**B. Anthropogenic factors**
- **Infrastructure without geological assessment.** Hydropower projects, tunnels, and road widening (Char Dham) cut slopes and destabilise them. Debris dumped into rivers worsens floods downstream.
- **Cumulative impact ignored.** EIAs assess projects one by one, not basin by basin (observed by the Supreme Court, 2025).
- **Unplanned urbanisation and tourism.** Multi-storey buildings, construction on riverbeds and flood plains, and poor drainage and sewerage push towns past their carrying capacity.
- **Land-use change.** Deforestation and loss of slope cover reduce infiltration, so runoff and landslide risk rise.
- **Weak governance.** Master plans and zoning are poorly enforced, and expert warnings go unimplemented (the Mishra Committee safeguards were never institutionalised).

**C. Critical view**
- Blaming only climate change excuses poor planning. Blaming only development ignores genuine needs: connectivity, defence and livelihoods in border areas.
- The trade-off is real. Wide roads serve strategic and pilgrim needs, but they carry ecological costs. The answer is *how* to build, not *whether*.

### Way forward
1. **Carrying-capacity studies** before approving new construction in Himalayan towns (NGT ordered such studies in March 2023; the Supreme Court has also taken up the issue).
2. **Basin-level cumulative impact assessment** and mandatory geological and geotechnical clearance before projects.
3. **Hazard zoning and no-construction zones**, with enforced building codes and drainage plans.
4. **Early warning and monitoring.** Automatic weather stations, glacial-lake monitoring and community-based warning systems.
5. **Eco-friendly engineering.** Bio-engineering, check dams, afforestation, and ecological flow in rivers.
6. **Local participation.** Involve residents and Panchayats, and rehabilitate those in danger zones.

### Conclusion (≈35 words)
The Himalayan crisis is a *hazard made into a disaster by human choice*. India should move from reactive relief to anticipatory planning, treating the mountains' ecological limits as a design constraint. Development that respects those limits is also the most durable security for hill communities and the plains downstream.
