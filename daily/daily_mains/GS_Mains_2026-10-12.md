> **This week's hook:** Parliament has re-written the tribunal framework — again.
>
> - **The Bill.** The **Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026** was passed by the **Lok Sabha on 10 Aug 2026 (reportedly without debate amid Opposition protests)** and the **Rajya Sabha on 11 Aug 2026** (LiveLaw; ETV Bharat). It **repeals the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** and creates a **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** — a chairperson (a former SC judge/HC Chief Justice), two judicial and two technical members — to run selection, review performance, oversee complaints against members and maintain a National Tribunals Data Grid. It covers 16 tribunals/authorities (CAT, SATs, DRT, AFT, NGT, ITAT etc.). Law Minister Meghwal said jurisdiction of tribunals is unchanged.
> - **Why now.** The Statement of Objects refers to the Supreme Court's directions on an NTC. Press commentary (Vajiram & Ravi) notes a **Nov 2025 two-judge Bench ruling** that struck down 2021-Act provisions as an "impermissible legislative override" and asked the Centre to set up an NTC within four months. Critics say the Centre still appoints the NTC's members and keeps rule-making power over qualifications, service conditions and removal.
> - **Why it matters for GS2:** separation of powers, judicial independence, legislative "override" of judgments, and the quality of access to justice.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> Tribunals were created to deliver specialised and speedy justice, yet Parliament and the Supreme Court have repeatedly clashed over how they are staffed and controlled. **Critically examine** the constitutional basis and evolution of tribunals in India, and **evaluate** whether the Tribunals Reforms Bill, 2026 can reconcile executive involvement with judicial independence. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction
Tribunals are quasi-judicial bodies set up under **Articles 323A and 323B** (inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**, on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation) to provide expert, speedy and cheaper adjudication and to decongest courts. Their legitimacy, however, rests on a basic-structure condition: independence from the executive, which is often the biggest litigant before them.

### Body

**1. Evolution through the courts**
- **L. Chandra Kumar (1997):** tribunals' decisions are subject to High Court review under Arts 226/227; judicial review is part of the basic structure; tribunals are "supplementary", not substitutes for High Courts.
- **Madras Bar Association cases:** the Court insisted on judicial-majority selection, security of tenure and parity with courts in service conditions.
- **Rojer Mathew (2019):** struck down rules under the Finance Act, 2017 that merged tribunals and gave the executive control over appointments and conditions; urged a National Tribunals Commission.
- **Tribunals Reforms Ordinance/Act, 2021:** re-introduced key provisions (executive-dominated search-cum-selection committee, a two-name panel, short tenure), which the Court again found problematic; a Nov 2025 Bench reportedly called it a legislative override and directed an NTC.

**2. What the 2026 Bill does right**
- Creates a **dedicated NTC** headed by a former SC judge/HC Chief Justice, a long-standing judicial demand, so the line ministries no longer handle appointments.
- Provides for **performance review, complaint inquiry and a National Tribunals Data Grid**, which improve accountability and transparency.
- Fixes **time-bound appointments** and removal grounds, easing the vacancy crisis that cripples tribunals.

**3. Where it falls short (critical view)**
- The **Centre still appoints the NTC's chair, members and secretary, funds it, and frames rules** on qualifications and service conditions — so the independence is structural only in part.
- **Judicial primacy** (CJI-led selection) is replaced with consultation, which risks repeating the pattern the Court has already rejected.
- **Passage without debate** weakens legislative scrutiny, and re-enacting struck-down features invites another round of litigation (the "judicial–legislative ping-pong" problem).
- **Tribunalisation** also pushes litigants to the Supreme Court via direct appeals, which defeats the aim of decongestion.

**4. Way forward**
A truly independent **NTC with judicial majority and a ring-fenced budget**; a uniform, statutory service code; and a periodic **parliamentary committee review** of which tribunals are worth keeping.

### Conclusion
Tribunals will only deserve public trust if they look and function independently of their largest litigant. The 2026 Bill is a **step in the right direction** by institutionalising the NTC. Whether it passes judicial scrutiny will depend on whether the executive's residual control is read as administrative support or as interference. A durable settlement needs **dialogue between Parliament and the Court, not repeated override**.

### Value-additions
- **Cases/constitutional:** Arts 323A/323B; L. Chandra Kumar (1997); Rojer Mathew (2019); Madras Bar Association line of cases; Art 50 (separation of judiciary from executive).
- **Committee:** Swaran Singh Committee (1976), the source of Arts 323A/323B.
- **Thinker/concept:** Montesquieu's separation of powers; the "**legislative override**" doctrine — Parliament can remove the *basis* of a judgment, but cannot simply re-enact what was struck down.
