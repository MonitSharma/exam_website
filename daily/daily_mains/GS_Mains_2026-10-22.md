## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction**
Delhi-NCR's air regularly breaches the national annual PM2.5 standard (40 µg/m³, NAAQS 2009) and is far above the WHO 2021 guideline (5 µg/m³), with sharp winter peaks. The response is largely seasonal: the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), operated by the statutory Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM, 2021), imposes curbs in stages once the AQI has already deteriorated.

**Why the emergency approach fails**
- **Reactive, not preventive.** GRAP stages trigger on AQI levels, so curbs (construction bans, truck entry limits, school changes) arrive after exposure begins; they treat symptoms, not sources.
- **Source mix is year-round.** Vehicles, road and construction dust, industry, power and solid-fuel burning operate all year; stubble burning is a short seasonal spike on top, not the whole story. Meteorology (low wind, inversion) decides whether the base load becomes a crisis.
- **Airshed ≠ administrative boundary.** Pollutants cross state lines, but enforcement sits with separate governments (Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, UP, Rajasthan) with different capacities and incentives.
- **Weak incentives on the farm.** Burning is the cheapest option in a short sowing window; farmers bear the cost of alternatives while the health burden falls on others. Penalties are weakly enforced and paddy-centric procurement persists.
- **Enforcement and data gaps.** Patchy compliance checks, slow scrapping of old vehicles, thin monitoring outside the city, and reliance on court monitoring in place of routine administration.

**A year-round, airshed-based approach**
- **Airshed governance with accountability.** Use CAQM's mandate to set binding airshed-wide, source-wise reduction targets with state action plans and public dashboards; link central funding to performance (the 15th Finance Commission's air-quality grants for million-plus cities are a precedent).
- **Source-specific shifts.** Public transport and EVs, scrapping/retrofitting old diesel vehicles, cleaner fuels in industry, dust control at construction sites, clean cooking in the periphery.
- **Fix farm-fire incentives.** Support for in-situ management machinery and ex-situ residue markets (pellets, compressed biogas), crop diversification, direct-seeded rice, and procurement signals that do not default to water-intensive paddy; reward rather than only penalise.
- **Science and data.** Denser low-cost sensors, real-time source apportionment, and forecast-linked pre-emptive action 2–3 days before an inversion instead of AQI-triggered action.
- **Health and citizens.** Health-based advisories for schools and outdoor workers; community participation.

**Conclusion**
Air pollution is a public-health and governance problem that seasonal bans cannot solve. A cooperative airshed model, with farmer incentives, transparent state-level targets and sustained funding, should replace winter firefighting so that clean air becomes a year-round reality under Article 21.

**Value additions**
1. **Legal:** Right to a pollution-free environment is part of Art 21 (*Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar*, 1991); the *M.C. Mehta* line of cases drove Delhi measures such as CNG conversion; the **CAQM Act 2021** replaced EPCA and created a single regional authority with powers over adjoining states.
2. **Programme:** National Clean Air Programme (2019) — city-level PM reduction targets; verify current target/funding before quoting numbers.
3. **Comparison:** China's Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region-wide action plan combined industrial restructuring, coal-use caps and regional coordination — a model of airshed-scale governance, adapted to India's different source mix.
