# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-13 (Sunday — Reflective / Essay-style GS question)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Sunday = one reflective, cross-cutting question spanning Polity & Federalism (GS2), Population & Society (GS1) and Fiscal Federalism (GS3). Attempt it on paper first; the model answer is below the divider.*

> **This week's hook:** India's most consequential unresolved constitutional question is not in a courtroom — it is in a census schedule. **Article 82** requires that Lok Sabha seats be readjusted after every census; the **42nd Amendment (1976)** froze the inter-State allocation on the **1971 Census**, and the **84th Amendment (2001)** extended that freeze until *"the first census after 2026"* — explicitly, in its Statement of Objects and Reasons, as a **"motivational measure"** so that States could pursue population stabilisation without losing political weight. **Census 2027 carries a reference date of 1 March 2027.** The freeze therefore lapses by its own terms.
>
> The Centre moved first. On **16 April 2026** three Bills were introduced in the Lok Sabha — the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**, the **Delimitation Bill, 2026** and the **Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** — proposing to raise the Lok Sabha's ceiling from **550 to 850 members** (up to **815** from States, **35** from UTs), to let **Parliament decide by ordinary law** which census delimitation would use (the Delimitation Bill specified the latest *published* census, i.e. **2011**), and to delink the **106th Amendment's women's reservation** from the "first census after 2023" trigger. On **17 April 2026** the Constitution Amendment Bill was **defeated in the Lok Sabha — 298 ayes to 230 noes out of 528 present**, short of the two-thirds required; the two dependent Bills became infructuous and were withdrawn. It is among the very few constitutional amendments in Indian history to fail on the floor.
>
> The arithmetic explains the politics. On PRS projections using the 2011 Census with the House size unchanged, **Tamil Nadu falls 39 → 32** and **Kerala 20 → 15**, while **Uttar Pradesh rises 80 → 89**, **Bihar 40 → 46** and **Rajasthan 25 → 30**. Expanding the House does not dissolve the problem, only disguises it: at ~815 seats every State gains in absolute terms, yet **UP's share still rises from 14.73% to 16.36%** while **Kerala's falls from 3.68% to 2.71%**. A **Joint Action Committee** of southern and allied States, convened at **Chennai on 22 March 2025** under **CM M.K. Stalin**, had already resolved to seek a further **25-year extension** of the 1971 freeze. Meanwhile the **Rajya Sabha's 250-seat ceiling is untouched**, so a 815-member Lok Sabha would shift the LS:RS ratio from roughly **2.2:1 to 3.3:1** — weakening the federal chamber precisely where federal anxiety is highest.
>
> That collision — *between "one person, one vote, one value" and a federal bargain that rewarded States for demographic restraint* — is today's reflective theme, deliberately distinct from the last Sunday essay on **urban governance and municipal finance (6 September)**, from the **demographic dividend** essay (28 June), from the GS1 **caste-enumerated Census** question (1 July), and from this week's GS2 questions on **Article 142 (7 Sept)** and **BRICS (11 Sept)**.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (anchor + data)

Delimitation is the periodic readjustment of constituencies so that representation tracks population — mandated by **Article 82** after every census. The **42nd (1976)** and **84th (2001)** Amendments froze the inter-State allocation on the **1971 Census** until "the first census after 2026", expressly as an incentive for population stabilisation. With **Census 2027** (reference date 1 March 2027), that bargain expires. The **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**, which sought an 850-seat ceiling and delimitation on the 2011 Census, was **defeated in the Lok Sabha on 17 April 2026 (298:230)** — the deferral has been extended, not resolved.

### A. The democratic imperative to readjust

- **Unequal votes.** On 2011 figures, a Rajasthan MP represents ~27.4 lakh people, a Kerala MP ~16.7 lakh — a Kerala vote weighs over 1.6 times a Rajasthan vote. Ambedkar's promise of *"one man one vote and one vote one value"* (25 November 1949) is arithmetically unmet.
- **Under-representation overall.** One MP per ~26 lakh citizens, against ~7.7 lakh in the US and ~1 lakh in the UK, thins accountability and inflates constituency workload.
- **Frozen data distorts within States too**, entrenching rural over-representation as India urbanises.

### B. The federal objection, taken seriously

- **Penalising compliance.** States that met national population goals would lose weight for having done so — the State reneging on its own inducement, a fairness problem, not merely a political one.
- **No federal shock-absorber.** Unlike the US Senate, India's Rajya Sabha is itself population-weighted (Fourth Schedule); raising the Lok Sabha to 815 against a fixed 250 shifts the ratio from ~2.2:1 to ~3.3:1, weakening the federal chamber in joint sittings and Presidential elections.
- **Compounding fiscal grievance.** Seat loss lands atop existing disputes over devolution shares and cess-and-surcharge erosion.
- **Expansion is not a solution**, only an anaesthetic: relative share still shifts (UP 14.73%→16.36%; Kerala 3.68%→2.71%).

### C. Procedural concerns

Empowering Parliament to choose the census by **ordinary law** removes a constitutional guarantee and makes the timing of representation itself contestable. Women's reservation under the **106th Amendment (2023)** remains hostage to the deadlock — a second justice claim held up by a first.

### Conclusion — principles for a settlement

Neither indefinite freeze nor unilateral thaw is legitimate: the first corrodes democratic equality, the second the federal compact. A workable settlement would guarantee that **no State loses in absolute terms**, insulate the **Rajya Sabha** by making it a genuine chamber of States, constitutionally protect **fiscal shares** for demographic performers, and negotiate through the **Inter-State Council (Article 263)** rather than by floor majority. Representation is not only arithmetic; it is consent. India's task is not to choose between democracy and federalism, but to prove — as it did in 1976 and 2001 — that it can still bargain its way through the choice.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS TO WEAVE IN (pick 2–3, not all)

1. **Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949** — *"one man one vote and one vote one value"* as political democracy's promise, unmet in social and economic life. Deploy it as the *standard* the current allocation fails, not as decoration.
2. **Alfred Stepan's "demos-constraining" federalism** — federations deliberately over-represent smaller units in an upper chamber to constrain raw majoritarianism. India's Rajya Sabha, being population-weighted, is unusually *demos-enabling*, which is exactly why a Lok Sabha shock has nowhere to go. This is the single sharpest analytical point available on this question.
3. **US Permanent Apportionment Act, 1929** — the House fixed at 435 since 1929, so each census *redistributes* without expanding. India's proposal does the opposite. Useful comparative, but note the trade-off: a cap worsens the citizens-per-legislator problem India already has.
4. **Fifteenth Finance Commission** — used the 2011 population with a **12.5% weight for "demographic performance"** precisely to offset the penalty on States that reduced fertility; the **Sixteenth Finance Commission (Arvind Panagariya), report tabled 1 February 2026**, keeps the fiscal-federal conversation live. Shows the *fiscal* precedent for compensating demographic performers — the natural template for a *political* one.
5. **84th Amendment, Statement of Objects and Reasons (2001)** — the freeze described as a "motivational measure" for population stabilisation. Quoting the State's own stated inducement is the most economical way to make the fairness argument.
6. **87th Amendment, 2003** — allowed readjustment of constituencies *within* States on the 2001 Census without altering State totals; the last Delimitation Commission (2002) finalised its orders in 2008. Precedent that India has already separated *intra-State* from *inter-State* delimitation — a possible bridge.
7. **Sarkaria (1988) and Punchhi (2010) Commissions**, and **Article 263 / Inter-State Council** — the institutional route for negotiated federal settlements rather than floor votes.

## EXAMINER'S NOTE — how this answer earns marks

- **The trap** is writing a descriptive answer: what delimitation is, its history, the 1976/2001/2003 amendments, the Delimitation Commissions. That is a 6–7/15 answer. The directive is *critically examine* a **tension**, which requires you to argue both sides in their strongest form.
- **The differentiator** is Section B's second bullet — most candidates will say "southern states lose seats." Very few will notice that the Rajya Sabha cannot absorb the shock *because India's upper house is itself population-weighted*, unlike the US Senate. That single observation converts a current-affairs answer into a constitutional-design answer.
- **Do not take a partisan side.** The examiner is testing whether you can hold two legitimate claims simultaneously. The conclusion should offer *procedure* (consent, negotiation, compensation) rather than a verdict on which State deserves what.
- **Data discipline:** use two or three figures, not ten. TN 39→32 and Kerala 20→15 against UP 80→89 is enough; the share-shift pair (14.73%→16.36% vs 3.68%→2.71%) is the one that proves expansion doesn't solve it.
- **Time check:** ~22 minutes, ~250 words. If you overshoot on the intro, you will lose Section C and the settlement principles — which is where the top marks are.
