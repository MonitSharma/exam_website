> **This week's hook:** India has just *added judges* to its top court while its dockets keep growing.
>
> - **Capacity.** The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 was amended to raise sanctioned strength from **34 to 38** (including the CJI). A President's Ordinance (**16 May 2026**, Art. 123) did it first; Parliament passed the Bill on **5 August 2026**, with the Opposition boycotting the Rajya Sabha vote. Stated aim: "mounting case pendency" and efficiency.
> - **Backlog.** The Law Minister told Parliament (**23 July 2026**) that about **5.64 crore** cases are pending across courts, with **96,000+** in the Supreme Court (26 over 30 years old; 558 over 20 years) and **80,660** High Court cases over 30 years old. Earlier government data put pendency at **5.39 crore on 31 Dec 2025**, with the Supreme Court's pendency up about **11% in a year**. Treat the exact figures as indicative and check NJDG before quoting.
> - **This week's Supreme Court rulings** (LiveLaw's September list) were mostly about *process*: no forcible vehicle repossession by banks/NBFCs, written grounds for re-arrest, limits on reliance on custodial confessions in NSA detention.
>
> The reflective point is that a court can have the right to decide and still fail the citizen if the decision comes a generation late. This is deliberately distinct from the **Article 142 question (7 Sep)** (what the Court may do) and the **Governor/Speaker questions** (who decides). Today is about *when* justice arrives.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> *"A right that can be enforced only after a generation has passed is, to the person waiting, indistinguishable from a right denied."*
>
> With over five crore cases pending and the Supreme Court's sanctioned strength now raised to 38, **critically examine** whether adding judges can by itself solve India's pendency crisis. Reflect on what a realistic, multi-pronged programme for timely justice would involve. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

**Directive note:** "Critically examine" = test the claim (more judges = less backlog) against evidence, then give a balanced verdict and a way forward. Spend roughly 40% on why numbers alone fall short, 40% on the multi-pronged fix, 20% on intro and conclusion.

## MODEL ANSWER (≈250 words)

**Introduction**
Speedy trial is part of Article 21 (*Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar*, 1979) and flows from Article 39A's promise of equal access to justice. Yet roughly 5.6 crore cases are pending (Law Minister, July 2026), including 96,000+ in the Supreme Court. Raising the Supreme Court's strength to 38 helps at the apex, but pendency is mostly a district-court and process problem.

**Why more judges alone will not suffice**
- **Wrong tier:** the bulk of cases sit in district and subordinate courts and High Courts, where vacancies and slow appointments matter more than a 4-seat apex increase.
- **Demand outruns supply:** the Supreme Court's docket is swelled by SLPs and routine appeals; more benches simply attract more filings.
- **Government as chief litigant:** a large share of cases involve the State, and avoidable appeals persist despite the National Litigation Policy.
- **Process delay:** repeated adjournments, summons and service problems, slow forensic reports, and thin prosecution/investigation capacity.
- **Infrastructure and staff:** shortage of courtrooms, court staff and stenographers, and uneven digitisation.

**A multi-pronged way forward**
- **Fill vacancies on time:** a time-bound appointment calendar (MoP clarity); use Art. 224A ad hoc judges (*Lok Prahari v. Union of India*, 2021).
- **Triage and docket management:** case-flow rules, adjournment limits and priority for old cases.
- **Divert disputes out of court:** Mediation Act, 2023; pre-litigation mediation under the Commercial Courts Act; Lok Adalats; Gram Nyayalayas.
- **Cut State litigation:** screen appeals and discourage reflex appeals.
- **Technology:** e-Courts Phase III (₹7,210 crore) for e-filing, electronic summons and digital records, with safeguards for the digitally excluded.
- **Measure outcomes:** track disposal and age of cases, not just sanctioned strength.

**Conclusion**
Judges are necessary, not sufficient. Timely justice needs a system view across appointments, litigation discipline, procedure and technology. A court's legitimacy rests as much on when it decides as on what it decides.

**Value-additions**
1. **Law Commission, 120th Report (1987):** recommended raising judge strength to 50 per million population; India remains well below that.
2. ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** and **Art. 39A:** speedy justice as a fundamental right and a directive principle.
3. **e-Courts Phase III (₹7,210 crore, approved 2023)** and **Mediation Act, 2023:** the two main non-judge levers.
