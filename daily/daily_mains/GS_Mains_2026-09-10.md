# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-10 (GS3 — Science & Technology / Environment: Energy)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Thursday = GS3 (Sci-Tech / Environment). One question to write today; the model answer is below the divider — attempt it on paper first.*

> **This week's hook:** India has just rewritten the legal foundation of its atomic programme. The **Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act, 2025** — introduced in the Lok Sabha on **15 December 2025**, passed by the **Lok Sabha on 17 December** and the **Rajya Sabha on 18 December**, with **Presidential assent on 22 December 2025** — **repeals and replaces both the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 and the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010**. For the first time since 1962, **private Indian companies and joint ventures can be licensed to build, own and operate nuclear power plants** and to fabricate, transport, trade and store nuclear fuel, subject to safety authorisation from the **Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB)**, which the Act finally puts on a **statutory footing**. Crucially, the Act **removes the operator's right of recourse against suppliers of defective equipment** — the clause that had frozen foreign vendor participation since 2010 — and replaces the flat **₹1,500 crore** operator liability with a **tiered cap ranging from ₹100 crore to ₹3,000 crore** by plant capacity, within an overall ceiling of about **300 million SDRs**. The commercial machinery is already moving: NPCIL's **Bharat Small Reactor (BSR)** request-for-proposals to private industry ran to **31 March 2026** (Tata Power, Adani, Reliance, JSW and Jindal among the interested), the **Nuclear Energy Mission** announced in **Budget 2025–26** carries **₹20,000 crore** for **five indigenous SMRs by 2033**, and the government's roadmap targets **100 GW by 2047** against just **8.78 GW across 24 reactors as of July 2026** (~**3.1%** of electricity generation in 2025–26). But the law is now before the Supreme Court: in **E.A.S. Sarma v. Union of India, W.P.(C) No. 240/2026**, a Bench of **CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana** has issued notice to the Union and AERB on whether statutory liability caps can bind constitutional courts, with the petitioners invoking **absolute liability** and the **polluter pays** principle. That collision — *between an energy transition that needs clean firm power and a safety-and-liability regime built after Bhopal* — is today's GS3 theme, deliberately distinct from the recent GS3 questions on **labour codes (8 September)** and **trade/FTAs (11 August)**, from Wednesday's **GS1 monsoon** question (9 September), and from the last Sci-Tech question on **AI regulation (25 June)**.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> The SHANTI Act, 2025 opens India's nuclear power sector to private participation and substantially restructures the civil nuclear liability regime. **Examine** the rationale for this shift in the context of India's energy transition, and the safety, regulatory and liability concerns it raises.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (context + anchor)

Nuclear power is India's principal source of **clean *firm* electricity** — low-carbon generation available irrespective of sun, wind or season, and therefore complementary to, not competing with, renewables. Yet it supplies only about **3.1% of generation**, with **8.78 GW across 24 reactors (July 2026)**, against a target of **100 GW by 2047**. The **SHANTI Act, 2025** (assented **22 December 2025**), which repeals the **Atomic Energy Act, 1962** and the **CLND Act, 2010**, is the legal instrument meant to close that gap.

### A. The rationale: why the law was changed

- **Capital and capacity constraints.** A 100 GW target implies more than an elevenfold expansion. NPCIL alone cannot finance or build it; the Act therefore licenses **Indian companies, joint ventures and other permitted persons** to build, own and operate plants and handle fuel — ending the state monopoly of 1962.
- **Unblocking technology inflows.** The **CLNDA's right of recourse against suppliers** (Section 17(b)) was an outlier from global practice and effectively deterred foreign and domestic vendors for fifteen years. SHANTI **removes recourse on the ground of defective equipment**, channelling liability exclusively to the operator and aligning India with the **Convention on Supplementary Compensation (CSC)**, which India ratified in 2016.
- **Energy-transition arithmetic.** Firm low-carbon capacity is essential to integrate a renewables-heavy grid and to meet **net-zero by 2070**; rising AI/data-centre and industrial demand sharpens the need for round-the-clock supply.
- **Technology shift.** Smaller, factory-built units — **Bharat Small Reactors (220 MWe PHWRs)** and **SMRs** under the **₹20,000 crore Nuclear Energy Mission** — suit brownfield and captive industrial sites, which requires precisely the private participation the old law forbade.

### B. The concerns it raises

- **Victim compensation and constitutional standards.** Operator liability is now **tiered from ₹100 crore to ₹3,000 crore** by capacity — modest against Fukushima-scale costs. Indian jurisprudence has moved the opposite way, from **absolute liability** in *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987, Oleum Gas) to **polluter pays** in *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996). Whether a statutory cap can limit compensation under **Article 21** is precisely the question the Supreme Court has taken up in **E.A.S. Sarma v. Union of India (W.P.(C) 240/2026)**.
- **Moral hazard in the supply chain.** Removing supplier recourse concentrates risk on the operator and weakens the commercial incentive for vendors to guarantee component quality — a live concern where private operators face competitive cost pressure.
- **Regulatory independence.** The Act gives the **AERB statutory status** — a long-standing demand of the **CAG Performance Audit (Report No. 9 of 2012)** and the lapsed **Nuclear Safety Regulatory Authority Bill, 2011**. But its members are appointed by the Central Government through a committee constituted by the **Atomic Energy Commission**, i.e. by the ecosystem it regulates; terms are short (3 years, extendable by 3). Structural independence remains incomplete.
- **Appellate architecture.** Appeals lie to an **Atomic Energy Redressal Advisory Council** chaired by the AEC Chairperson and including the AERB Chairperson — an overlap of promoter and adjudicator — before reaching the **Appellate Tribunal for Electricity**.
- **Unresolved back-end issues.** Spent-fuel management, decommissioning liabilities, uranium import dependence, water requirements for siting, and local consent (Kudankulam, Jaitapur) are untouched by a liability reform.

### Conclusion (balanced, forward-looking)

SHANTI corrects a genuine policy failure: for fifteen years India professed nuclear ambition while retaining a legal regime that made it unachievable. But investor confidence and public confidence must be built together — a regime that is attractive to capital and unconvincing to citizens will stall at the first local protest. The reform will succeed if it is completed by three things: an AERB made independent in appointment and funding, not merely statutory; a **supplementary compensation pool** (insurance plus the CSC's international tier) that credibly covers a worst-case event rather than a nominal cap; and transparent siting and waste-management protocols. Handled thus, nuclear energy becomes the firm backbone of a renewables-led grid; handled carelessly, India risks trading a legal bottleneck for a legitimacy one.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS (weave 2–3 in, don't list them)

1. **Law/case law:** *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987) — **absolute liability**, no exceptions, for hazardous enterprises; *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) — **polluter pays** as part of Indian environmental law. Both are the constitutional benchmark against which a statutory liability cap is being tested in **W.P.(C) 240/2026**.
2. **Committee/audit:** **CAG Performance Audit Report No. 9 of 2012** on the *Activities of the Atomic Energy Regulatory Board* flagged that AERB lacked statutory authority and was subordinate to the very Department it regulated; the **Nuclear Safety Regulatory Authority Bill, 2011** lapsed. SHANTI's statutory AERB is a partial answer to a fourteen-year-old finding.
3. **Scheme/data:** **Nuclear Energy Mission** (Budget 2025–26) — **₹20,000 crore**, **five indigenous SMRs by 2033**; **Bharat Small Reactors** (220 MWe PHWRs) offered to private industry via NPCIL RFP (open to **31 March 2026**); capacity path **8.78 GW (2026) → ~22 GW (2031–32) → 100 GW (2047)**.
4. **Concept to deploy:** *clean **firm** power* vs *clean **variable** power* — the framing that separates a good answer from a generic one. Also usable: India's **three-stage nuclear programme** (PHWR → FBR → thorium) and the **Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor at Kalpakkam** as the long-run indigenous logic behind fuel-cycle activities the Act reserves to the Centre.
5. **Constitutional/international anchors:** **Article 21** (right to life, read to include compensation for hazardous activity); **Article 48A** and **Article 51A(g)**; **Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage (CSC)** — signed 2010, ratified 2016 — whose channelling principle SHANTI now mirrors.
