> **This week's hook:** Two anti-defection cases reached the Supreme Court in the same week, and both turned on the same thing: the Speaker's delay, not the law.
>
> - **Telangana.** On **24 September 2026**, a Bench of **CJI Surya Kant, Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana** refused to interfere with the **Telangana High Court's 18 September 2026** judgment. That judgment had set aside Speaker **Gaddam Prasad Kumar's** rejection (**11 March 2026**) of disqualification petitions against **Danam Nagender**. Nagender was elected on a BRS ticket from Khairatabad in 2023 and then contested the **2024 Lok Sabha election from Secunderabad as a Congress candidate**. The HC held that this alone attracted **Paragraph 2(1)(a)** of the Tenth Schedule and **disqualified him from 23 April 2024**, the date he filed his nomination. The case began with the Supreme Court's **31 July 2025** ruling in ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana***, which ordered the Speaker to decide within three months.
> - **West Bengal.** On **28 September 2026**, the same Bench disposed of a plea by TMC MLA **Sovandeb Chattopadhyay**. The petitions were filed on **8 July 2026**, so the window runs to about **8 October**.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> The Tenth Schedule was meant to end the "*Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram*" culture. Yet its effectiveness now depends less on the law itself than on *when*, and *whether*, the Presiding Officer chooses to decide. In the light of recent judicial interventions, **critically examine** the working of the anti-defection law and evaluate the case for moving the power of adjudication away from the Speaker.

*Directive decoded:* "Critically examine" asks for **both** sides of the working: what the law has achieved and where it has failed. "Evaluate the case" asks for a **judgement** on the alternatives. Don't just list them.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (anchor + context)

The **52nd Amendment (1985)** added the Tenth Schedule. It disqualifies a legislator who **voluntarily gives up party membership [Para 2(1)(a)]** or **defies the party whip without condonation [Para 2(1)(b)]**. The **Speaker/Chairman decides** these cases (Para 6). In September 2026 the Supreme Court had to intervene again, in Telangana and West Bengal, to get Speakers to decide pending petitions. That shows the law's weak point is its adjudicator, not its text.

### A. What the law has achieved

- **Deterrence and stability:** individual floor-crossing for office has become costly. The **91st Amendment (2003)** removed the "one-third split" exception and barred disqualified members from ministerial office until re-elected (**Arts. 75(1B), 164(1B)**).
- **Judicial reinforcement:** *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) upheld the Schedule but held that the Speaker acts as a **tribunal subject to judicial review**. *Ravi S. Naik* (1994) held that "voluntarily giving up" can be **inferred from conduct**. The Nagender ruling applies exactly this principle.

### B. Where it has failed

- **Delay as a weapon:** Speakers sit on petitions until the term effectively ends. The Court called this "**operation successful, patient died**" (*Padi Kaushik Reddy*, 2025).
- **Partisan adjudicator:** a Speaker's continuing party affiliation makes neutrality structurally doubtful.
- **Loopholes:** the **Para 4 merger** exception (two-thirds of the legislature party) allows *group* defection. Resignation followed by re-contest avoids disqualification altogether.
- **Collateral cost:** because whips cover *every* vote, the law suppresses legitimate dissent and weakens deliberation.

### C. Evaluating the alternatives

| Option | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Enforceable time limit** (*Keisham Meghachandra*, 2020: ~3 months) | Least disruptive | Still needs a court to enforce it, case by case |
| **President/Governor on ECI's advice** (*Dinesh Goswami Committee*, 1990) | Mirrors Arts. 103/192 | Governors carry their own neutrality problem |
| **Independent tribunal** (suggested in *Keisham Meghachandra*; echoed in 2025) | Removes the conflict of interest | Needs a constitutional amendment; raises the question of legislative privilege |

### Conclusion

On balance, a **statutory deadline plus an independent tribunal**, with the Speaker's role kept for procedure, is the best available option. It should come with **whips limited to confidence and money votes** (as the *Law Commission's 170th Report* and *NCRWC* argued). Anti-defection law should protect the voter's mandate without silencing the legislator's conscience.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS (weave in 2–3, don't list them)

1. **Case-law chain in one line:** *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992: Speaker = tribunal, reviewable) → *Ravi S. Naik* (1994: conduct can prove defection) → *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020: decide in ~3 months; Parliament may consider an independent tribunal) → *Subhash Desai* (2023, Maharashtra: the **political party**, not the legislature party, appoints the whip; the Court would not decide disqualification itself) → *Padi Kaushik Reddy* (2025: Speaker has no "constitutional immunity" for inaction).
2. **Manipur 2020 (the extreme case):** after the Speaker sat on petitions against minister **Th. Shyamkumar** for about three years, the Supreme Court used **Article 142** to restrain him from entering the Assembly and effectively removed him from the ministry. This is useful proof that delay pushes courts into the Speaker's domain, and it links to your Art. 142 answer of 7 September.
3. **Reform committees:** **Dinesh Goswami (1990)** recommended that the President/Governor decide on ECI advice and that the law be limited to voluntary resignation and votes on confidence/money. **Law Commission 170th Report (1999)** recommended deleting the split/merger exceptions and limiting whips. **NCRWC (2002)** recommended that a defector's vote be treated as invalid and that he be barred from office until re-elected.
4. **Ethics angle (GS4 cross-over):** the Speaker's office rests on *constitutional morality*. The Westminster convention that the Speaker cuts ties with party (UK: the Speaker traditionally stands unopposed by major parties at elections) is a useful contrast.

## COMMON TRAPS ON THIS QUESTION

- **Writing the history of "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" (Haryana, 1967).** One clause is enough. The question is about *adjudication*.
- **Declaring the Speaker "biased" as a flat fact.** Frame it as **structural conflict of interest**. The examiner rewards institutional framing over political commentary.
- **Missing the merger loophole.** Para 4 explains why *group* defections still succeed even when individual ones fail. Leaving it out makes Section B incomplete.
- **Listing reforms without choosing.** "Evaluate the case" requires a verdict. Say which option you prefer and why the others fall short.
- **Forgetting the dissent cost.** The strongest critiques (NCRWC, Law Commission) are about *over*-reach: whips on every vote. A balanced answer names both under-enforcement and over-reach.
