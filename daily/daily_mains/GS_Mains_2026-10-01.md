## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction (≈40 words)**
The Great Nicobar Island project, implemented by ANIIDCO, is a roughly ₹81,000 crore plan (some reports now cite about ₹91,000 crore) for a container transshipment port at Galathea Bay, a dual-use airport, a township and a power plant. It needs about 166 sq km of land, including about 130 sq km of forest, on an island that is a Biosphere Reserve.

**Case for the project (strategic and economic)**
- **Geography:** Great Nicobar lies near the Malacca Strait, a chokepoint for a large share of world trade. A transshipment hub could cut India's reliance on Colombo, Singapore and Klang for transshipped cargo.
- **Security:** a dual-use airport and naval presence strengthen India's Indian Ocean posture. The government also cites surveillance against smuggling and illegal migration.
- **Economic:** jobs, tourism and port-led growth, in line with Sagarmala and the Maritime Amrit Kaal vision. The NGT accepted the project's "strategic importance" and found safeguards adequate.

**Case against (ecological and rights-based)**
- **Ecology:** it falls in the Sundaland hotspot and involves tropical evergreen and littoral forest, and the felling of a very large number of trees. Galathea Bay is a leatherback turtle nesting site, and its wildlife sanctuary was denotified. The endemic Nicobar megapode is also at risk. Mitigation such as translocation and compensatory afforestation is hard to verify for old-growth forest.
- **Tribal rights:** the Shompen are a PVTG of a few hundred people with little immunity to outside disease. The Nicobarese were displaced by the 2004 tsunami, and the Tribal Council withdrew its consent in 2022. The petitions allege that Forest Rights Act processes were flawed: the gram sabhas were settler panchayats, there was no Forest Rights Committee, and Shompen consent was obtained through an agency.
- **Hazard:** the island lies in a seismically active zone; the 2004 tsunami submerged Indira Point.
- **Process:** the 22 Sep 2026 EIA amendment, which doubles port clearance validity, lets a coastal baseline age by up to two decades. Critics read it as project-specific tailoring.

**Critical assessment**
Strategic need is real, but strategic *location* is not the same as strategic *design*. Orissa Mining Corporation v. MoEF (2013, the Niyamgiri case) made gram sabha consent central where tribal religious and cultural rights are affected, and the FRA (Sec 3(1)(e), habitat rights of PVTGs; Sec 4(5)) cannot be treated as a formality. Sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter-pays principle (Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum, 1996) all require the ecological burden of proof to rest on the proponent.

**Way forward**
- Complete FRA compliance before any irreversible work, with the Tribal Council's free, prior and informed consent and Shompen consultation through anthropologists, not agencies.
- Independent, published baseline and monitoring of turtles, megapodes and coral reefs, with a mid-term baseline revalidation despite the longer EIA validity.
- Phase the project and keep the Shompen reserve untouched, keeping the Shompen Policy 2015 (minimal intervention) as the ceiling on development.
- Decouple regulatory certainty from scrutiny, and set up a seismic and disaster-resilience audit.

**Conclusion**
India need not choose between a Blue Economy and a living island. A project that earns tribal consent, ecological credibility and legal finality will serve strategy better than one that wins clearance but loses legitimacy.

**Value-additions (use 2–3)**
1. **Case law:** *Orissa Mining Corp. v. MoEF (2013)* — gram sabha consent; *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum (1996)* — precautionary principle.
2. **Law/policy:** FRA 2006 (Sec 3(1)(e), 4(5)); **Shompen Policy 2015**; **EIA 2006 as amended 22 Sep 2026**; **CRZ 2019**.
3. **Designation:** Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve (UNESCO MAB network); Sundaland hotspot; PVTG status of the Shompen.
