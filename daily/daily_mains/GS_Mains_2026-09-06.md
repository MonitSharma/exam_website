# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-06 (Sunday — Reflective / Essay-style GS question)

> **This week's hook:** India is quietly finishing the **Houselisting phase of Census 2027 (April–September 2026)** — the exercise that will, for the first time in sixteen years, officially tell us how urban India is. The official answer is already contested. The **Economic Survey 2025–26**, using the satellite-based **Global Human Settlements Layer**, suggests India was about **63% urban as far back as 2015** — roughly *double* the **31% recorded by Census 2011**. Meanwhile the **UN's World Urbanization Prospects 2025** finds India will add **more urban residents than any other country between 2025 and 2050** (well over 200 million). The policy machinery has begun to respond: the **Sixteenth Finance Commission (Arvind Panagariya)**, whose report for **2026–31 was tabled in Parliament on 1 February 2026**, raised the urban share of local-body grants to **45%** (**₹3.56 lakh crore** of a total **₹7.91 lakh crore**, ~116% above the 15th FC), and **NITI Aayog released "Moving Towards Effective City Government — A Framework for Million Plus Cities" on 25 April 2026**. Yet municipal revenues remain stuck near **1% of GDP**. That gap — between a country that is already urban and a state that still governs it as if it were not — is today's reflective theme, deliberately distinct from the recent Sunday essay on the *demographic dividend* (28 June) and from the GS1 *caste-Census* question (1 July).

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> *"India did not plan to become urban; it simply became urban while looking elsewhere."*
>
> India's cities generate the bulk of its national income and will absorb the largest addition of urban residents of any country in the coming quarter-century, yet they remain among the world's most under-empowered and under-financed units of government. **Critically examine** why India's urban transition has outpaced its capacity to govern cities, and reflect on what a genuinely "urban-ready" India would require.

## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction (context + data).**
Urbanisation is not merely the growth of cities but the structural shift of population, production and aspiration from farm to non-farm settlement. India's cities already generate about **63% of GDP** — projected by the World Bank to reach **75% by 2036** — and the **UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025** finds India will add more urban residents than any other nation between 2025 and 2050. Yet **Census 2011** recorded India as only **31% urban**, while the **Economic Survey 2025–26**, using satellite settlement data, suggests the true figure was nearer **63% by 2015**. The transition has happened; the governance of it has not caught up.

**Why the transition has outpaced governance:**

- **A definitional blind spot.** India's "urban" is defined by a 1961-vintage census test (population, density, non-agricultural male workforce). Thousands of dense settlements remain classified as *census towns* governed by panchayats — urban in fact, rural in law, and therefore urban in neither budget nor plan. What is not measured is not governed.
- **Fiscal anaemia.** Municipal revenues and expenditure have stagnated at roughly **1% of GDP for over a decade**, against **7.4% in Brazil** and **6% in South Africa** (RBI, *Report on Municipal Finances*); own-source revenue of municipal corporations is under **1% of GDP**, leaving cities dependent on discretionary state and central grants. Property tax remains under-assessed and politically untouchable.
- **Devolution on paper.** The **74th Constitutional Amendment (1992)** and its **Twelfth Schedule** listed eighteen municipal functions, and **Articles 243ZD and 243ZE** created District and Metropolitan Planning Committees. In practice, parastatals and state departments retain water, transport and planning; most Metropolitan Planning Committees are non-functional. Mayors are largely ceremonial, with short and indirectly-elected tenures — accountability without authority.
- **A capacity deficit.** A TCPO–NIUA study cited by **NITI Aayog's *Reforms in Urban Planning Capacity in India* (2021)** found states need over **12,000 town planners** against roughly **4,000 sanctioned posts, half of them vacant**. Cities are planned by those with no training in planning.
- **Climate exposure and informality.** Urban form now determines vulnerability: **nine cities — Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Faridabad, Gwalior, Kota, Ludhiana, Meerut and Surat — hold over 11% of the urban population and are the most heat-exposed**, while encroached drainage turns cloudbursts into disasters (Bengaluru recorded ~130 mm in twelve hours in 2025). Simultaneously, most urban workers are informal, living in settlements the master plan does not acknowledge.

**The counter-view — the ledger is not one-sided.**
Indian cities have delivered: they absorbed distress migration without the scale of urban collapse seen elsewhere, and remain engines of social mobility and anonymity-as-freedom. Missions such as AMRUT, Smart Cities and Swachh Bharat–Urban improved measurable service outcomes, and metro rail now runs in over twenty cities. The **Sixteenth Finance Commission (report for 2026–31, tabled 1 February 2026)** marks a genuine correction — raising the urban share of local-body grants to **45%**, or **₹3.56 lakh crore**, and, significantly, tying **10% of inter-se allocation to an own-source revenue index**, rewarding cities that raise their own money. **NITI Aayog's April 2026 framework for million-plus cities** pushes empowered mayors and professional municipal cadres. The direction is right; the question is depth and speed.

**Conclusion (forward-looking).**
A country becomes urban-ready not when it builds more cities but when it *trusts* them — with money, mandate and competent staff. That requires an honest redefinition of "urban" (which **Census 2027** offers a rare chance to fix), buoyant own-source revenue anchored in property tax and land-value capture, functional Metropolitan Planning Committees with directly elected and empowered mayors, and climate-resilient master plans drawn by trained planners. Ambedkar's warning against romanticising the village and Gandhi's insistence on local self-rule are reconcilable in exactly one place: the **empowered city government**. India's demographic and economic future will be decided in its cities — the choice is whether that future is planned or merely endured.

### Value-additions to weave in (pick 2–3, don't dump all)

1. **Data anchor:** Economic Survey 2025–26 (GHSL satellite data) — India ~**63% urban in 2015** vs Census 2011's **31%**; cities = **63% of GDP**, rising to **75% by 2036** (World Bank). UN WUP 2025 — India the largest single contributor to global urban growth, 2025–50.
2. **Fiscal comparison (RBI, *Report on Municipal Finances*):** municipal revenue/expenditure ~**1% of GDP** in India vs **7.4% (Brazil)** and **6% (South Africa)**; intergovernmental transfers to municipalities ~**0.45% of GDP** vs 1.6–5.4% in comparable federations.
3. **Committee/Commission:** **Sixteenth Finance Commission (Panagariya)**, 2026–31 — ₹7.91 lakh crore local-body grants, **45% urban share (₹3.56 lakh crore)**, with **90% weight to 2026 urban population and 10% to an own-source revenue index**. Also **HPEC on Urban Infrastructure and Services (Isher Judge Ahluwalia, 2011)**, which estimated ~**₹39.2 lakh crore** of urban investment need over 2012–31.
4. **Constitutional hook:** **74th Amendment Act, 1992** — Twelfth Schedule (18 functions), **Art. 243ZD** (District Planning Committee), **Art. 243ZE** (Metropolitan Planning Committee), **Art. 243Y** (State Finance Commission).
5. **Thinkers:** **Jane Jacobs** on organic, mixed-use streets and "eyes on the street" as the real source of urban safety; the **Ambedkar–Gandhi tension** — Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly remark (4 November 1948) dismissing the village as "a sink of localism" against Gandhi's *Gram Swaraj* — resolved by making *local* self-government urban as well as rural.

### Answer-writing notes for this question

- It is a **reflective** prompt: the last two lines must do real work. Don't end on a scheme list; end on a judgement about what the state owes its cities.
- The strongest structural move is the **"urban in fact, rural in law"** framing — it converts a data point (63% vs 31%) into an argument and carries the whole answer.
- Keep the counter-view genuinely fair: 16th FC and the NITI 2026 framework show the state *is* moving. A "critically examine" answer that only indicts loses marks for balance.
- If short on time, drop the informality bullet before dropping the fiscal one — money is the load-bearing argument here.
- A small map-sketch or flow (Migration → unrecognised settlement → no plan/no revenue → service and climate failure) can be drawn in 30 seconds and earns structure marks.
