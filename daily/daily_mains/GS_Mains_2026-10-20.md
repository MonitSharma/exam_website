## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> India's foreign exchange reserves have fallen by about $60 billion from their September peak as the RBI sells dollars to defend a depreciating rupee. **Critically examine** the rationale for, and the limits of, using reserves to manage the exchange rate, and **suggest** a policy mix to strengthen India's external resilience. **(15 marks, 250 words)**

## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction.** Reserves are the economy's external shock-absorber. India's reserves peaked at ~$785.7 bn (4 Sep 2026) and slid to ~$726 bn by 9 Oct, with the rupee testing ~₹97/$ amid costly crude, firm US yields and FPI outflows. The RBI says it targets *volatility, not a level* — but the scale of dollar sales raises the question of how long such defence is sustainable.

**Why the RBI uses reserves**

- **Disorderly volatility hurts real activity:** a sharp fall raises the import bill (oil, electronics, fertiliser, edible oil) and feeds imported inflation — directly relevant when the MPC has just turned hawkish.
- **Balance-sheet protection:** corporates and banks carry unhedged foreign-currency debt; a spiral of depreciation → hedging demand → more depreciation can damage balance sheets.
- **Expectations and confidence:** reserves of this size (still roughly a year of imports, on rough reckoning) signal capacity to meet obligations and discourage one-way bets.
- **Structural reason:** a managed float suits an economy with a persistent trade deficit and volatile portfolio flows.

**Limits and costs**

- **Finite ammunition:** much of the "usable" stock is not fully liquid — a large forward book (market estimates ~$200 bn, unofficial) and swap-linked liabilities mean *net* reserves are lower than headline.
- **Valuation effects:** part of the fall reflects revaluation of gold and non-dollar assets, so the headline overstates actual sales — but also means reserves are not entirely RBI-controlled.
- **Monetary-policy trilemma (impossible trinity):** with an open capital account and a managed exchange rate, independent monetary policy is constrained; sterilisation has a fiscal and liquidity cost.
- **Moral hazard and signalling:** unhedged borrowers expect a backstop; defending a level invites speculative attacks if credibility slips.
- **Fundamentals not fixed:** intervention buys time but does not close the oil/gold/electronics import gap or raise stable inflows.

**Assessment.** Reserve use is justified for smoothing a *temporary, external-shock-driven* depreciation; it becomes unsustainable if used to hold a rate the fundamentals do not support. Allowing gradual depreciation while curbing excessive volatility is the better rule.

**Way forward**

1. **Current-account side:** cut oil dependence (ethanol blending, EVs, strategic reserves, diversified sourcing), push electronics/solar/chemicals import substitution and services exports.
2. **Capital-account side:** attract *stable* inflows (FDI, longer-tenor NRI deposits, bond-index inclusion), hedging depth and reduced reliance on volatile FPI "hot money".
3. **Macro-prudential:** supervise unhedged corporate exposure, calibrate FX-derivative rules without choking genuine hedging.
4. **Transparency:** publish intervention and forward-book data on a timely basis to reduce speculation (many EMEs, e.g. through forward book disclosure, already do some of this).
5. **Coordination:** fiscal restraint, credible inflation targeting, and an orderly exit from special swap windows.

**Conclusion.** Reserves are a bridge, not a destination: the durable defence of the rupee is a narrower current-account deficit, a deeper domestic savings-investment base and credible institutions — with reserves used to smooth, not to fight, the tide.

### Value-additions (for the exam)

1. **Concepts:** **Impossible trinity (Mundell–Fleming)**; **Greenspan–Guidotti rule** (reserves ≥ short-term external debt) and import-cover metrics as adequacy benchmarks; "**fear of floating**" in emerging markets.
2. **Precedents:** **1991 BoP crisis** (reserves covered ~2 weeks of imports; gold pledge, devaluation, LPG reforms); **2013 taper tantrum** — RBI's **FCNR(B) swap window** raised ~$34 bn (the 2026 swap window echoes this); 2022 sell-off after the Ukraine war.
3. **Institutional/legal:** **FEMA, 1999** (current-account convertibility, regulated capital account); **Tarapore Committees (1997, 2006)** on capital-account convertibility; RBI's role under **RBI Act, 1934** in managing reserves.
