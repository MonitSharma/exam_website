> **This week's hook:** India hosts the **18th BRICS Summit at Bharat Mandapam, New Delhi, on 12–13 September 2026** — tomorrow. India assumed the **BRICS chairship on 1 January 2026** (its fourth, after 2012, 2016 and 2021) under the theme **"Building for Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability" (BRICS)**, drawn from the PM's "Humanity First, People-centric" framing. The summit is the first of an enlarged grouping: from the original five, BRICS now has **ten full members** — Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the UAE and Indonesia (2025) — with **Saudi Arabia invited in 2023 but still not formalising membership**, plus about **ten partner countries**; together the members hold roughly **half the world's population and over 40% of global GDP (PPP)**, against the G7's ~28%. The optics are extraordinary: **Xi Jinping arrives in India for the first time in nearly seven years**, with a **Modi–Xi bilateral on 12 September** — their first on Indian soil since the **2020 Galwan clash** — following the **25th round of Special Representatives' talks (Doval–Wang Yi, Beijing, 25 August 2026)** and the resumption of the Kailash Mansarovar Yatra, direct flights and border trade; **Putin attends** as well. But the fault lines are equally visible. **More than 67% of intra-BRICS trade was settled in national currencies in 2025**, the **NDB (Dilma Rousseff, re-elected 2025)** targets **30% local-currency lending in 2026**, and BRICS Pay is being wired into **UPI, China's CIPS and Russia's SPFS** — even as **President Trump has threatened 100% tariffs** on states pursuing de-dollarisation and **EAM Jaishankar has repeatedly said India "has never been for de-dollarisation"** and has "no interest in weakening the dollar," with no BRICS currency proposal on the table. India will also seek to convert this platform into support for its **UNSC non-permanent seat bid for 2028–29**, with the PM provisionally addressing the **UNGA General Debate on 25 September 2026**. That tension — *between a grouping India needs for Global-South legitimacy and one it must stop from becoming an anti-Western bloc* — is today's GS2-IR theme, deliberately distinct from this week's GS2 question on **Article 142 (7 September)** and from the GS3 questions on **labour codes (8 Sept)**, **the monsoon (9 Sept)** and **the SHANTI Act (10 Sept)**.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> As chair of the 18th BRICS Summit, India presides over a grouping that has doubled in size and now speaks for nearly half of humanity, yet is increasingly pulled between its developmental agenda and a geopolitical one. **Critically examine** the significance of BRICS for India's foreign policy objectives, and the constraints that its expansion and internal divergences impose.

**(Directive: *critically examine* — establish first what BRICS genuinely delivers for India (Global-South leadership, reformed multilateralism, financing, strategic autonomy, a managed China channel); then weigh honestly the constraints (China's dominance, the de-dollarisation trap and US tariff exposure, dilution through expansion, Iran–Russia's anti-Western tilt, intra-BRICS bilateral disputes, weak institutional depth); close with a reasoned judgement on how India should steer it. Do not write a "history and achievements of BRICS" answer — the marks lie in the *dilemmas*, not the description. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction

BRICS — conceived as an investment acronym by Jim O'Neill (2001), institutionalised at Yekaterinburg (2009) — has become the principal non-Western plurilateral of the emerging world. Its 2024–25 enlargement to ten members plus partner states gives it roughly half the world's population and over 40% of global GDP (PPP), against the G7's ~28%. India, chairing the **18th Summit at New Delhi (12–13 September 2026)** under the theme *"Building for Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability,"* must convert this weight into leverage without letting the platform harden into a bloc — the classic test of **strategic autonomy** under Article 51 of the Constitution.

### Body — Why BRICS matters to India

**1. Reformed multilateralism.** BRICS is India's most consistent institutional voice for restructuring a post-1945 order in which it remains excluded from the UNSC. The platform feeds directly into India's **UNSC non-permanent seat bid for 2028–29**.

**2. Global-South leadership.** Following the African Union's G20 induction at New Delhi (2023) and the Voice of Global South Summits, BRICS lets India speak *for* developing countries on debt, climate finance and technology access, contesting China's claim to the same mantle.

**3. Development finance.** The **New Development Bank** (Fortaleza, 2014; $100 bn authorised capital) and the **Contingent Reserve Arrangement** ($100 bn) offer conditionality-light finance; the NDB targets **30% local-currency lending in 2026**, cushioning exchange-rate risk.

**4. A managed channel to China.** The **Modi–Xi bilateral (12 September 2026)** — Xi's first India visit in nearly seven years — follows the **25th Special Representatives' round (August 2026)** and border-trade resumption. BRICS gives India a *multilateral* setting to stabilise its hardest bilateral.

**5. Economic pragmatism.** Energy security (discounted Russian crude), supply-chain resilience, MSME and digital-trade cooperation are tangible, low-cost gains.

### Body — The constraints

**1. Asymmetry.** China's GDP exceeds all other members combined; enlargement risks turning BRICS into an instrument of Chinese influence rather than a balancing forum.

**2. The de-dollarisation trap.** Over **67% of intra-BRICS trade** was settled in national currencies in 2025 and BRICS Pay is linking UPI, CIPS and SPFS — but the US is India's largest trading partner, and **Trump's 100% tariff threat** makes financial signalling costly. Hence Jaishankar's position: India "has never been for de-dollarisation"; rupee internationalisation is not dollar displacement.

**3. Dilution through expansion.** Ten members with divergent regimes and interests weaken consensus; **Saudi Arabia's continued non-formalisation** shows the coalition's own members hedge.

**4. Internal contradictions.** Iran and Russia seek an explicitly anti-Western platform; Egypt–Ethiopia (GERD) and India–China disputes sit *inside* the grouping. India's Quad, I2U2 and IMEC commitments must not be read as duplicity.

**5. Thin institutions.** Beyond the NDB, BRICS lacks a secretariat, dispute mechanism or binding instruments — a summit-driven grouping with limited delivery capacity.

### Conclusion

BRICS is valuable to India precisely as a **hedge, not a home**. Jaishankar's formulation — that BRICS is *non-Western, not anti-Western* — should be the operating principle of India's chairship: push practical deliverables (payments interoperability, supply chains, climate finance, AI and health cooperation) while resisting currency-bloc symbolism and single-power capture. A BRICS that widens the space for multipolarity strengthens India; one that becomes a counter-alliance would narrow India's options in a world where its interests run in every direction at once.

| **Quotable framing** | Jaishankar: BRICS is *"non-Western, not anti-Western"*; and India "has never been for de-dollarisation" |
| **Constitutional anchor** | **Article 51** (DPSP) — promotion of international peace and security, respect for international law |
| **Concept** | Multi-alignment / strategic autonomy; "issue-based coalitions" — India simultaneously in BRICS, Quad, SCO, I2U2 and IMEC |

### Traps to avoid

- Writing a chronology of BRICS summits. The examiner wants *India's dilemmas*, not the grouping's biography.
- Endorsing de-dollarisation as India's position — it is not. Distinguish **rupee internationalisation** (India's stated goal) from **dollar displacement** (India's stated non-goal).
- Treating BRICS and Quad as contradictory. Frame them as **complementary instruments of multi-alignment** — that framing itself earns marks.
- Over-claiming the China thaw. Note the resumption of dialogue, flights, the Yatra and border trade *and* the unresolved boundary, trade deficit and trust deficit.
