## MODEL ANSWER

### Introduction
The Ganga Water Treaty (12 Dec 1996, 30 years) fixes dry-season (Jan–May) sharing of Ganga flows at the Farakka Barrage between India and Bangladesh. It has been among the few durable water agreements in South Asia, but it expires on **12 Dec 2026**. Bangladesh wants a longer-term deal with a guarantee clause; India wants to reflect its own growing demand. Renewal is therefore a test of both water diplomacy and "Neighbourhood First".

### Challenges in renegotiation

**1. Hydrological**
- The formula is flow-based (70,000/75,000 cusecs thresholds), so lean-season shortfalls bite hardest in the driest weeks; Bangladesh calls it biased and wants a **guaranteed minimum**, as in the **1977** agreement.
- Climate change, glacier-melt variability, upstream abstraction and siltation reduce the very flows the formula assumes; fixed shares become harder to honour.

**2. Domestic and federal**
- Water is a **State subject (Entry 17, List II)** while inter-State rivers and treaties are Union subjects (Entry 56 List I; Entry 14 List I; Art. 253). West Bengal's consent, as in the **Teesta (2011)** impasse, is politically decisive; Bihar and West Bengal both press for more water.
- Kolkata port's need for flushing flows and the Farakka feeder canal's purpose compete with Bangladesh's demands.

**3. Political and strategic**
- Post-2024 shifts in Dhaka and bilateral irritants (trade curbs at land ports, border killings, migration, minority concerns) reduce trust; Dhaka's outreach to China (Teesta project talk) and Pakistan adds a geopolitical overlay.
- India's **Indus Waters Treaty abeyance (2025)** may make Dhaka fear that India treats treaties as bargaining chips; it also complicates India's moral claim as a responsible upper riparian.

**4. Institutional and legal**
- The Joint Rivers Commission (est. 1972) has limited data-sharing and no strong dispute-resolution mechanism. India is not party to the **1997 UN Watercourses Convention**, so the framework is purely bilateral.
- The treaty covers only the Ganga; Teesta and 52 other shared rivers remain unaddressed, so "package" bargaining is difficult.

### Way forward
- **Start now and decouple:** keep the water track insulated from trade and political irritants; aim to conclude before December or provide an automatic interim extension so no legal vacuum arises.
- **Adaptive, science-based sharing:** joint real-time flow monitoring and data exchange, periodic review, a minimum-flow safeguard for the lean period, climate-resilience clauses.
- **Federal buy-in:** involve West Bengal and Bihar early through the Inter-State Council / consultation, with Centre compensating through augmentation projects.
- **Basin approach:** augmentation (storage, Ganga–Padma barrage idea), Teesta agreement, and shared flood-forecasting; include Nepal's upstream storage potential (Mahakali model) in a regional frame.
- **Institution-building:** empower the JRC with a dispute mechanism and joint technical secretariat.

### Conclusion
A flexible, longer and more climate-resilient treaty, negotiated without linkage to unrelated disputes, would show that India's upper-riparian status comes with responsibility. In a neighbourhood being courted by China, a generous but sustainable water bargain is among India's cheapest strategic investments. As the **Gujral Doctrine's** principle of non-reciprocal accommodation reminds us, trust built on water outlasts governments.

### Value additions
- **Treaties/precedents:** 1977 Farakka agreement (guarantee clause); 1996 Mahakali Treaty (Nepal); Indus Waters Treaty 1960 (World Bank-brokered, now in abeyance).
- **Constitution:** Art. 253, Art. 262 (inter-State water disputes), Entry 56 List I.
- **Doctrine/thinker:** Gujral Doctrine (non-reciprocity); "Neighbourhood First" policy (2014).
- **Data to quote with care:** 35,000 cusecs allotted per side in alternate 10-day periods; 54 shared rivers is commonly cited (verify before quoting).
