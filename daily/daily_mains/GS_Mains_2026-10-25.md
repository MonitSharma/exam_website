## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction.** India's founders rejected the "one nation = one culture" template. Patel's integration of the princely states created political unity; the Constitution then made that unity durable by making room for diversity. Article 1 calls India a "Union of States", and Ambedkar's formulation in the Constituent Assembly was that the Union is *indestructible* though its parts are not. Unity here is a negotiated settlement, not an imposed sameness.

**1. Constitutional accommodation**
- **Federal design:** Seventh Schedule division of powers, Article 3 (reorganisation of states), and the Supreme Court's *S.R. Bommai (1994)* ruling that federalism is part of the basic structure.
- **Asymmetric arrangements:** Article 371 (and 371A–J) for specific regions such as Nagaland and Mizoram, and the **Fifth/Sixth Schedules** for tribal self-governance, recognise distinct histories.
- **Cultural and linguistic safeguards:** Articles 29–30 (minority cultural/educational rights), the **Eighth Schedule (22 languages)**, Article 350A/B, and Articles 25–28 on religious freedom with secularism as a basic feature.
- **Language compromise:** Hindi as official language with English continuing, and the three-language formula, as a political bargain.

**2. Historical-political evidence**
- **Linguistic reorganisation:** The initial fear that linguistic states would fragment India proved unfounded. After the Fazl Ali–led **States Reorganisation Commission (1953)** and the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**, regional identity was channelled into the federal system. The 1960s anti-Hindi-imposition agitations ended with the accommodative Official Languages (Amendment) Act, 1967.
- **Peace accords:** Settlements such as the Mizo Accord (1986) and the Bodo accords brought insurgencies into the democratic fold.
- **Federal institutions:** The Finance Commission, the GST Council (a cooperative-federal body) and Inter-State Council show negotiated governance; electoral democracy lets regional parties share power at the Centre.

**3. Where the thesis is tested (limits)**
- **Imposition fears:** Disputes over language policy, delimitation and fiscal federalism show accommodation needs constant renewal.
- **Security-driven unity:** Left-wing extremism and insurgencies show that where development and rights are delayed, alienation hardens; the declared end of armed Naxalism is a security success, but durable integration needs tribal land rights (PESA, FRA) and services.
- **Majoritarian and identity politics** can turn diversity into a zero-sum contest; excess accommodation can also entrench vote-bank politics.
- **Critics' view:** Some argue a strong centre (a "unity first" approach) is needed against separatism; India's own record shows balance, not either extreme, has worked.

**Conclusion.** India's resilience since 1947 lies in being a "state-nation" rather than a nation-state: shared constitutional citizenship held together by plural identities. As Ekta Diwas is observed, the best tribute to Patel's work is to pair security and integration with cooperative federalism, inclusive development of border and tribal regions, and respect for linguistic and cultural plurality — unity *through* diversity, in line with the constitutional fraternity that assures "the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation."

### Value-additions (use 2–3 in your answer)
1. **Ambedkar (Constituent Assembly, Nov 1948):** "indestructible Union of destructible states" — federation is not an agreement among states, which cannot secede.
2. **Alfred Stepan's "state-nation" concept** (Stepan, Linz & Yadav, *Crafting State-Nations*, 2011) — India as a model of unity with plural identities.
3. **Cases/commissions:** *S.R. Bommai (1994)*; **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** and **Punchhi Commission (2010)** on Centre–State relations; **SRC 1953**.
4. **Preamble:** "Fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation."
