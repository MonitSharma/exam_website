# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-17 (GS3 — Science & Technology / Environment: Climate Policy & Carbon Markets)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Thursday = GS3 (Sci-Tech / Environment). One question to write today; the model answer is below the divider — attempt it on paper first.*

> **This week's hook:** India's industrial decarbonisation stopped being voluntary this year, and the world stopped waiting for it in the same year.
>
> Domestically, the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)** — notified by the **Ministry of Power on 28 June 2023** under **Section 14AA of the Energy Conservation Act, 2001**, inserted by the **Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022** — has entered its first binding cycle. The **Greenhouse Gases Emission Intensity (GEI) Target Rules**, in force from **9 October 2025**, are **India's first legally binding industrial emission-intensity obligations**, covering roughly **490 obligated entities**; by **March 2026** GEI targets stood notified across **nine energy-intensive sectors** — aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, pulp & paper, iron & steel, fertiliser, petrochemicals, petroleum refining and textiles. The architecture is unusual: **BEE** administers and issues Carbon Credit Certificates (CCCs), **MoEFCC** notifies targets, **Grid Controller of India** operates the registry, and **CERC** regulates the trading platform. Verified FY 2025–26 data was due by **31 July 2026**, with the **first compliance-market trading of CCCs expected from around October 2026** — weeks away. Crucially, the scheme caps **intensity (tCO₂e per unit of output)**, not absolute emissions, and a parallel **offset mechanism** (ICM portal open for voluntary registration since **June 2025**) lets non-obligated entities earn credits.
>
> Externally, the deadline has already passed. The **EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026**: every consignment of Indian steel, aluminium and cement entering the EU now carries a carbon charge, with **mandatory third-party verification** of embedded emissions. Exporters without verified product-level data face **EU default values reportedly 30–80% above actual emissions**, and **GTRI** estimates price cuts of **15–22%** may be needed to absorb the burden. The trade effect is visible: India's unwrought aluminium shipments to the EU fell about **41.7%** year-on-year in the twelve months to January 2026 (≈18,654 t to ≈10,875 t). And the **India–EU FTA, whose negotiations concluded in New Delhi on 27 January 2026** — with signature proposals before the Council in **September 2026** — **buys no CBAM exemption**: the European Commission has said plainly that no more favourable treatment is granted. What India did secure is narrower and forward-looking — most-favoured treatment on any CBAM flexibilities the EU extends to third countries, technical cooperation on **recognition of India's domestic carbon price and accredited verifiers**, and adaptation support.
>
> All of this sits inside an ambition that has just been raised: the Cabinet approved India's **NDC for 2031–35 on 25 March 2026** — a **47% cut in emissions intensity of GDP by 2035 (over 2005)** and **60% non-fossil power capacity** — against a record of having crossed **52.57% non-fossil capacity by February 2026**, meeting the 50% goal five years early, and a **36% intensity reduction between 2005 and 2020**. Power-sector decarbonisation is running ahead of schedule; **industrial** decarbonisation is where the next NDC will be won or lost, and the carbon market is the chosen instrument.
>
> That collision — *between a young, intensity-based domestic carbon market and a mature, absolute-emissions carbon tariff at the border* — is today's GS3 theme. It is deliberately distinct from the recent GS3 questions on the **SHANTI Act and nuclear energy (10 September, energy supply)**, **private capex (15 September)** and the **Labour Codes (8 September)**; from the GS1 question on the **changing monsoon (9 September, climate science)**; and from the GS2-IR question on **BRICS (11 September)** — though one line of the BRICS/Global-South material is legitimately borrowable for the CBDR argument, and one line from the monsoon answer for why India's stake in mitigation is not merely diplomatic.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme has entered its first binding compliance cycle even as the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism became fully operational in January 2026. **Evaluate** the design of India's carbon market as an instrument of industrial decarbonisation, and assess how far it can shield Indian exporters from carbon border measures.

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (definition + anchor)

A carbon market prices emissions so that abatement occurs where it is cheapest. India's **CCTS**, notified in **2023** under **Section 14AA of the Energy Conservation Act, 2001**, became binding with the **GEI Target Rules of October 2025** — India's first legally enforceable industrial emission-intensity obligations, covering about **490 entities across nine sectors**, with trading expected from **late 2026**. It arrives just as the **EU's CBAM turned definitive on 1 January 2026**.

### A. Where the design is sound

- **Intensity, not absolute caps:** targets in **tCO₂e per unit of output** decouple emissions from growth — appropriate for an economy that must still expand steel and cement capacity, and consistent with the NDC's own intensity framing (**47% cut by 2035**).
- **Institutional separation:** MoEFCC sets targets, **BEE** issues CCCs, **Grid Controller of India** holds the registry, **CERC** regulates the platform — reducing the conflict of interest that plagues single-agency schemes.
- **Compliance plus offset:** an offset track lets MSMEs, forestry and renewables outside the obligated sectors monetise abatement, widening participation.
- **Continuity:** it builds on a decade of **PAT** monitoring, so MRV is not being created from zero.

### B. Where it is vulnerable

- **Weak scarcity:** intensity targets notified close to business-as-usual, plus an offset inflow, risk repeating the **PAT ESCerts** experience — chronic oversupply and a price too low to shift investment. No price floor or reserve has been established.
- **Total emissions can still rise** even under full compliance — a poor fit for a world moving to absolute budgets.
- **MRV depth:** verification capacity, not target-setting, is the binding constraint, and it is exactly what CBAM tests.
- **Coverage gaps:** transport, buildings and most MSMEs — a large share of industrial emissions — remain outside.

### C. The CBAM interface: partial shield at best

CBAM deducts a **carbon price actually paid** on embedded emissions; it does not credit intensity improvements as such. Three problems follow. First, a **low CCC price yields a small deduction**. Second, CBAM requires **third-party-verified, product-level** data; without it, exporters face **default values reportedly 30–80% above actual emissions** — GTRI estimates price cuts of **15–22%** to stay competitive, and India's unwrought aluminium exports to the EU already fell about **41.7%** year-on-year to January 2026. Third, recognition is **legal, not automatic**: the **India–EU FTA concluded on 27 January 2026 grants no CBAM exemption**, only most-favoured treatment on future flexibilities and cooperation on recognising India's carbon price and verifiers.

### Conclusion (forward-looking, sequenced)

The CCTS is a well-architected first step, not a shield. Its value will be decided by **price**, not design. India should sequence: tighten GEI trajectories with a **transparent price floor and market-stability reserve**; build accredited **verifier capacity** at scale so exporters carry their own emissions data; pursue **mutual recognition** of the Indian carbon price under the FTA's cooperation clause and **Article 6** linkages; and fund the abatement itself — green hydrogen in steel, blended cement, scrap-based aluminium — since a price signal without technology only relocates production. A carbon market that makes decarbonisation profitable at home is the only durable answer to a carbon tariff abroad.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS (weave in 2–3, don't list them)

1. **The PAT precedent (BEE, from 2012):** India's ESCerts market under Perform-Achieve-Trade is the single most useful cautionary analogy — lenient targets plus oversupply produced a price signal too weak to drive investment. Citing it shows you understand that *a market exists only when scarcity does*, and it is far better than a generic "EU ETS comparison".
2. **Article 6 of the Paris Agreement:** Grid Controller of India's registry is designed to link to international systems, making Article 6.2 (cooperative approaches) and 6.4 (the PACM mechanism) the legal route by which Indian credits could gain external recognition. Use this in the conclusion, not the body.
3. **CBDR-RC and the equity argument:** CBAM taxes *territorial* emissions at the border while the EU's historical and consumption-based responsibility goes unpriced — the basis of India's position that unilateral trade measures sit uneasily with **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities** and with WTO non-discrimination. One sentence only; the examiner rewards the legal framing, not the grievance.
4. **Data anchors worth memorising:** ~490 obligated entities, nine sectors; GEI Rules in force 9 October 2025; CBAM definitive 1 January 2026; NDC 2031–35 approved 25 March 2026 (47% intensity cut, 60% non-fossil capacity by 2035); 52.57% non-fossil capacity already reached by February 2026.
5. **A usable line for the intro or conclusion:** *"A carbon price that is not scarce is only an accounting entry — at home it does not abate, and at the border it does not count."*
