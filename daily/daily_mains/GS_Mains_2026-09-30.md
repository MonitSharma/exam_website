## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> "Vande Mataram" began as a literary hymn and became the most potent slogan of India's freedom struggle, yet it also became a site of contestation over the meaning of the nation. **Examine** the role of Vande Mataram in the national movement, and discuss why it generated controversy, and how the Congress and the Constituent Assembly sought to resolve it.

## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction**
Composed by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay in **1875** and later placed in his novel ***Anandamath* (1882)**, Vande Mataram imagined the motherland as a nurturing, empowering presence. Over 1905–47 it travelled from a literary work to a mass political slogan — and, because the novel's context and later stanzas carried religious imagery, it also became a point of debate on whether a national symbol could be inclusive of all communities.

**Role in the national movement**
- **Cultural awakening:** gave the nation an emotional, *affective* form — "motherland" as something to serve and sacrifice for — at a time when political identity was still being forged.
- **Congress association:** **Rabindranath Tagore** sang it at the **1896 Calcutta Congress session**, placing it in the organisation's public culture.
- **Swadeshi–Boycott phase (1905–08):** after the **Partition of Bengal (1905)** it became the *battle-cry* of the movement. The **Barisal Conference (1906)** was dispersed by police for shouting it; colonial authorities responded with bans on public chanting, fines and expulsions of students. The journal ***Bande Mataram*** (edited by Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo Ghose) carried the radical nationalist line.
- **Revolutionary and international nationalism:** **Madame Bhikaji Cama** unfurled a flag inscribed with "Vande Mataram" at Stuttgart (**1907**); it was the last word on the lips of many revolutionaries.
- **Mass mobilisation:** chanted in Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience and Quit India demonstrations; it was a *shared* slogan across regions and linguistic groups.

**Why it became contentious**
- **Religious imagery:** the later stanzas (and the *Anandamath* context, in which the "Children" fight a predominantly Muslim-ruled regime) personify the nation as a Hindu goddess (Durga/Lakshmi/Saraswati) — which many Muslims and some others felt made it a devotional rather than civic song. Concerns about *idol-worship* were the core objection.
- **Politics of the 1930s:** as communal politics sharpened, the Muslim League and other voices objected, and the issue became part of a larger argument on the *symbols of a composite nation*.
- **Contested memory:** historians stress that the song meant different things in different phases — a *modern civic slogan* for crowds, a *religious-revivalist text* for some readers.

**Attempts at resolution**
- **1937 — Congress Working Committee:** after Jawaharlal Nehru consulted **Tagore**, the CWC resolved that only the **first two stanzas** — which speak of the land's beauty and abundance and carry no overtly religious reference — be sung at national gatherings, and that no one should be *compelled* to sing it. (Today this is itself debated: critics argue the compromise dropped part of the work; defenders see it as inclusive statesmanship.)
- **24 January 1950 — Constituent Assembly:** President **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** declared that **Jana Gana Mana** would be the national anthem and that **Vande Mataram**, having played a historic part in the struggle, would be **honoured equally** and have *equal status*.

**Conclusion**
Vande Mataram shows that national symbols are *made* historically, not given — they unify most when they are *invitations* rather than *tests* of belonging. The Constituent Assembly's settlement (honour without compulsion) reflects the constitutional ideal of **fraternity** (Preamble) and the freedom of conscience in **Art. 25**; and *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986)* affirmed that one cannot be forced to sing the anthem if it offends genuine conscientious belief. The anniversary is best used to recover the song's history, not to reopen the question of who is "sufficiently national".

**Value additions**
1. **Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986):** SC held that standing respectfully during the anthem while not singing it is not disrespect — the freedom of conscience principle (Art. 19(1)(a)/25) applies to national symbols.
2. **Tagore's view:** Tagore appreciated the first stanzas' spirit but warned that the later parts could not be reconciled with the faith of *all* Indians — echoing his wider critique of aggressive nationalism (*Nationalism*, 1917).
3. **Constitutional hooks:** Art. 51A(a) (duty to respect the Constitution, national flag and anthem); Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 (covers the Constitution, flag and anthem — check current text before quoting).
