## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

**"India has chosen to govern Artificial Intelligence through voluntary guidelines rather than a dedicated law, while remaining dependent on imported compute."** Critically analyse the adequacy of this approach in balancing innovation, safety and technological sovereignty.

## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction**
India's **AI Governance Guidelines (Nov 2025)** adopt a pro-innovation, principle-based, light-touch model, relying on existing laws (DPDP Act 2023, IT Act 2000, sectoral regulators like RBI/SEBI) instead of a horizontal statute such as the EU's risk-based AI Act. Simultaneously, the IndiaAI Mission's subsidised compute (~34,000 GPUs) runs largely on foreign, export-control-exposed hardware. The question is whether this mix is adequate.

**Why the approach has merit**
- **Innovation and agility:** Technology evolves faster than legislation (the Collingridge dilemma); sandboxes and self-certification avoid premature, rigid rules and compliance burden on start-ups/MSMEs.
- **Existing legal cover:** Data protection (DPDP Act), IT Act intermediary rules, consumer and sectoral laws already reach many harms; the Guidelines retain the option of tougher rules if needed (notably on deepfakes).
- **Institutional architecture:** An AI Governance Group for inter-ministerial coordination, an expert committee and an AI Safety Institute give a techno-legal, adaptive structure.
- **Development orientation:** IndiaAI Mission, Indian-language models and AI-on-DPI (Aadhaar/UPI-style rails) promote inclusion.

**Where it falls short**
- **Weak enforceability:** Voluntary compliance has no liability, audit or penalty teeth; high-stakes uses (credit, hiring, policing, health) need binding duties — algorithmic bias, opacity and accountability gaps persist. Puttaswamy (2017) proportionality tests expect legal backing for rights-affecting systems.
- **Institutional lag:** Proposed bodies are reportedly not all operational; fragmented sectoral oversight risks regulatory gaps and overlap.
- **Harms outpace guidelines:** Deepfakes, misinformation, copyright of training data, and frontier-model risks need clear rules.
- **Sovereignty deficit:** Dependence on Nvidia hardware exposes India to export controls and possible extraterritorial limits on remote compute access; governance without compute and indigenous models (semiconductor, data centre capacity) is hollow. Also energy/water footprint of data centres.

**Way forward**
- A **risk-tiered** approach: voluntary for low-risk, binding obligations (impact assessment, audit, human oversight, grievance redress) for high-risk uses.
- Statutorily empower the AI Safety Institute; clarify liability along the AI value chain; resolve AI–copyright and deepfake labelling.
- Deepen compute sovereignty: India Semiconductor Mission, domestic data-centre and green power capacity, diversified supply partnerships, open Indian-language models.
- Align with global processes (G20, GPAI, the Feb 2026 AI Impact Summit in New Delhi) while retaining policy space.

**Conclusion**
Light-touch regulation is defensible at an early stage, but "voluntary" cannot remain the end-state where rights and safety are at stake. India should move from principles to proportionate, risk-based, enforceable rules, anchored in genuine compute and model sovereignty — innovation and safety are complements, not trade-offs.

**Value-additions**
- **Data/scheme:** IndiaAI Mission (approved Mar 2024, outlay ~₹10,372 crore); ~34,000 GPUs as reported by the government.
- **Law/case:** DPDP Act 2023; *K.S. Puttaswamy v. UoI (2017)* — privacy and proportionality; *Shreya Singhal (2015)* — limits on speech restrictions (relevant to deepfake rules).
- **Comparative/thinker:** EU AI Act (risk-based, in force since 2024) vs US sectoral approach; David Collingridge's "dilemma of control".
