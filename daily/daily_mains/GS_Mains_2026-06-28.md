> **This week's hook:** The **State of Working India 2026** (Azim Premji University) found unemployment running near **40% among graduates aged 15–25** and ~20% for 25–29; among unemployed youth aged 20–29, about **two-thirds (67%) were graduates** in 2023 (≈1.1 crore of 6.3 crore). The **PLFS Annual Report 2024–25** shows a calmer headline — overall unemployment **3.1%**, youth (15–29) **9.9%** — but with roughly **a quarter of youth in NEET** (not in employment, education or training). Against this, India's **demographic-dividend window (≈2005–06 to 2055–56)** is already half-spent, with the working-age share set to **peak around 2041**. The clock and the data together make youth, jobs and aspiration the live cross-cutting theme — distinct from last Sunday's *care-economy* essay, and from this week's GST, Governor, Emergency and AI questions.

## MODEL ANSWER

**Intro (context + data).** A *demographic dividend* — the growth potential that opens when the working-age share (15–64) exceeds dependents (UNFPA) — is India's defining opportunity. The window runs roughly **2005–06 to 2055–56**, longer than any other country, but it is finite: the working-age share is projected to **peak around 2041**, and the median age has already climbed from 24 (2011) to about **29 today**. A dividend, however, is *earned, not given* — and the early evidence is sobering. The **State of Working India 2026** finds graduate unemployment near **40%** for those aged 15–25, and the share of graduates among unemployed youth has **doubled in two decades** (≈32% in 2004 → 67% in 2023). The bulge is here; the dividend is not assured.

**Why conversion is failing — a multidimensional view:**

- **Jobs deficit, not a skills deficit alone.** Roughly five million graduates enter the workforce yearly against far fewer quality jobs created; **less than 7% of male graduates** secure a permanent salaried job within a year. The problem is as much *demand* (slow formal-job creation) as *supply* (employability).
- **Education–employment mismatch.** Rising enrolment has not meant rising *capability*; degrees often signal credentials without market-relevant skills, leaving graduates over-qualified for available work and under-prepared for good work.
- **NEET and discouraged youth.** With about **a quarter of youth in NEET**, a large cohort is neither building human capital nor earning — a silent erosion of potential.
- **Gender gap.** Female LFPR (~40%) less than half the male rate means India is harnessing barely half its dividend.
- **Regional & informality dimensions.** Dividend timing is uneven — younger, poorer states (Bihar, UP) face peak youth pressure with the weakest job markets, while ~90% informality caps productivity and security.

**The other side — why pessimism must be qualified.** Unemployment is concentrated among *educated, aspirational* youth who can afford to *search* for better jobs, not the destitute — itself a sign of rising aspiration. Overall LFPR (59.3%) and female participation are improving, and a young, digitally-fluent population still anchors India's services, manufacturing and AI ambitions. The dividend is *delayed and at risk*, not *lost*.

**Conclusion (forward-looking, values-anchored).** Realising the dividend needs more than schemes; it needs an *ecosystem* — labour-intensive manufacturing and care/green jobs to absorb numbers, NEP-2020-style vocationalisation tied to industry, credible apprenticeships, and decisive action on female participation. But the deeper task is ethical and developmental: as **Amartya Sen** argues, growth must expand *capabilities and freedoms*, not just credentials. A youth bulge converts into a dividend only when a society treats each young person's aspiration as a public responsibility — otherwise the window closes on a generation that was promised much and absorbed little.

### Value-additions to memorise (use 2–3, not all)

1. **Data anchors:** Demographic window **≈2005–06 to 2055–56** (UNFPA, longest in world); working-age share peaks **~2041** (Economic Survey 2018–19); **median age ~29 (2026) → 36 by 2036**; **State of Working India 2026** (Azim Premji Univ.) — graduate unemployment ~**40%** (15–25), graduates = **67%** of unemployed youth 20–29 (2023); **PLFS 2024–25** — LFPR **59.3%**, unemployment **3.1%**, youth **9.9%**, female LFPR **~40%**, ~**25% NEET**.
2. **Scheme/policy:** **Employment Linked Incentive (ELI) Scheme** (Cabinet-approved; outlay **₹99,446 cr**, targeting 3.5+ crore jobs) under the Budget 2024–25 **PM's package of 5 schemes (₹2 lakh crore, 4.1 crore youth over 5 years)**; **NEP 2020** vocational/skilling thrust; **Skill India / PMKVY**.
3. **Thinker/framework:** **Amartya Sen**, *Development as Freedom* — the **capability approach** (human *capability* > human *capital*); pair with the warning that an unconverted youth bulge becomes a **"demographic disaster,"** and the East-Asian lesson that the dividend was *policy-made*, not automatic.
