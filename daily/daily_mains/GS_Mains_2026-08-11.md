## Model Answer

**Introduction (context + data):**
India's external sector absorbed a severe tariff shock in 2025–26 — the US effective tariff briefly touched ~50% before a February 2026 bilateral deal pared it to ~18% — yet **merchandise exports grew ~2.4% and services exports ~6.5% in April–December 2025**, and the **current account deficit held at 0.8% of GDP in H1 FY26** (vs 1.3% a year earlier). This resilience owes much to a deliberate strategy of **market diversification** and a wave of **free trade agreements (FTAs)**.

**How diversification and FTAs de-risk the external sector:**

- **Reducing single-market dependence:** With the US as India's largest export destination, tariff exposure was concentrated. Redirecting shipments toward the **EU, UK, West Asia and Southeast Asia** cushions country-specific shocks — a portfolio-diversification logic applied to trade.
- **Preferential market access:** The **India–UK CETA** (in force 15 July 2026) makes ~**99% of Indian goods duty-free** into the UK, aiding labour-intensive sectors (textiles, leather, gems). The **India–EU FTA** (concluded January 2026, ~EUR 120 billion trade) opens a 27-nation bloc.
- **Services and remittances as ballast:** India's **services export share rose from 2% (2005) to 4.3% (2024)**; services surpluses and remittances structurally offset the merchandise deficit, keeping the CAD manageable.
- **Integrating into supply chains:** FTAs with rules-of-origin and mutual recognition help India plug into **"China+1"** diversification and global value chains, supporting the PLI-led manufacturing push.

**Critical limitations:**

- **Trade diversion vs creation:** FTAs can widen deficits with partners (India's past experience with ASEAN/RCEP-type concerns) if domestic industry is uncompetitive; **inverted duty structures** and weak logistics blunt gains.
- **Concessions carry costs:** The US deal reportedly involves **near-zero tariffs on US goods** and large energy/agri import commitments, raising **import-surge and farm-sensitivity** worries.
- **Non-tariff barriers:** EU/UK **carbon border adjustments (CBAM), sustainability and standards** requirements can erode preferential access for MSMEs.
- **Structural export weakness:** India's **share in global merchandise exports is still only ~1.8% (2024)**; diversification cannot substitute for competitiveness, scale and R&D.
- **External vulnerability persists:** Energy-price and geopolitical shocks (West Asia) still transmit directly to the CAD and the rupee.

**Conclusion (forward-looking, balanced):**
Diversification and FTAs are necessary shock-absorbers, but they are **enablers, not substitutes** for competitiveness. De-risking must be paired with **fixing inverted duties, cutting logistics costs (target of the National Logistics Policy), quality upgradation for CBAM-type norms, and deepening services and high-value manufacturing exports**. Used together, market diversification and rules-based trade agreements can move India credibly toward its **US$2 trillion exports by 2030** ambition while insulating the external account from single-market and geopolitical shocks.
