# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-16 (GS1 — Indian Society: Population, Migration & Urbanisation)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Wednesday = GS1 (History/Society/Geography). One question to write today; the model answer is below the divider — attempt it on paper first.*

> **This week's hook:** India is about to *look properly* at its own internal migrants for the first time in nearly two decades. **MoSPI/NSO will run a year-long National Migration Survey from July 2026 to June 2027** — the first dedicated migration round since the **NSS 64th Round (2007–08)** — covering short-term, long-term and return migration, reasons for movement, remittances, housing and employment status, across all States and UTs.
>
> The data vacuum it fills is striking. The last authoritative picture came from the **EAC-PM's "400 Million Dreams!" (December 2024)**, which used Railway UTS ticketing, TRAI roaming and district banking data to estimate **40.20 crore internal migrants in 2023 — 28.88% of the population — down from 45.57 crore (37.64%) in the 2011 Census**, an **11.78% fall**. Whether that decline reflects genuine convergence between States or merely the limits of proxy data is exactly what the new survey must settle.
>
> Meanwhile the migrant's *legal* position has moved, and his *social* position has moved in the opposite direction. The **four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025**; the **OSH Code, 2020** subsumed the **Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979**, widened the definition to include **self-migrating workers earning up to ₹18,000 a month**, and created a **journey allowance** for an annual round trip home after 180 days of work. **e-Shram crossed 31.89 crore registered unorganised workers by 18 August 2026** (**54.28% women**), with **24.46 crore** linked to **ONORC** — even as the Supreme Court, in **In Re: Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers**, has had to repeatedly extend deadlines for States to issue ration cards to e-Shram registrants who still have none.
>
> And in **September 2026**, a different question is before the Court altogether: in a PIL by the **West Bengal Migrant Welfare Board**, a Bench of **CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana** has asked the Union to verify allegations that **Bengali-speaking migrant workers were detained as suspected foreigners essentially on the basis of the language they speak**, reiterating that the **Foreigners Act does not authorise detention on mere suspicion**; MPs have written to the Home Ministry over reported profiling in Chhattisgarh and elsewhere.
>
> That gap — *between a migrant who is economically indispensable and a migrant who is administratively, politically and socially a partial citizen at his destination* — is today's GS1 Society theme. It is deliberately distinct from the GS1 questions on the **monsoon (9 September, Geography)**, the **caste-enumerated Census (1 July, Society)** and the **Emergency (24 June, History)**; from the Sunday essays on **urban governance and municipal finance (6 September)** and **delimitation (13 September)**; and from the GS3 questions on the **Labour Codes (8 September)** and **private capex (15 September)** — though one line of municipal-finance material is legitimately borrowable for why destination cities under-provide for migrants.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> The internal migrant is central to India's economy but remains, at his destination, a partial citizen — counted by no register, represented by no constituency, and entitled to little that is not tied to domicile. **Critically examine** the causes of this exclusion, and assess whether India's recent institutional responses adequately address it.

**(Directive: *critically examine* — break the exclusion into its distinct components and test each against evidence, then judge the response rather than merely listing schemes. Do **not** write a descriptive "types and causes of migration / push and pull factors" answer; that is a 4-mark answer. The marks lie in (a) separating **statistical**, **administrative**, **political** and **social** exclusion as *different* failures with different remedies; (b) taking the **identity/"sons of the soil"** dimension seriously, including its constitutional limits; (c) a genuinely two-sided assessment of ONORC, e-Shram and the OSH Code — crediting what they changed before naming what they cannot do; and (d) a forward-looking close built on the idea of **portable social citizenship**, sequenced rather than listed. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (framing + anchor)

India's welfare state is built around **residence**; its labour market runs on **movement**. The **EAC-PM's "400 Million Dreams!" (2024)** counts **40.20 crore internal migrants (28.88% of the population)**, and the **NSO's National Migration Survey (July 2026–June 2027)** — the first since 2007–08 — is itself an admission that we have been governing this population without measuring it. The result is a worker who is economically **portable** but whose **citizenship is not**.

### A. Four distinct exclusions

- **Statistical invisibility.** Census categories capture *reason for last move*: **66.7% of female migration is recorded as marriage**, against **26% employment/business among males (Census 2011)** — so the woman who migrates on marriage and then works is counted as a bride, not a worker. Circular and seasonal migrants, who return before any enumeration window, are largely uncounted. Policy cannot budget for a population it cannot size.
- **Administrative exclusion.** Entitlements — subsidised grain, housing, school admission, ICDS, health cards, even utility connections — are anchored to **domicile and address proof**, precisely the two things a circular migrant lacks. Destination municipalities plan for their *resident* population and finance from *resident* revenue, so migrant settlements fall between two governments: neither the origin State nor the destination city treats them as its charge.
- **Political exclusion.** A migrant votes where he is registered, which is usually home. He therefore represents **no vote at his destination**, and provision for him carries **no electoral return** for the destination legislator. The **ECI's remote voting (RVM) proposal (2023)** remains unimplemented. This, more than any budget constraint, explains persistent under-provision.
- **Social exclusion and identity.** Migration triggers nativist politics — what **Myron Weiner** analysed in ***Sons of the Soil: Migration and Ethnic Conflict in India* (1978)**: competition for jobs and urban space gets reframed as a contest between "locals" and "outsiders". Its contemporary form is sharper, because **linguistic identity is being read as evidence of foreign origin** — the issue the **Supreme Court (CJI Surya Kant's Bench, 2026)** is examining in the **West Bengal Migrant Welfare Board** PIL on the detention of Bengali-speaking workers.

### B. Assessing the institutional response

**What has genuinely changed.**

- **ONORC** decoupled the ration card from the fair-price shop — the single most important portability reform India has made; **24.46 crore** e-Shram registrants are ONORC-linked.
- **e-Shram** created, for the first time, a national register of **31.89 crore unorganised workers (August 2026)** — the precondition for any targeted delivery.
- **The OSH Code, 2020 (in force 21 November 2025)** replaced the almost-unenforced **Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act, 1979**, extended coverage to **self-migrating workers earning up to ₹18,000/month** (the old Act covered only contractor-recruited labour, which is why it caught so few), and added a **journey allowance** and portability of benefits.
- **Judicial insistence.** In ***In Re: Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers***, the Court has converted registration into an enforceable claim, directing States to issue ration cards to e-Shram registrants who lack them.

**What it does not reach.**

- **Registration is not entitlement.** The Court has had to extend its ration-card deadlines repeatedly precisely because being on a database does not automatically produce a card, a school seat or a hospital bed.
- **Only food is portable.** Housing, schooling, maternity benefits, ICDS and most State schemes remain domicile-locked. ONORC is a template that has not been generalised.
- **Enforcement presumes an employer.** The OSH Code's protections are triggered through registered establishments and contractors; the self-migrating worker in construction, domestic work or street vending — the bulk of the flow — has no such counterparty.
- **No institutional owner.** The **MoHUA Working Group on Migration (2017)** and **NITI Aayog's Draft National Policy on Migrant Workers (2021)** — which deliberately chose a **rights-and-capability approach over cash-and-quota handouts** — were never adopted. There is no nodal architecture, no inter-State migration council, no migration resource centres at scale.
- **The identity problem is not a welfare problem.** No amount of portability answers profiling. Here the constitutional floor matters: **Article 19(1)(d) and (e)** guarantee free movement and residence anywhere in India, and **Article 15(1)** bars discrimination on grounds of **place of birth** — which is why the **Punjab & Haryana High Court struck down the Haryana State Employment of Local Candidates Act, 2020 on 17 November 2023** for reserving 75% of lower-paid private jobs for local candidates.

### Conclusion (balanced, forward-looking)

The direction of travel is right; the pace and the frame are not. The task is **portable social citizenship** — and it sequences naturally: **measure** (use the 2026–27 Migration Survey to size circular and female work-migration, and feed migrant headcounts into Finance Commission devolution and city plans) → **generalise portability** beyond food to health, schooling, maternity benefit and rental housing, with e-Shram as the spine → **give the migrant a stake where he lives**, whether through remote voting or destination-side registration → and **defend the constitutional floor**, so that free movement within India is not conditioned on the language a worker speaks. India's internal migrant already subsidises its cities with cheap labour and its villages with remittances; the minimum return is that he should not have to choose between his livelihood and his entitlements.

## Examiner's note — what separates a 9 from a 12 here

1. **Do not write a migration-typology answer.** "Push and pull factors, Ravenstein, types of migration" is the trap. The question is about *exclusion at the destination*. Spend no more than one clause on why people move.
2. **Separate the four exclusions explicitly.** Statistical, administrative, political and social are *different* failures needing *different* remedies — measurement, portability, representation, constitutional protection. Most candidates blur them into "problems faced by migrants". The separation itself earns marks.
3. **The political point is the discriminator.** Very few answers will say that a migrant's disenfranchisement at the destination is the *root* of under-provision there. Write it in one sharp sentence.
4. **Be fair to the reforms.** ONORC and e-Shram are real achievements; an answer that dismisses them reads as ideological. Credit them with a figure, *then* state the limit.
5. **Handle the identity dimension carefully and constitutionally.** Frame it through **Articles 19(1)(d)/(e) and 15(1)** and through what the Court is examining — not through party politics. UPSC rewards constitutional framing and penalises partisan framing.
6. **Keep one line for women.** The marriage-migration statistic lets you show that a large part of India's female workforce is statistically filed under a domestic category. That is a genuinely multidimensional point in a GS1 Society answer.
