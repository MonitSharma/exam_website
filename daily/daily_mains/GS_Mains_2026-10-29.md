## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction (context)**
Gaganyaan aims to put Indian astronauts in low-Earth orbit on an Indian rocket (human-rated LVM3) and return them safely. Alongside it, the Indian Space Policy 2023 and the creation of IN-SPACe (2020) move ISRO from being India's sole space actor to an enabler of a wider ecosystem. Together they turn space from a symbol of prestige into an instrument of national power and growth.

**Body**

*1. Strategic*
- Human-rating of launchers, life support, crew escape and re-entry builds a sovereign capability that few nations hold, and lessens dependence on partners for crewed access.
- Space is a contested domain: dual-use launch, communication and surveillance capacity strengthens deterrence and situational awareness.
- Space diplomacy: lending credibility to partnerships (e.g., the Artemis Accords, in which India is a signatory) and to leadership in the Global South.

*2. Scientific and technological*
- Microgravity research, life sciences and materials experiments; a base for the planned Bharatiya Antariksh Station and longer-term lunar goals.
- Spin-offs in avionics, thermal protection, ECLSS and robotics (Vyommitra) that feed civilian industry and a deeper STEM pipeline.

*3. Economic*
- The 2024 FDI liberalisation (automatic route up to 74% in satellite manufacturing/operation, 49% in launch vehicles, and 100% for components) and a dedicated venture fund for space start-ups crowd in private capital.
- Private players (launch start-ups, satellite builders, Earth-observation firms) can cut costs, raise launch cadence and capture a larger share of the global commercial market, with ISRO's NSIL as commercial arm.

*4. Challenges and regulatory gaps*
- **No comprehensive space law.** The draft Space Activities Bill has not been enacted, so authorisation, licensing, liability, insurance and dispute resolution rest on policy documents, not statute.
- **International obligations.** India is party to the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and the Liability Convention (1972), under which the State bears international liability for private activity; domestic law must allocate this risk (licence conditions, insurance, indemnity).
- **Schedule and safety.** Repeated delays since the original 2020 target show how exacting crew-safety validation is; a failure would be costly in both lives and credibility.
- **Capacity and funding.** Dependence on a single agency's testing facilities; small private R&D budgets; limited launch-range capacity; risk of debris and orbital congestion; data-policy and export-control clarity for dual-use items.
- **Institutional clarity.** Avoiding overlap between ISRO, IN-SPACe and the Department of Space, and keeping IN-SPACe an independent regulator rather than a promoter-regulator hybrid.

**Way forward**
Enact a space law with clear licensing, liability caps and insurance norms; give IN-SPACe statutory independence; share ISRO test and launch infrastructure on transparent terms; fund indigenous research on propulsion, materials and life-support; adopt debris-mitigation and space-traffic rules; and pursue anchor-customer procurement for start-ups.

**Conclusion**
Gaganyaan will be judged less by the day astronauts fly than by whether it leaves behind a safe, law-governed and commercially vibrant space economy. Pairing ISRO's heritage reliability with regulated private dynamism would make space an engine of Viksit Bharat rather than a showpiece.

## VALUE ADDITIONS
1. **Treaties:** Outer Space Treaty 1967 (non-appropriation, State responsibility for national activities); Liability Convention 1972 (launching State absolutely liable for damage on Earth).
2. **Institutions/policy:** IN-SPACe (2020); Indian Space Policy 2023; FDI liberalisation in the space sector (Feb 2024); NSIL as ISRO's commercial arm.
3. **Case:** Shubhanshu Shukla's flight to the ISS on Axiom-4 (2025), India's second astronaut in space after Rakesh Sharma (1984), as a capability-building step; the US commercial crew model (NASA–SpaceX) as an example of regulated private participation.
