*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Monday = GS2 (Polity/Governance). One question to write today; the model answer is below the divider — attempt it on paper first.*

> **This week's hook:** India is being asked to decide a question it has always dodged — not *who may contest* an election, but *who may continue to govern* while under arrest.
>
> The **Constitution (One Hundred and Thirtieth Amendment) Bill, 2025**, introduced in the Lok Sabha by Home Minister **Amit Shah on 20 August 2025**, amends **Articles 75, 164 and 239AA**, with companion amendments to the **Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act** and the **Government of Union Territories Act, 1963**. The trigger is mechanical: a Union Minister **arrested and detained for thirty consecutive days** on an allegation of an offence **punishable with five years' imprisonment or more** is to be removed by the President on the Prime Minister's advice — and if no advice is tendered, **ceases to hold office on the thirty-first day**. The same applies to State Ministers (Governor) and Delhi's Ministers (Lieutenant Governor). A **Prime Minister or Chief Minister must resign by the thirty-first day**. Re-appointment is permitted after release.
>
> What makes it live *today* is that the Bill is stuck — and stuck on the most interesting point. The **Joint Committee of Parliament, chaired by BJP MP Aparajita Sarangi**, circulated a draft report with five recommendations whose central move was to convert **removal into suspension**, pending the outcome of proceedings, with an **automatic reversal** on acquittal or if the prosecution does not proceed within a specified period. On **17 July 2026**, mid-way through voting on those recommendations, the Committee **deferred adoption of its own draft** for wider stakeholder consultation — days before the Monsoon Session (**20 July – 13 August 2026**) — and the report is now owed to Parliament at the start of the next session. The constitutional debate has spilled into the journals: a **Law School Policy Review** piece of **9 September 2026** argues the removal/suspension distinction is itself a basic-structure question.
>
> Both halves of the problem are real. The **ADR analysis of the 2024 Lok Sabha (June 2024)** found **251 of 543 MPs (46%) with declared criminal cases, 170 (31%) with serious cases and 27 convicted** — a **124% rise in MPs with serious cases since 2009**. The amicus curiae's report in ***Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India*** told the Supreme Court that **4,192 criminal cases remain pending against sitting and former MPs and MLAs**, **754 of them five to ten years old**; in 2025 alone, **1,243 were decided and 1,050 freshly registered** — a docket that replenishes itself. Against that, the Court's own recent instinct runs the other way: in ***Manish Sisodia v. Directorate of Enforcement* (9 August 2024)**, after roughly **17 months of incarceration without the trial commencing**, it held the right to a speedy trial to be a facet of **Article 21** and restated that **"bail is the rule, jail is the exception."**
>
> That collision — *between an electorate's right to be governed by people not sitting in custody, and a constitutional order in which arrest is an executive act and innocence is presumed until a court says otherwise* — is today's GS2 theme. It is deliberately distinct from this month's GS2 questions on **Article 142 and judicial power (7 September)**, **land titling and DILRMP 3.0 (14 September)** and the IR questions on **BRICS (11 September)** and the **Quad (18 September)**; from the Sunday essays on **delimitation (13 September)** and **intermediary rules and free speech (20 September)** — though *one* line from the 7 September answer on institutional trust, and *one* from the 20 September essay on procedure being the real safeguard, are legitimately borrowable.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> The Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025 provides that a Prime Minister, Chief Minister or Minister detained for thirty consecutive days on charges carrying a punishment of five years or more shall cease to hold office. **Critically examine** whether this is an appropriate constitutional response to the criminalisation of politics, and suggest what a defensible framework for ministerial accountability would require.

**(Directive: *critically examine* — investigate both sides and end with a reasoned judgement; do **not** write a descriptive "criminalisation of politics: causes, effects, way forward" answer, which is a 5-mark answer. The marks lie in (a) stating the case *for* the Bill honestly first — Article 75(2)'s "pleasure" doctrine already permits removal, and a minister running a ministry from a prison cell is a real governance failure; (b) locating the objection precisely — the trigger is **arrest, not adjudication**, so the standard is set by an investigating agency rather than a court; (c) the **federal** dimension — the agencies that arrest report to the Union, while the office lost may be an opposition Chief Minister's; (d) noting the **JPC's suspension-with-reversal** alternative and judging it; and (e) a forward-looking close that **sequences** reform — entry-stage disqualification and time-bound trials before automatic cesser. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (anchor + data)

India disqualifies legislators on **conviction** (**Section 8, RPA 1951**), but has never regulated who may *hold executive office* while under investigation. The **130th Amendment Bill, 2025** fills that silence mechanically: thirty days' detention on a charge carrying five years or more, and the office falls away. The problem it addresses is not imagined — **ADR (2024)** records **46% of Lok Sabha MPs with declared criminal cases and 31% with serious ones**, and **4,192 cases against MPs/MLAs remain pending** before the Supreme Court's monitored courts. The difficulty is that the Bill answers a **judicial** problem with an **executive** trigger.

### A. The case for the Bill

- **It restates an existing power, not a new one.** Ministers already hold office **during the pleasure of the President** (**Article 75(2)**) or Governor (**Article 164(1)**). The amendment does not create removability; it removes *discretion* from it, which is precisely what makes it politically honest — a Prime Minister is no longer able to protect a colleague by inaction.
- **Governance is a real casualty.** Collective responsibility under **Article 75(3)** and **164(2)** presumes ministers who can deliberate, sign and answer. A ministry run by proxy from custody is a fiction that damages both the department and the legislature's ability to hold it to account.
- **Constitutional morality has failed as a substitute.** In ***Manoj Narula v. Union of India* (2014)**, a Constitution Bench held it could **not read an additional disqualification into Article 75(1)**, and instead relied on the **constitutional trust** reposed in the Prime Minister, who is "expected not to choose" persons facing framed charges for heinous offences or corruption. Twelve years of rising numbers suggest that expectation is not self-executing — the invitation to Parliament was real, and this Bill accepts it.

### B. Why the design is constitutionally fragile

- **The trigger is arrest, not adjudication.** Detention establishes that an agency suspects, and that a magistrate has not yet granted bail. It is not a finding of guilt. Making it the operative fact inverts the **presumption of innocence**, which the Court has tied to **Article 21** — in ***Manish Sisodia v. Directorate of Enforcement* (2024)** it granted bail after ~17 months precisely because delay had defeated the right to a speedy trial, and restated that **"bail is the rule, jail is the exception."** A Bill that converts prolonged custody into loss of office rewards the very delay the Court was condemning.
- **The standard-setter is an interested party.** The CBI, ED and NIA function under the Union executive. Under **Article 164** and **239AA**, the office extinguished may be that of an **opposition Chief Minister**. Thirty days becomes a target that an investigating agency can, in principle, aim at — and bail delay, not conviction, decides who governs a State. That is a **federalism** objection, and federalism is part of the **basic structure** (***S.R. Bommai*, 1994**).
- **It is over- and under-inclusive at once.** The five-year threshold sweeps in a wide range of offences — including those arising from political agitation — while a minister who secures bail on day twenty-nine, however grave the allegation, is untouched. The variable is the bail calendar, not culpability.
- **It treats the symptom, not the source.** The disease is entry, not tenure: parties nominate candidates with antecedents because they win. Nothing in the Bill changes that incentive.

### C. The JPC's alternative, and its limits

The Committee's draft (deferred **17 July 2026**) proposed **suspension rather than removal**, with **automatic reversal** on acquittal or lapse of prosecution. This is a genuine improvement — it is **reversible**, proportionate, and does not treat an untested allegation as final. But it does not cure the root defect: the trigger remains **custody**, not a judicial finding, and a suspended Chief Minister's government falls in practice even if his office is formally preserved.

### Conclusion (balanced, forward-looking)

Parliament is right that ministerial office cannot be treated as immune to the criminal process; it is wrong to let an **arrest warrant do the work of a verdict**. A defensible framework would **sequence** three reforms rather than substitute one for another: act at **entry**, by adopting the **Law Commission's 244th Report (2014)** proposal for disqualification on the **framing of charges** for offences punishable with five years or more — a judicial act, with the safeguard that charges framed within a year of scrutiny of nominations do not count; make the **trial the remedy**, by resourcing the Supreme Court-monitored **Special Courts for MPs/MLAs** to statutory timelines, since a concluded trial makes the thirty-day device unnecessary; and only then attach consequences to custody — **suspension with automatic restoration**, triggered by a **court's** satisfaction rather than an agency's, with an independent authority sanctioning prosecution of constitutional functionaries. The test of any such law is simple and should be stated: it must be one a government would be content to see applied to itself.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS (weave in 2–3, don't list them)

1. **Law Commission of India, 244th Report (2014)** — proposed a new **Section 8B** in the RPA, 1951: disqualification from the **date of framing of charges** for offences punishable with five years or more, lasting six years or until quashing/acquittal, with the safeguard that charges framed less than one year before scrutiny of nominations do not attract it. The reasoning is directly usable: conviction-based disqualification has failed *because trials are slow*.
2. **Second ARC, 4th Report, "Ethics in Governance"** — recommended amending Section 8 of the RPA to disqualify persons facing charges for grave and heinous offences and corruption where charges were framed **six months before the election**, with **Special Courts at the High Court level** to test the legality of such charges within a strict timeframe, and an express caution against political vendetta.
3. ***Manoj Narula v. Union of India* (2014)** — the Constitution Bench's twin holdings: courts cannot add a disqualification to **Article 75(1)**, but the Prime Minister is bound by **constitutional morality** and **constitutional trust** in choosing ministers. The single most efficient citation here, because it establishes that this is Parliament's job, which is exactly what makes the *design* of the Bill the real question.
4. ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2013)** — struck down **Section 8(4)** of the RPA, so disqualification operates from the date of conviction. Useful for one line showing the existing framework is **conviction-anchored**, which is what the 130th Amendment departs from.
5. **Data pairing** — ADR's **46% / 31%** alongside the amicus report's **4,192 pending cases, 1,243 decided against 1,050 fresh registrations in 2025**. The second number is the better one: it shows the backlog is self-replenishing, which is the strongest argument that *trial reform*, not removal reform, is the binding constraint.

## COMMON TRAPS ON THIS QUESTION

- **Writing a "criminalisation of politics" essay.** The question is about a specific constitutional device. Causes of criminalisation (money, muscle, party finance, winnability) earn marks only where they explain why a *tenure*-stage remedy misses the target.
- **Taking a side in the first paragraph.** "Critically examine" rewards holding both propositions — that the Bill addresses a real failure, and that its trigger is constitutionally unsound — before judging.
- **Ignoring federalism.** Articles **164** and **239AA** are in the Bill for a reason. An answer that discusses only the Prime Minister has missed half the objection.
- **Forgetting the JPC.** The removal-versus-suspension distinction is the examiner's likely differentiator in September 2026, and it is a September-2026 fact that most answers will not have.
- **Ending on a wishlist.** "Strengthen institutions, ensure speedy justice, raise awareness" is a 6-mark close. Sequence the reforms and state the test.
