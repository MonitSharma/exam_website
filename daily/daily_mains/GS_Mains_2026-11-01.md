## MODEL ANSWER

**Introduction.** India's climate diplomacy rests on equity: the atmosphere is a shared, finite resource, and countries that used most of the carbon budget should lead (the principle of *common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities*, CBDR-RC, in the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement). Yet India is among the most climate-exposed large economies: heatwaves, floods, erratic monsoons and sea-level rise land on a population with limited buffers. The question is whether the "right to grow" argument abroad and the "need to adapt" reality at home can sit together. They can, but only if equity is used as a bargaining principle and not as an excuse for slow domestic action.

**1. The justice case abroad (why the argument is strong)**
- **Historical responsibility:** India's cumulative share of emissions is a small single-digit percentage, and its per-capita emissions are well below the global average (check the latest Global Carbon Project / Our World in Data numbers before quoting). Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain's *Global Warming in an Unequal World* (1991) framed the carbon-budget argument that India still uses.
- **Finance and technology gap:** The $100 bn promise was met late, and the COP29 goal of $300 bn a year by 2035 is far below the trillion-dollar scale developing countries sought; India publicly called it inadequate. Climate finance is largely loans and mitigation-heavy, while adaptation is underfunded.
- **Trade as a new front:** Measures like the EU **CBAM** can function as unilateral carbon tariffs on developing-country exports, which sits uneasily with CBDR-RC.
- **Delivery on its own terms:** India's NDC (updated 2022) commits to a 45% reduction in emissions intensity of GDP by 2030 from 2005 levels and 50% non-fossil installed capacity by 2030. Reports say the capacity target was reached years early (verify the exact date). Panchamrit (2021) set 500 GW non-fossil capacity and net-zero by 2070. This gives India credibility in demanding equity.

**2. The domestic reality (why justice abroad is not enough)**
- **Vulnerability is local and unequal:** Heat stress hits outdoor labourers and informal workers; floods and droughts hit small and marginal farmers; coastal communities face cyclones and erosion. Rain-fed agriculture means a deficit monsoon, such as 2026's, moves rural incomes and inflation together.
- **Right to a climate-safe life:** In ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (2024)** the Supreme Court read a right against the adverse effects of climate change into Articles 14 and 21. Articles 48A and 51A(g) add the State's and citizens' duties. Equity abroad therefore has a constitutional counterpart at home.
- **Growth and mitigation can align:** Solar and storage, public transport, efficient cooling and electric mobility cut import dependence and local pollution. Coal is still the base of power supply, so a "just transition" for coal regions (jobs, district finances) is a real equity question within India.

**3. Critical assessment (where the argument is weak)**
- **"Right to develop" can become cover:** Absolute emissions are rising; India is the world's third-largest emitter in absolute terms, and the per-capita argument weakens as the economy grows. Credible leadership of the Global South needs a visible peak-and-decline path.
- **Adaptation is the neglected side:** Heat action plans, early warning, crop insurance and resilient infrastructure exist (for example, Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan, India's push for the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure), but are patchy at the city and village level. Finance is thin and coordination weak.
- **Carbon pricing at home:** The Carbon Credit Trading Scheme under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 is still maturing; CBAM exposure suggests India should price carbon domestically so the revenue stays in India.
- **Low-carbon manufacturing, green hydrogen and critical minerals** need technology access that is itself contested in trade and IPR forums.

**Conclusion.** Climate justice and climate-resilient development are two sides of one strategy: demand finance, technology and trade fairness abroad, and show in numbers that India's own pathway is already decarbonising. Making adaptation finance, a just transition for coal regions and local early-warning systems as visible as the diplomatic position will turn the equity argument from a defensive plea into moral leadership — a development model that does not repeat the West's carbon-intensive path, in the spirit of "needs, not greed" (Gandhi) and Mission LiFE.
