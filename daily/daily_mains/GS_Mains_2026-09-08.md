> **This week's hook:** India's biggest labour-market reform in seven decades is now in its implementation phase. The **four Labour Codes** — Code on Wages (2019), Industrial Relations Code (2020), Code on Social Security (2020) and OSH & Working Conditions Code (2020) — **came into force on 21 November 2025**, rationalising **29 central labour laws** into four, with **draft Central Rules gazetted on 30 December 2025** and state rules still being finalised. Their headline promise is universal social security: for the first time in Indian law, **gig and platform workers** get statutory cover under **Chapter IX (Sections 109–114) of the Social Security Code**, funded by an **aggregator contribution of 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of payments made to such workers**. But a **Data Point analysis published on 4 September 2026** shows the gap between law and reach: while **e-Shram crossed 31.89 crore registrations by August 2026** (five years after launch), only about **8.58 lakh gig workers** were registered by January 2026 — against **NITI Aayog's estimate of ~1.27 crore gig workers in 2024–25**, projected to hit **2.35 crore by 2029–30**. Meanwhile **PLFS Annual Report 2025** shows unemployment at **3.1%** and LFPR at **59.3%**, but **self-employment still at 56.2%** and regular salaried work at only **23.6%**. That gap — between *statutory formalisation* and *lived informality* — is today's GS3 theme, deliberately distinct from the recent GS3-Economy questions on **trade diversification and FTAs (11 August)** and **flexible inflation targeting (30 June)**, and from Sunday's cross-cutting **urban governance and municipal finance** essay (6 September).

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (definition + anchor)

Formalisation means bringing workers within the ambit of written contracts, minimum wages, and social security — not merely into the tax net. The four Labour Codes, effective **21 November 2025**, consolidate **29 central laws** and act on the **Second National Commission on Labour (2002)** recommendation to replace India's fragmented labour architecture with a small set of codes. Their most significant advance is statutory recognition of **gig and platform workers** under **Sections 109–114 of the Social Security Code**.

### The case for the reform

- **Universalised social security:** aggregators must contribute **1–2% of annual turnover (capped at 5% of payments to gig workers)** — a first attempt to make platform capital finance platform precarity. India's social protection coverage has already risen from **19% (2015) to 64.3% (ILO, 2025)**, covering ~94 crore people.
- **Wage floor and definitional clarity:** a **statutory national floor wage** and a uniform definition of "wages" (with basic pay ≥50% of CTC) raise PF and gratuity entitlements, giving effect to **Article 43 (living wage)**.
- **Equity for fixed-term workers:** parity in pay and benefits with permanent staff, plus gratuity after one year, weakens the incentive to hire informally.
- **Compliance simplification:** single registration, single licence and one return reduce transaction costs for MSMEs — the segment where informality concentrates.

### The structural constraints

- **Rules, not statutes, do the work:** labour is in the **Concurrent List (Entries 22–24)**, so codes bite only when *state* rules are notified; uneven state readiness means uneven rights.
- **Registration ≠ protection:** only **~8.58 lakh gig workers** registered by January 2026 against **~1.27 crore** estimated (NITI Aayog) — coverage on paper, exclusion in practice.
- **Portability and identity gaps:** benefits linked to a single aggregator sit awkwardly with multi-apping and high churn; the Codes stop short of resolving whether gig workers are *employees* at all.
- **The demand-side problem:** with **self-employment at 56.2%** and regular salaried work at just **23.6% (PLFS 2025)**, informality reflects a shortage of productive formal jobs, which no statute can legislate into being.
- **Flexibility concerns:** a raised threshold for retrenchment approval and stricter strike-notice rules have drawn trade-union objection that formalisation is being bought with bargaining power.

### Conclusion

The Codes are best read as necessary but insufficient — they build the *plumbing* of universal social security while the *pressure* must come from labour-intensive growth in manufacturing, construction and care work. Success will turn on three things: rapid and harmonised notification of state rules; making benefits **portable and worker-linked rather than employer-linked** (an ONORC-style architecture for social security); and pairing legal formalisation with MSME credit and skilling so that compliance is affordable. Formalisation that raises registration without raising real security would be, in Amartya Sen's terms, an expansion of entitlements without an expansion of capability.

## VALUE ADDITIONS (use 2–3, not all)

1. **Committee anchor:** *Second National Commission on Labour (2002), chaired by Ravindra Varma* — recommended consolidating central labour laws into four–five codes and a universal minimum social security floor. This is the intellectual parent of the Codes; citing it signals depth.
2. **Constitutional anchors:** **Article 43** (living wage and decent conditions), **Article 41** (right to work and public assistance), and **Concurrent List Entries 22–24** — the last explains *why* implementation is state-dependent.
3. **Federal innovation:** **Rajasthan Platform Based Gig Workers (Registration and Welfare) Act, 2023** — India's first state law for gig workers — and the **Karnataka Platform Based Gig Workers (Social Security and Welfare) Act, 2025**, which levies a **welfare fee of 1–5% per transaction** into a state welfare fund. Useful to show states as laboratories running *ahead* of the Centre.
4. **Data anchors:** PLFS 2025 — unemployment **3.1%**, LFPR **59.3%**, self-employed **56.2%**, regular salaried **23.6%**; e-Shram **31.89 crore** registrations (Aug 2026); NITI Aayog gig projection **2.35 crore by 2029–30**.
5. **Thinker/frame:** the **ILO's "decent work"** framework, or **Amartya Sen's** distinction between entitlements and capabilities, for a values-anchored conclusion.
