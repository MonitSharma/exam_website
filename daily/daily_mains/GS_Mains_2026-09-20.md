# GS Mains Answer — 2026-09-20 (Sunday — Reflective / Essay-style GS question)

*For: Monit · UPSC CSE 2027 · Sunday = one reflective, cross-cutting question spanning Fundamental Rights & Regulation (GS2), Society & Social Harmony (GS1), Technology & Internal Security (GS3) and Dignity as a public value (GS4). Attempt it on paper first; the model answer is below the divider.*

> **This week's hook:** India's most consequential speech law of this decade was not passed by Parliament. It was notified by a ministry.
>
> On **10 February 2026**, MeitY notified amendments to the **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021**, in force from **20 February 2026** — a ten-day compliance window. The amendments insert a statutory definition of **"synthetically generated information" (SGI)**: audio, visual or audio-visual content algorithmically created or altered so that it is "likely to be perceived as indistinguishable" from a real person or real-world event. The definition turns on how real the content *looks to an ordinary viewer*, not on whether AI was used. Permitted SGI must be **prominently labelled**, carry an **audio disclosure** where it is audio, and — to the extent technically feasible — embed **permanent provenance metadata and a unique identifier** of the tool that made it, which platforms must not allow users to strip. **Rule 3(3)(a)(i)** goes further and requires intermediaries offering generative tools to deploy **automated technical measures to prevent** the creation of unlawful SGI — non-consensual intimate imagery, CSAM, false electronic records, false depictions of a real person or event. **Rule 4(1A)** makes significant social media intermediaries **ask users to declare** whether an upload is synthetic and **verify that declaration** before it is published.
>
> The timelines are the sharper change. The amendments replace **36 hours with three hours** under Rule 3(1)(d), **24 hours with two hours** under Rule 3(2)(b), **15 days with seven** and **72 hours with 36** under Rule 3(2)(a)(i). A safeguard was added — police intimations must now come from an officer **not below the rank of Deputy Inspector General**, specifically authorised in writing. Some of the October 2025 draft's cruder ideas were dropped: the requirement that a label occupy **10% of the visual frame** is gone, replaced by a standard of prominence; routine editing, accessibility and good-faith document creation are expressly carved out.
>
> Around that rule sits a wider architecture. The **Karnataka High Court, on 24 September 2025, in *X Corp v. Union of India***, upheld **Section 79(3)(b)** of the IT Act, **Rule 3(1)(d)** and the MHA/I4C **Sahyog portal**, and held that a foreign corporation cannot claim **Article 19** protection. Reported figures put **MeitY's blocking orders at roughly 24,300 in 2025, against about 12,600 in 2024**, with around **60% concerning a single platform**, and the Sahyog portal generating a further **~2,312 orders across 19 platforms** between October 2024 and October 2025. Blocking orders under **Section 69A** remain confidential by rule, so the speaker is typically told that content is unavailable, not why.
>
> The courts have been pulling in both directions, and both pulls are legitimate. On **25 August 2025**, hearing the **Cure SMA Foundation** petition over online jokes about persons with disabilities, a Bench of **Justices Surya Kant and Joymalya Bagchi** asked the **Attorney General to prepare guidelines for conduct on social media**, balancing Article 19(1)(a) against "the rights and duties of others", and in **November 2025** urged the Centre to consider a stricter law on derogatory remarks against the disabled — dignity under **Article 21** prevailing where it collides with unfettered speech. Yet in **March 2026** the same Court recorded **Haryana's refusal (order dated 3 March 2026) to sanction prosecution** of Ashoka University professor **Ali Khan Mahmudabad** over Facebook posts on Operation Sindoor, closing a prosecution that should arguably never have begun. And on **20 September 2024** — two years ago to the day — the **Bombay High Court**, through tie-breaker **Justice A.S. Chandurkar**, struck down the **Fact Check Unit** rule in ***Kunal Kamra v. Union of India*** as violating **Articles 14, 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g)** and *ultra vires* the IT Act.
>
> The scale makes the stakes plain: TRAI's **Performance Indicator Report for January–March 2026 (released 22 June 2026)** puts India's internet subscriber base at **109.28 crore (1,092.79 million)**, up 6.24% in a single quarter. The public square is now digital for most Indians, and the rules of that square are made by delegated legislation, enforced by private companies, and executed in hours.
>
> That collision — *between a state with real and legitimate reasons to act against online harm, and a constitutional order that limits who may silence whom, by what procedure, and with what explanation* — is today's reflective theme. It is deliberately distinct from the last Sunday essay on **delimitation and federal representation (13 September)**, from the **urban governance** essay (6 September), from the GS3 Sci-Tech question on **India's AI strategy and light-touch regulation (25 June)** — that one was about *capability and innovation*, this one is about *rights and procedure* — and from the GS4 case on **algorithmic welfare exclusion (12 September)**, though one line from that case on automation hardening error is legitimately borrowable.

## QUESTION — 15 marks · 250 words

> *"The hard question about free speech is rarely what may be said. It is who decides, how fast, and to whom that decision must be explained."*
>
> India's response to genuine online harm — synthetic media, coordinated hate, the public humiliation of the vulnerable — has been built largely out of delegated rules, compressed timelines and enforcement by private platforms. **Critically examine** the tension between protecting dignity in the digital public square and preserving constitutional limits on the power to silence, and reflect on the principles by which a legitimate settlement might be reached.

**(Directive: *critically examine* — this is a Sunday reflective question, so the marks lie in the *reasoning*, not the recall. Take the case for regulation seriously first and on its own terms: the harms are real, they are unevenly distributed, and "let the market of ideas sort it out" is not an answer to a deepfake. Then locate the constitutional objection precisely — it is about **process, not appetite**: delegated rule-making, unreviewable executive discretion, private enforcement and speed that forecloses reasons. Then name the structural problem — **collateral censorship**, where liability placed on an intermediary makes over-removal the rational choice. Close with **principles**, not a wishlist. Do **not** write a descriptive "Article 19(1)(a), its restrictions under 19(2), and challenges of social media" answer — that is a 6-mark answer. 15 marks, ~250 words.)**

## MODEL ANSWER (~250 words)

### Introduction (anchor + data)

**Article 19(1)(a)** may be limited only by law that is *reasonable* and falls within one of the **eight grounds in Article 19(2)**; in ***Kaushal Kishor* (2023)** the Supreme Court held those grounds **exhaustive**. Yet most speech in India is now governed not by statute but by the **IT Rules, 2021**, amended on **10 February 2026** to cover **"synthetically generated information"** and to cut takedown timelines from **36 hours to three**. With **109.28 crore internet subscribers (TRAI, March 2026)**, the public square is digital — and its rules are delegated, private and fast.

### A. The case for regulation, taken seriously

- **The harm is real and asymmetric.** Synthetic media can fabricate a person's voice, face or consent; non-consensual intimate imagery, communal fabrication and the ridicule of persons with disabilities inflict injuries that no counter-speech repairs. In the **Cure SMA Foundation** matter (**25 August 2025**) the Court rightly located this in **Article 21 dignity**, not mere offence.
- **Scale defeats adjudication.** A falsehood propagates in minutes; a writ petition takes years. Insisting on case-by-case judicial remedy is, in practice, a decision not to remedy.
- **Provenance is the least restrictive tool.** Labelling and metadata add information rather than subtract speech — regulation of *form*, not *content*. This is the 2026 amendment's best idea.

### B. Why the method is nonetheless constitutionally fragile

- **Delegated legislation carrying a fundamental right.** Speech restrictions are being written by executive notification with minimal pre-legislative consultation — a design the **Bombay High Court** rejected in ***Kunal Kamra* (20 September 2024)**, striking down the Fact Check Unit rule under **Articles 14, 19(1)(a), 19(1)(g)**.
- **Speed forecloses process.** Three hours permits removal but not reasons, notice or hearing. It is procedurally identical for a non-consensual deepfake and for satire — yet only one of these needs the clock.
- **Collateral censorship.** Liability placed on the intermediary makes over-removal costless and under-removal expensive; a private firm therefore rations speech by risk, not by law. **Shreya Singhal (2015)** read down **Section 79(3)(b)** precisely to prevent this, and warned of the **"chilling effect"**.
- **Opacity.** **Section 69A** blocking is confidential by rule; reported orders roughly **doubled to ~24,300 in 2025**. A citizen cannot appeal a reason never given. ***Anuradha Bhasin* (2020)** required publication, proportionality and periodic review — a standard not yet generalised.
- **The wrong actor decides.** The **Karnataka High Court (24 September 2025)** upheld the **Sahyog** architecture, but holding that a *platform* lacks Article 19 rights leaves open the question of the *user's* rights, exercised through it.

### C. The deeper asymmetry

Those with least power are doubly exposed: most injured by hate and impersonation, and most likely to be silenced by an opaque takedown they cannot contest. Regulation that ignores this protects the powerful twice.

### Conclusion — principles for a legitimate settlement

Neither an unregulated square nor an administratively cleansed one is constitutionally acceptable. A defensible settlement would rest on: a **statutory basis** debated in Parliament rather than notified rules; **strict Article 19(2) tailoring** and the **Puttaswamy** proportionality test; **graduated remedies** — label, de-amplify, de-monetise, then remove — instead of binary takedown; **reasoned, published orders** with notice to the speaker and an independent appellate route; **transparency reporting** on volumes and grounds; and, alongside law, the **Article 51A(e)** duty of harmony and a serious investment in digital literacy. The question India must answer is not how much speech to permit, but how to build a power to silence that is itself answerable.

## VALUE-ADDITIONS TO WEAVE IN (pick 2–3, not all)

1. **Jack Balkin's "collateral censorship" and "new school speech regulation"** — the State does not censor the speaker; it regulates the infrastructure, and the infrastructure censors the speaker. This is the single sharpest analytical framing available for this question, and almost no candidate will use it. Deploy it once, in Section B, and the answer stops reading like current affairs.
2. ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015)** — struck down **Section 66A**; read down **Section 79(3)(b)** to require a court order or government notification; and gave the **discussion / advocacy / incitement** trichotomy, holding that only *incitement* may be restricted. It is the load-bearing precedent; cite it for the *chilling effect*, not merely as "66A was struck down".
3. ***Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020)** — orders restricting access must be **published**, are subject to **proportionality** and require **periodic review**. The most economical way to argue that opacity, not restriction as such, is the defect.
4. ***Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP* (Constitution Bench, 3 January 2023)** — the **Article 19(2)** grounds are **exhaustive**; no further restriction may be read in, including by invoking another fundamental right. The same Bench held, **4:1**, that Articles 19 and 21 are enforceable **against private and non-State actors** — which is precisely the doctrinal hook for arguing that a platform's moderation power is not beyond constitutional reach. Use the first holding to discipline the dignity argument, and the second to discipline the platform.
5. **Law Commission of India, Report No. 267 on Hate Speech (March 2017)** — following ***Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan* (2014)**, it proposed new **Sections 153C and 505A** of the IPC, distinguishing incitement to hatred from causing fear or alarm. **Neither was enacted**, and the **BNS, 2023** retains the older overlapping offences (**§§ 152, 196, 197**). Proof that India chose rules over a statute because it never wrote the statute.
6. **Proportionality under *Puttaswamy* (2017)** — legitimate aim, rational nexus, necessity/least restrictive means, and balancing. Apply it explicitly to the three-hour rule: the aim is legitimate, the nexus rational, but the *least restrictive means* prong is where it strains.
7. **Article 51A(e) and (f)** — the fundamental duty to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood. Useful in the conclusion for the point that civility is a *social* achievement law can support but not manufacture — echoing Ambedkar's warning that constitutional morality "is not a natural sentiment… it has to be cultivated."
8. **The Indian counter-example of self-correction** — **Mahmudabad (Haryana's refusal to sanction, 3 March 2026)** and **Kunal Kamra (2024)** show the system does correct itself, but only for those who can reach a constitutional court. A one-line use of this is far better than a paragraph.

## EXAMINER'S NOTE — how this answer earns marks

- **The trap** is the template answer: "Article 19(1)(a) → reasonable restrictions under 19(2) → challenges of fake news, hate speech, deepfakes → way forward: awareness, regulation, media literacy." That is a 6/15 answer, and it is what most of the hall will write. The question is not *whether* to regulate; it is *by what authority and procedure*.
- **The differentiator** is Section B's third bullet. Anyone can say "risk of censorship". Very few will explain the *mechanism* — that placing liability on the intermediary makes over-removal the rational commercial choice, so the chilling effect is produced by **private incentive**, not State malice. That single move converts a current-affairs answer into a constitutional-design answer.
- **Credit the regulation before you critique it.** An answer that only attacks the 2026 rules is as unbalanced as one that only praises them. The labelling-and-provenance design genuinely *is* the least restrictive instrument available; say so, then show that the takedown timelines are governed by a different logic.
- **Hold both claims.** The examiner is testing whether you can defend dignity under Article 21 and procedure under Article 19 simultaneously, without collapsing into either free-speech absolutism or "reasonable restrictions" boilerplate.
- **Data discipline:** three or four figures, not ten. The **36 hours → 3 hours** change, **~24,300 blocking orders in 2025**, and **~109 crore subscribers** are enough. Dates on *Shreya Singhal*, *Kamra* and *Kaushal Kishor* are worth more than extra statistics.
- **Time check:** ~22 minutes, ~250 words. If the introduction runs long you will lose the conclusion's principles — which is exactly where a reflective question awards its top marks. Write the conclusion's four principles *first* in the margin if you must.
