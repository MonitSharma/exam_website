## Case Study (Marks: 20 | Suggested length: ~250 words per sub-answer)

You are the Regional Officer of the State Pollution Control Board, responsible for granting and renewing "Consent to Operate" clearances for industries in your region. A large textile-dyeing cluster on the outskirts of your city employs nearly 8,000 workers, most of them migrant labourers and women, and is the single largest source of livelihood in the area.

During a routine inspection, your junior scientist reports that three of the largest units in the cluster have quietly bypassed their Common Effluent Treatment Plant and have been discharging untreated chemical effluent into a river that feeds drinking-water intakes for two downstream villages. Preliminary samples show heavy-metal concentrations far above permissible limits. Under the rules, such a violation warrants immediate closure notices pending correction.

That evening, you receive a call from a senior official in the state industries department, who is also a personal mentor from your early service years. He urges you to "go slow" — a closure now, he says, would trigger mass layoffs, invite adverse media attention before an investment summit, and embarrass the government. He suggests you issue a warning, give the units ninety days to comply, and keep the report "internal" for now. He reminds you, gently, that your pending transfer to a coveted headquarters posting is "still being considered."

Your junior scientist, meanwhile, is anxious that if no action is taken, she will be seen as complicit, and the downstream villagers remain unaware of the contamination in their water.

---

### Sub-Questions

**(a)** Identify the ethical issues involved in this case and the various stakeholders affected. **(5 marks, ~150 words)**

**(b)** Evaluate the following options available to you, bringing out their merits and demerits: **(8 marks, ~250 words)**
   1. Issue immediate closure notices to the violating units and make the contamination report public.
   2. Accept the senior official's advice — issue a warning, grant ninety days, and keep the report internal.
   3. Devise a middle path that protects public health without an abrupt, total shutdown.

**(c)** What course of action would you finally adopt, and why? **(5 marks, ~150 words)**

**(d)** Which core values and administrative principles are at stake in this dilemma? **(2 marks, ~60 words)**

## Model Approach

*(Read this only after you have written your own answer.)*

### Stakeholders
- **Downstream villagers** — right to safe drinking water, health, life (immediate and highest-priority stake).
- **8,000 workers** (largely migrant and women) — livelihood and job security.
- **You, the Regional Officer** — professional integrity, duty, career prospects.
- **The junior scientist** — professional conscience, fear of complicity, whistleblower vulnerability.
- **The senior official / mentor** — political and administrative pressures, personal relationship.
- **The violating industries** — economic interest, legal liability.
- **The State / public at large** — environmental commons, rule of law, credibility of the regulator.

### Core Ethical Dilemmas
- **Public health & environmental protection vs. livelihood and economic stability.**
- **Rule of law and statutory duty vs. loyalty to a mentor and political convenience.**
- **Integrity and personal conscience vs. career advancement (a subtle conflict of interest / inducement).**
- **Transparency (informing affected villagers) vs. institutional pressure to keep the report internal.**

### Evaluation of Options

**Option 1 — Immediate total closure + full disclosure.**
*Merits:* Upholds the law, prioritises the right to life, protects the regulator's credibility, sends a strong deterrent signal. *Demerits:* Abrupt mass layoffs cause genuine hardship to thousands of vulnerable workers; may be seen as rigid and lacking proportionality; punishes workers for owners' violations.

**Option 2 — Warning, 90-day window, report kept internal.**
*Merits:* Avoids immediate economic disruption; maintains the mentor relationship and career prospects. *Demerits:* Ethically indefensible — knowingly allows continued poisoning of a drinking-water source; violates statutory duty; conceals material public-health information; amounts to succumbing to inducement; exposes you and the scientist to future liability. This option fails the basic test of legality and the public interest.

**Option 3 — Calibrated, health-first middle path.**
*Merits:* Balances proportionality with the non-negotiable duty to protect life; legally defensible; humane toward workers. *Demerits:* Operationally demanding; requires courage to resist pressure and firm follow-up.

### Recommended Course of Action
Adopt a **calibrated but firm response anchored in the right to life**:
1. **Immediately stop the harm** — issue directions to the three violating units to cease effluent discharge at once and reconnect to the CETP; where effluent cannot be treated, order a targeted production halt of the offending process (not necessarily the entire cluster) under the precautionary principle.
2. **Protect health now** — formally alert the downstream village panchayats and the public-health/water authority so contaminated intakes are shut and alternative water is arranged. This is non-negotiable and cannot be kept "internal."
3. **Document and report through proper channels** — record the mentor's call factually, place the inspection report on the official file, and inform the Member Secretary/Chairman, insulating both yourself and the junior scientist.
4. **Protect the whistleblower** — shield the junior scientist from any reprisal and credit her diligence.
5. **Enable a corrective, time-bound compliance and revival plan** — allow compliant units to keep running and give violators a supervised path to restart only after certified treatment, so livelihoods are preserved without compromising health.

Respond to the mentor with respect but firmness: explain that the law and the villagers' safety leave no room to "go slow," and that career considerations cannot enter a decision involving public health. This resolves the dilemma by **refusing the false trade-off** between jobs and lives — protecting health while keeping the economic cost proportionate.

### Values & Thinkers to Cite
- **Public interest and the right to life (Article 21)** as the supreme test; the **precautionary principle** and **"polluter pays"** as settled environmental-governance principles.
- **Integrity, courage of conviction, and objectivity** — echoing the **Second ARC (4th Report, "Ethics in Governance")** emphasis on integrity and public service values, and the **Nolan Committee's Seven Principles of Public Life** (selflessness, integrity, accountability, openness).
- **Kant's categorical imperative** — treating the villagers not merely as means to industrial or political ends; and **Gandhi's talisman** — "recall the face of the poorest and weakest" (here, the workers *and* the villagers) when in doubt.
